Nobility After Aristocracy: A Reading in the Late Nietzsche

Editorial revision by Bob Smith · 6 September 2026

The mature account of master and slave morality arrives in Beyond Good and Evil (1886) and On the Genealogy of Morals (1887) as a challenge to the moral self-understanding of modern Europe. Nietzsche describes the victory of a valuation he associates with Christianity and the claims of the many. That victory is not the same proposition as the disappearance of every politically effective aristocracy. Nor does it end the conflict: Genealogy I §16 explicitly leaves it alive in certain places and within higher natures. His presentation of Napoleon as a reappearance of the aristocratic problem further complicates any settled story of decline. The question for a present reader therefore begins with a tension. Nietzsche addresses an age he regards as increasingly hostile to noble valuations, yet continues to attach importance to nobility. What could that address ask of someone whose life has no inherited aristocratic standing? Read Genealogy I §16.

Historical mourning and a wish to restore hereditary privilege are possible responses to the aristocratic vocabulary, but they do not exhaust its philosophical uses. I want to defend another possibility: the account of nobility can help articulate a form of self-relation that is not exhausted by social rank. This is an interpretation to be argued, and a proposal about what a contemporary reader might cultivate. It is not a demonstration that Nietzsche himself abandoned social hierarchy. The central evidence lies in his distinction between ways of generating values, his description of self-reverence, and his accounts of the work by which a life acquires form. The difficulty lies in the continuing connection he draws between spiritual elevation and unequal social conditions. A reading of nobility after aristocracy is strongest when it keeps that difficulty visible. Its task is to identify what can travel beyond the historical institution, while acknowledging what must be questioned or revised in order to make that journey.

The Typology

Beyond Good and Evil §260 distinguishes two moralities and relates them to different positions within social life. Read alongside Genealogy I §10, the distinction concerns the direction in which valuations are generated. Noble valuation begins with an affirmation of the noble person’s own powers and manner of existence; the negative judgment of what is unlike it is secondary. The morality of ressentiment begins by constructing an evil other, against whom it establishes its own goodness. The distinction is not adequately captured by a list of approved actions. Assistance, for example, can express the abundance Nietzsche associates with noble giving, while superficially similar conduct may have another motive and meaning. What matters is the organization of the valuation, including the relation to those toward whom it is directed. That makes the account useful for self-examination, although it does not dissolve the social distinctions from which Nietzsche develops it. Read §260 and Genealogy I §10.

Two qualifications prevent that psychological use from becoming an oversimplification. First, §260 says that the moralities may be mixed, confused, or juxtaposed, sometimes within one person. It does not say that every actual person necessarily contains both, or that ruling and subordinate groups cease to matter. Its point makes personal classification difficult rather than supplying a universal inventory of the soul. Second, the typology has evaluative force as well as diagnostic use. Nietzsche is not neutral about the modes of life he describes. A reader therefore cannot avoid the question of whether his preferences are defensible by calling the account mere psychology. The inward question remains valuable: does this judgment express something I affirm, or does it acquire its importance chiefly through the person or condition I oppose? But an answer about its origin is the beginning of an assessment, not a substitute for considering its truth, consequences, and worth.

Genealogy I §10 sharpens the structural point by granting creativity to ressentiment itself. A reactive valuation can invent, organize, and endure; its dependence on an external opposition does not make it historically ineffective. Nietzsche also allows resentment to occur in a noble person, while emphasizing the capacity to discharge it, recover, and forget. The distinction therefore cannot be reduced to anger versus serenity. Nor does the mere fact of having suffered an injury establish that a person’s values are governed by ressentiment. The relevant question concerns the place the injury comes to occupy in the life: does opposition become the condition of self-worth, or can the person continue to value and act beyond it? This is the point at which the typology can speak to circumstances very different from the societies in Nietzsche’s genealogy. Its contemporary application should remain an interpretation of particular patterns, not a diagnosis automatically imposed upon anyone who has a grievance.

Why the Aristocratic Vocabulary

Why, then, does Nietzsche speak so insistently of aristocracy, castes, and ranks? One reason is genealogical. In Genealogy I §§4–5, he argues that terms of moral worth emerge from names and distinctions of social status. These etymological arguments are evidence he offers, not findings that become authoritative merely because he offers them. The fifth section also includes racial and physiological conjectures that need critical scrutiny. Yet it contains a suggestive movement for the present interpretation: in discussing the Greek claim to truthfulness, Nietzsche describes a word surviving the decline of the nobility and acquiring a psychological meaning. Here there is textual support for distinguishing a quality from its first social bearers. That linguistic development does not establish that the quality’s social conditions have become unnecessary. It does, however, prevent us from treating every use of noble as a simple reference to a currently occupied class position. The psychological possibility is connected to the history without being identical to a surviving title or office. Read §§4–5.

The second reason is harder to accommodate. Beyond Good and Evil §257 links the external pathos of distance to a widening of distances within the soul: the development of higher, rarer, more comprehensive states. But the opening does not merely say that aristocratic societies performed this work once. It extends the claim into the future. Section 258 insists that a healthy aristocracy treats society as a condition of its higher existence and accepts the subordination of others; §259 continues the argument through exploitation and the organization of power. These claims place a real burden on any attempt to detach inward nobility from hierarchy. The phrase about self-overcoming in a supermoral sense does not, by itself, announce that the external institution can now be discarded. A psychological reading can ask whether the inward achievement requires every social arrangement Nietzsche associates with it, but the answer cannot simply be attributed to him. My proposal is that the achievement remains worth examining while its purported dependence on inherited or coercive rank remains contestable. That preserves a philosophical question instead of making a difficult passage harmless. Read §257 and continue through §§258–259 in Zimmern’s edition.

Reverence for Self

Beyond Good and Evil §287 asks how nobility can be recognized under conditions Nietzsche describes as increasingly plebeian. His answer distinguishes an underlying certainty about oneself from works that may merely express a longing to be noble. The decisive term is belief: a fundamental assurance of the noble soul concerning itself. This is not equivalent to subscribing to a religious creed, but neither is belief rejected. The old religious contrast between faith and works is being given another use. The section therefore directs attention toward the relation a person has to their own existence, while warning that impressive achievements may be ambiguous evidence of it. It does not say that conduct is irrelevant to understanding someone, or that an inward claim can excuse any deed. Still less does the passage explicitly cancel §§257–259. What it gives this essay is a psychological dimension of nobility whose relation to those social claims must remain under examination. Read §287.

The closing image of a soul that reveres itself invites a distinction from vanity. Vanity requires an audience whose response must continually renew the feeling of worth. The certainty described in §287 seems less dependent on such ratification. Yet a reader should hesitate before claiming it. Someone may announce independence from public opinion while organizing their entire life around contempt for the public. The apparent refusal of an audience can become another way of needing one. Self-reverence, as I understand the possibility here, would involve enough confidence to act without making every disagreement a verdict on one’s existence. It would not imply infallibility, immunity from criticism, or the conviction that other people have no claims upon us. Those further conclusions do not follow from the description of certainty. This interpretation preserves the force of Nietzsche’s distinction between having a quality and displaying the desire to possess it, while leaving open how reliably anyone could recognize the difference in themselves.

The greatest difficulty for a constructive reading is that §287 describes a certainty that cannot simply be sought and found. Genealogy I §13 creates a related difficulty by attacking the fiction of an indifferent doer behind the deed, free to express an entirely different nature. Neither passage offers a simple instruction to choose nobility. But the denial of a self that originates itself independently of conditions does not logically establish that dispositions can never change. Habituation, encounters, practices, and the reorganization of competing drives may be among the conditions through which a different person develops. We need to distinguish announcing an identity from undergoing a formation. The text does not guarantee that the latter produces the specific certainty of §287, and it does not establish an equal capacity or opportunity for everyone to do so. The modest constructive claim is that some practices may cultivate less dependent forms of self-relation without giving their practitioners a certificate of nobility. That claim requires evidence from Nietzsche’s accounts of transformation as well as recognition of their limits. Read Genealogy I §13.

The Constructive Program

One resource is the earlier Gay Science §290, which describes giving style to one’s character as demanding work on materials already present. Strengths and weaknesses are surveyed, altered where possible, or placed within a different arrangement. What resists removal may be reinterpreted. This is a model of formation without creation from nothing: the activity depends on a nature, on constraints, and on sustained practice. Yet the passage is less uniform than a doctrine of compulsory self-mastery would suggest. Nietzsche also allows a different route to spirits who resist the constraint of style, including spirits he acknowledges may be of high rank. Their relation to themselves may be sustained through the apparently unregulated character of their surroundings. The closing concern is satisfaction with oneself and its relation to revenge. It does not establish that every coherent life is admirable, or that one aesthetic plan is a universal cure for ressentiment. It offers evidence that self-relation can be worked upon through more than one arrangement of a life. Read §290.

A second resource is Zarathustra II, “On Redemption,” where the problem of revenge is extended to the will’s relation to time. What has happened cannot simply be willed away. The speech explores how frustration before the past can turn into condemnation of becoming itself, and it reaches toward a willing that could affirm what has been. But its questions about redemption remain questions, and the dramatic ending complicates the authority of the teaching. A reading that connects the speech with eternal recurrence must acknowledge that connection as interpretive work. For the present argument, the useful possibility is a changed relation to an irreversible past: a life need not grant every injury the power to determine all its future valuations. That is different from declaring every injury justified or forbidding resistance to further harm. Genealogy II §11, with its argument against deriving justice from ressentiment, gives another reason to keep the distinction between opposition to wrong and the cultivation of revenge open. Read “On Redemption” and Genealogy II §11.

A third resource is Ecce Homo, “Why I Am So Wise,” §6. Nietzsche presents his understanding of resentment as something learned through illness and through changing conditions of strength. During exhaustion he describes a practice of conserving himself by limiting reaction; when strength returns, he characterizes resentment as beneath him. The account is autobiographical and should not be converted into a general medical prescription or a judgment that illness establishes moral inferiority. Its philosophical interest here lies in the differences between the responses he describes. Freedom from resentment is not represented by one outward behavior equally appropriate to every condition. Nor does the author’s claim prove that he has perfectly achieved it. What the passage offers is a picture of resistance to ressentiment as a task within a vulnerable life, rather than a judgment delivered from an existence untouched by wounds. This makes the constructive possibility more credible without making it easy or assured. Read §6.

These readings support a possible orientation: to give a life enough form that its worth is not continually borrowed from an audience, an enemy, or an imagined reversal of fortune. They do not establish that this orientation exhausts noble morality, or that Nietzsche offers it without conflicting commitments. His noble figures can be severe, retaliatory, socially exclusive, and dependent on the traditions they honor. The contemporary task proposed here therefore involves selection and criticism as well as fidelity. The connection with amor fati is an aspiration toward affirmation, not proof that self-reverence, noble valuation, and love of fate are interchangeable names for a single achieved state. Their relations need to be worked out. It is enough for the present argument that Nietzsche’s inquiries allow us to ask how an existence might become less dependent on condemning what lies outside it, without pretending that its difficulties have thereby disappeared.

What Remains

Does this amount to a private inward aristocracy, a consolation for people who lack an institution that recognizes their worth? It might, if the argument ended by treating solitude as the only possible setting of excellence. But that conclusion would be too quick. Nietzsche’s future philosophers have ambitions concerning the creation and direction of values, not merely the protection of an inward retreat. In Beyond Good and Evil §211, their activity is described in terms of command and legislation. Such ambitions complicate both an exclusively private reading and any easy identification of his project with a liberal ideal of personal development. The psychological use of nobility defended here is consequently one strand of a larger and less comfortable philosophy. Its value does not depend on claiming that it is the only strand Nietzsche finally intended. Read Beyond Good and Evil §211.

There is also a contemporary question about the conditions of the inward work itself. Time, education, security, relationships, and opportunities for sustained effort cannot simply be assumed to exist equally for everyone. A proposal to cultivate independence should therefore examine the dependencies that make such cultivation possible. It would be inconsistent to celebrate the courage of self-formation while ignoring the arrangements that give some people much greater room for it. Nietzsche’s insistence on the social conditions of elevation can sharpen this question even when his conclusions about subordination are rejected. The alternative to hereditary standing need not be an isolated soul whose accomplishments come from nowhere. It could involve forms of association that support difficult work without treating their members’ ranks as fixed in advance. That is a contemporary proposal to be assessed in its own right, not an institutional program already established by the passages considered here.

Nobility after aristocracy is therefore best understood as a question about inheritance and transformation. Which possibilities first made visible within unequal societies can be cultivated under different conditions, and which of their features should be left behind? Nietzsche’s account of self-reverence gives that question psychological depth; his accounts of rank prevent its answer from becoming complacent. I would retain the demand that a person examine what sustains their valuations, the suspicion of a self that needs enemies to know its worth, and the possibility of working upon a character without imagining oneself its unconditioned creator. I would not treat these commitments as a vindication of inherited privilege or as proof that Nietzsche quietly withdrew his defense of hierarchy. The argument is more limited, but its demand remains substantial: to seek a form of life whose affirmation can survive criticism, whose independence acknowledges its conditions, and whose relation to the past makes room for a future that is more than revenge. That possibility is worth exploring even when the name noble remains contested.

Texts and revision

This essay develops a possible contemporary interpretation of Nietzsche’s nobility. Claims about what his texts establish are distinguished from proposals about what a reader might retain or revise. The original argument has been revised to correct the account of Beyond Good and Evil §287, address §§257–259, qualify the use of §260, and clarify the difference between choosing an identity and developing a disposition.

References use book and section numbers. Links to the companion compilation provide the displayed source texts and translator credits. The online editions used here are Helen Zimmern’s Beyond Good and Evil, Horace B. Samuel’s The Genealogy of Morals, Thomas Common’s The Joyful Wisdom and Thus Spake Zarathustra, and Anthony M. Ludovici’s Ecce Homo. Discussions are paraphrase and interpretation, apart from short marked terms. Kaufmann and Hollingdale remain the preferred study editions where applicable.
