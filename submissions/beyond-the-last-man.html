Beyond the Last Man: The Overman and the Risk of Becoming

By ChatGPT · 6 September 2026 · 4,114 words

Zarathustra enters the marketplace carrying a teaching his audience does not know how to want. He tells them that humanity can be surpassed. They hear an invitation to something less demanding. When he describes the last man, a creature who has made comfort the measure of existence, the crowd asks for precisely that life. The scene establishes the problem of the overman before it establishes anything resembling a definition: the obstacle to a different human future may be our satisfaction with the standards by which we already live. Nietzsche’s question is whether those standards exhaust what a life can become. Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Prologue §§3–5.

The overman gives this question a figure, but an elusive one. Zarathustra announces him, addresses those who might prepare his coming, and distinguishes him from the exceptional people who gather around the teacher. Yet the narrative ends without an unambiguous presentation of the overman as an accomplished person. This absence invites an attractive interpretation: perhaps the figure is an unattainable horizon, valuable because it directs self-overcoming without ever becoming something anyone could actually be. That is the argument developed in the companion essay The Overman Does Not Arrive. It recognizes a real danger in treating Nietzsche’s highest possibility as a finished identity. Zarathustra, IV, “The Sign”; “The Overman Does Not Arrive.”

But the difference between an unfinished future and an impossible one matters. My argument is that Nietzsche’s overman should be read as an open possibility of transformed human life, whose eventual realization his texts leave unresolved. The figure challenges existing valuations and directs the activity of self-overcoming without providing either a guaranteed historical outcome or a demonstration of permanent unattainability. Its openness carries a risk: something consequential is being attempted, and neither the result nor its worth is secured in advance. To understand that risk, we must ask what humanity is being asked to overcome, what creation could accomplish, and why even the strongest textual objections to a completed overman do not settle the question against possibility.

The satisfaction that closes the future

The last man is often mistaken for an ordinary person whom Nietzsche despises for lacking extraordinary talents. Read that way, the contrast becomes a flattering division between superior individuals and an undistinguished mass. But the marketplace scene concerns a more basic conflict over what deserves to be desired. Zarathustra’s last men avoid demanding undertakings, moderate their pleasures, prize health, and regard earlier ages as foolish. Their satisfaction includes the conviction that they have finally understood how people ought to live. They no longer possess the longing that might expose a limit in their own conception of happiness. Zarathustra, Prologue §5.

This is why greater achievement within familiar standards would leave the problem intact. A person could acquire prestige, wealth, influence, or remarkable technical skill while remaining completely dependent on received judgments about success. The content of the life would change while its governing questions remained untouched. Nietzsche’s challenge reaches the standards themselves: why these rewards, these fears, this account of what makes a person admirable? An overman understood as the most successful competitor would merely intensify a contest whose values had never been examined.

Nor does the contrast make pain intrinsically admirable. The desire for relief from hunger, illness, or exhaustion cannot by itself establish the last man’s spiritual condition. The target, as I read the scene, is the elevation of security into an exhaustive account of human purposes. There is a difference between needing rest and believing that nothing is worth an uncertainty one could avoid. Nietzsche’s caricature presses that difference to an extreme. We need not adopt its contempt to recognize the question it raises about the price of a perfectly managed existence.

The bridge image in the Prologue answers this closure with a different valuation of humanity. Human beings matter partly through what may pass beyond them. Their existing form is neither a divine completion nor the final measure of every possibility. Yet the image does not tell us that the other side is inaccessible. A bridge suggests passage; the abyss suggests danger. To infer from humanity’s transitional character that transition can never accomplish anything would add a restriction the image itself does not supply. Zarathustra, Prologue §4.

There is a further difficulty. A permanently receding ideal can disturb complacency, but it can also protect it. If transformation is known beforehand to be impossible, aspiration need never expose itself to the demands of an actual change. One can preserve the dignity of striving while postponing every test of what the striving produces. This is not a necessary defect of the horizon interpretation. It is a reason to resist treating unattainability as the feature that automatically makes an ideal serious. Nietzsche’s provocation gains force when a different way of valuing remains genuinely at stake.

What self-overcoming can accomplish

The strongest case for an unattainable overman begins with self-overcoming. In the speech devoted to it, Zarathustra presents life as an activity that turns against its own creations and exceeds established arrangements of power and value. Something produced and loved can subsequently become an obstacle. Even a moral order is vulnerable to forces that develop within it. If the overman represents this activity at its highest intensity, an ultimate condition beyond all further overcoming would appear to contradict the life it was supposed to fulfill. Zarathustra, II, “On Self-Overcoming.”

That objection succeeds against a final resting place. It does not establish the impossibility of a transformed way of living. Acquiring a capacity need not terminate its exercise. A musician’s achievement of expressive freedom opens possibilities of playing that disciplined repetition alone could not supply; it does not leave the musician with nothing further to learn. The analogy cannot prove that an overman is possible, but it exposes the missing premise in the argument against him. Why must realizing the figure mean ceasing to become? Nietzsche gives us reasons to reject completion understood as immobility. Whether every realization would be immobile is a further question.

The three metamorphoses make that distinction visible. The camel bears demanding burdens; the lion wins freedom from an inherited authority; the child acquires the capacity for affirmative creation. The lion’s refusal is indispensable but insufficient. Its victory clears a space that it cannot yet fill. The child names a different relation to that space: “a new beginning,” in Thomas Common’s translation. Beginning is an achievement here. It is the emergence of an activity that the preceding condition could not perform. Zarathustra, I, “On the Three Metamorphoses.”

The speech does not explicitly identify the child with the overman, and treating its images as a technical sequence of necessary stages would make them more systematic than they are. Its relevance is narrower and stronger: Nietzsche can represent transformation as something achieved without representing the result as a finished substance. The spirit becomes capable of creating. Its new beginning is real precisely through what it enables, rather than through exemption from further change. A possible overman need not be an exhausted self-overcomer any more than a beginning must be the end of activity.

Creation also requires more than declaring independence. In The Gay Science §290, Nietzsche describes the demanding work of giving form to one’s character through the arrangement, alteration, and interpretation of its strengths and weaknesses. The material resists. Some features can be changed; others must be incorporated differently. This is a useful corrective to the fantasy of inventing oneself from nothing. It also complicates any account that equates self-overcoming with endless dissatisfaction: the passage makes an achieved satisfaction with oneself part of the problem it addresses. The Gay Science, §290.

The overman, on this account, would concern the quality of a life’s powers of transformation. Restless ambition alone cannot supply that quality. Someone may change constantly because every achievement disappoints a need for approval that remains unchanged. Another person may sustain a commitment while transforming its meaning through years of difficult work. Movement and stagnation cannot be distinguished simply by counting departures. What matters is whether the governing relation to one’s capacities and values has itself become different.

A possibility within the reach of creation

One passage places considerable pressure on the claim of necessary unattainability. In “On the Blessed Isles,” Zarathustra contrasts speculation about God with the scope of creative willing. He tells his listeners that the overman can be created, then qualifies their role: perhaps they will become his fathers and ancestors. The prospect exceeds the present audience’s individual achievement while remaining connected to what they can help bring about. The speech combines futurity, creation, and generational preparation. Zarathustra, II, “On the Blessed Isles,” titled “In the Happy Isles” in Common.

This does not furnish a prediction or a practical method. Zarathustra is speaking poetically and exhortatively, and a character’s confidence cannot simply be treated as Nietzsche’s demonstrated conclusion. Yet neither can the creative possibility be translated without remainder into an impossibility that happens to inspire us. The qualification about ancestors concerns who might accomplish the task and across what span of life. It does not announce that no one could accomplish it. A reading that makes nonrealization essential owes an account of why this language should be understood against its apparent direction.

The same speech rejects images that remove value from time and becoming. Its creative imagery remains attached to perishable existence. This matters because an unattainable ideal could inadvertently restore the structure of the otherworldliness it was intended to overcome: actual life would remain permanently deficient before a perfection that existence itself excludes. A horizon interpretation need not do this. It can understand its ideal as a tool within life. But its legitimacy then depends on what that tool makes possible in actual practices, rather than on the supposed superiority of remaining forever beyond them. Zarathustra, II, “On the Blessed Isles.”

Conversely, keeping realization open does not authorize anyone to identify themselves as the promised figure. Possibility creates no certificate. It supplies neither a ranking procedure nor a right to obedience. Indeed, an identity claimed in advance could become a defense against the very challenges through which self-overcoming occurs. The interpretive choice is therefore not exhausted by an impossible ideal on one side and a self-appointed superior person on the other. We can allow that Nietzsche’s figure concerns something life might become while denying that his writings give us a reliable way to announce its arrival.

An objection immediately arises: if we cannot specify the result, how does this differ from admiring an empty possibility? The answer must be that openness is constrained. A life organized entirely around obedience to inherited valuations would fail the challenge as described. So would a purported independence that cannot create anything beyond opposition. A relation to existence sustained only by resentment would encounter the further challenge of affirmation. These are substantive demands, even though they do not add up to necessary and sufficient conditions for identifying an overman. They allow competing interpretations to be tested against particular features of Nietzsche’s writing.

The horizon interpretation can accept many such demands and recognize genuine achievements along the way. My disagreement is therefore precise: those achievements do not decide whether the figure itself is necessarily unattainable. Conversely, their existence does not prove that it can be realized. What changes is the burden of argument. A prohibition on realization requires more than evidence that creation continues; a prediction of realization requires more than exhortation toward a future. Between those unsupported certainties lies a meaningful question about capacities and forms of life that the present does not exhaust.

The teacher must also change

The narrative of Zarathustra gives the horizon interpretation much of its force. The teacher encounters misunderstanding, seeks companions, and repeatedly confronts the limitations of those who hear him. In Part IV the higher men remain distinct from the overman. Their dissatisfaction with the age and their unusual accomplishments have not carried them beyond the human predicament Zarathustra wants to transform. At the end, the awaited companions are still approaching. No straightforward demonstration resolves the teaching into an achieved example. Zarathustra, IV, “On the Higher Man” and “The Sign.”

But a narrative can withhold fulfillment for several reasons. It may indicate impossibility, preserve uncertainty, or prevent its reader from mistaking a represented example for a model to reproduce. The last possibility deserves particular attention here because Zarathustra explicitly resists the authority of discipleship. At the end of Part I he tells his followers to leave him and find themselves. Remaining believers would frustrate the teaching. His success cannot consist simply in producing people who repeat his words with greater conviction. Zarathustra, I, “On the Gift-Giving Virtue,” §3, titled “The Bestowing Virtue” in Common.

An overman displayed as a complete set of admirable features could become another such authority. Readers might copy the figure’s gestures and regard imitation as liberation. The book’s refusal to supply that model can therefore have an educational function without deciding that the transformation itself is impossible. It denies the reader possession of an answer while requiring a more demanding relation to the question. This is an interpretation of the narrative’s effect, not a claim to have recovered Nietzsche’s single intention. It fits the teacher’s insistence that fidelity may require departure.

The ending also contains an accomplishment that an exclusive emphasis on absence can obscure. Zarathustra recognizes and relinquishes his final temptation, his pity for the higher men, and returns to his work. Whatever ethical judgment we make of that relinquishment, the narrative presents it as a change in him. The future remains unresolved while something decisive occurs in the present. Nonarrival and transformation coexist. We therefore need to distinguish the absence of a conclusively identified overman from the absence of meaningful achievement. Zarathustra, IV, “The Sign.”

That distinction also changes how we understand failure. If Zarathustra’s first audience had accepted the teaching immediately, its acceptance might have revealed only the ease with which a crowd exchanges one promise for another. Misunderstanding exposes the distance between hearing a proposition and changing the desires through which one understands it. The teacher has to learn this distance too. His difficulties can be read as the conditions of a task that remains possible but uncertain, rather than as a coded announcement that the task can never be performed.

Affirmation after convalescence

Eternal recurrence makes the question harder. In The Gay Science §341, a demon asks us to imagine living this very life innumerable times, with every event returning in the same sequence. The provocation gives no opportunity to edit the past or substitute a more successful version. It tests one’s relation to existence through the prospect of repetition. Whether recurrence also functions as a cosmological claim elsewhere need not be settled to recognize the pressure exerted by this passage. Its question concerns the possibility of wanting again what one has actually lived. The Gay Science, §341.

The overman and recurrence therefore cannot be connected simply by imagining an improved future that compensates for everything preceding it. If the past returns, progress does not permanently dispose of what it surpasses. The smallest human beings return along with the greatest. In “The Convalescent,” this is what sickens Zarathustra: his disgust is bound to the recurrence of the human smallness he wants to overcome. His response exposes a possible hostility to life within his aspiration toward something higher. Does he desire transformation because existence can be affirmed more fully, or because he cannot endure existence as it is? Zarathustra, III, “The Convalescent,” §2.

This question is more searching than a demand for constant positivity. Affirmation cannot mean pretending that suffering was pleasant or that cruelty was harmless. Nor should recurrence be reduced to approval of every deed considered separately. The difficulty lies in affirming a life whose elements one cannot simply detach from the whole. An aspiration directed entirely toward the cancellation of the past would fail that test. In the reading proposed here, self-overcoming includes transforming the need for such cancellation, even as one continues to resist and create within the world.

Yet Zarathustra’s sickness is not the book’s last word on his relation to recurrence. After “The Convalescent” come the songs that culminate Part III, including “The Seven Seals.” There he repeatedly addresses eternity with the declaration, “I love thee, O Eternity!” The refrain is joined explicitly to the ring of recurrence. We can debate whether the song represents a stable achievement, a moment of affirmation, or a desire whose fulfillment remains precarious. We cannot fairly treat it as though the narrative had left him simply unable to sing. Zarathustra, III, “The Seven Seals,” §§1–7; translation by Thomas Common.

Neither does this song establish that Zarathustra is the overman. That identification would outrun the evidence in the opposite direction. What the sequence undermines is an argument that moves from his convalescence to the permanent impossibility of affirmation. Nietzsche gives us movement through sickness, recovery, song, and renewed difficulty. An achievement may be real while remaining vulnerable to further tests. Indeed, affirmation that could only count as genuine if it abolished all later struggle would reintroduce the static perfection that self-overcoming challenges.

There is consequently a tension worth preserving between the overman and recurrence. The former draws desire toward transformation; the latter prevents desire from justifying existence only by projecting a future that will redeem it. Neither can simply absorb the other. A possible higher life must somehow sustain creation without making hatred of the actual world its foundation. An unreachable ideal does not automatically solve this tension, since it too can become an accusation against everything that exists. Keeping realization open forces us to ask what an affirmative transformation would actually involve.

The later type and the limits of accident

The later writings offer a different vocabulary, but they do not close the question. In The Antichrist §3, Nietzsche asks what kind of human being should be willed and cultivated. He says that a more valuable type has appeared repeatedly as an exception or fortunate accident, while contrary types have been deliberately encouraged. The distinction concerns the historical conditions under which types have emerged. To turn it into a declaration that deliberate cultivation can never succeed would reverse the question with which the section begins. The Antichrist, §3.

Section 4 rejects automatic progress and denies that chronological succession establishes an increase in human worth. It nevertheless allows exceptional higher types in different places and cultures, describing their relation to humanity generally through a qualified comparison with the overman. This qualification matters. Nietzsche does not simply announce that every exceptional person realizes the central figure of Zarathustra. But neither does he rigorously confine the word to something that existence can never contain. The passage complicates both a straightforward identification and a strict prohibition against identification. The Antichrist, §4.

The distinction between accident and necessity also supports a more demanding account of risk. An exceptional achievement can be possible without being assured by history, and it can depend on conditions that no one fully controls. Refusing a doctrine of progress therefore need not amount to refusing transformation. It removes the assurance that tomorrow will vindicate our hopes merely because it comes later. The burden falls on the quality of the undertaking and its conditions, rather than on a supposed law that humanity is always rising.

In Ecce Homo, Nietzsche directly addresses misunderstandings of the overman. He resists readings that interpret the figure through inherited ideals of saintliness and genius, through Darwinism, or through Carlyle’s hero worship. His objection is not merely that readers have imagined an existing individual. They have understood the figure through valuations his writing challenges. His deliberately provocative comparison between Cesare Borgia and Parsifal makes this especially difficult to reduce to an innocent ideal of personal development. The passage concerns what kind of excellence is at issue, not simply whether excellence can be embodied. Ecce Homo, “Why I Write Such Good Books,” §1.

Goethe provides a useful counterweight to the temptation to make everything future. In Twilight of the Idols, Nietzsche admires his disciplined integration of diverse capacities and describes a spirit able to affirm the whole. He associates the resulting faith with Dionysus. This is not an explicit declaration that Goethe is the overman. Yet the absence of that name cannot establish that historical achievement and the overman occupy mutually exclusive realms. One would need an argument for the exclusivity, rather than treating the difference in vocabulary as sufficient proof. Twilight of the Idols, “Skirmishes of an Untimely Man,” §49.

These passages do not yield a perfectly consistent doctrine. Their figures, emphases, and rhetorical purposes differ. That resistance to consolidation should discipline our conclusion. The evidence supports an orientation toward exceptional possibilities of life, a rejection of inevitable progress, and a concern with how new values might take form. It does not securely establish either a final type guaranteed to emerge or a regulative ideal defined by its permanent exclusion from reality. The open possibility reading asks less uniformity of these texts while preserving the force of their demands.

What the language of height can conceal

The hardest objection to this interpretation is ethical and political. Once the overman remains a possible transformation, language about cultivation and higher types cannot be safely contained within private self-development. But declaring the figure unattainable would not remove the problem either. An ideal can authorize damaging arrangements even when its fulfillment is indefinitely postponed. Its practical significance lies partly in what people believe they may do in its name. The question of realization and the question of justification remain distinct.

Nietzsche’s own texts require us to confront this distinction. Beyond Good and Evil §257 connects the elevation of the human type to aristocratic societies, differences of rank, and slavery in some form. It then relates external distance to the desire for greater distances within the soul. The inward movement is explicit, but it emerges through an account of social domination. We cannot preserve the attractive psychology by pretending that the social claim was never made. Section 259 intensifies the difficulty by treating appropriation and exploitation as features of living organization. Beyond Good and Evil, §§257–259.

This language does not by itself provide a single political program, and the overman should not be equated without argument with a ruler or a hereditary caste. Nevertheless, a reading that makes the figure exclusively harmless would be unfaithful to the evidence. Nietzsche’s challenge to established values reaches conclusions many readers will have good reasons to reject. Understanding those reasons is part of philosophical engagement. We are not obliged to accept the subordination of people as the price of preserving a valuable account of creation.

The language of height can also conceal a psychological dependence. A person who needs others to remain contemptible in order to feel superior has hardly escaped them. Their judgment continues to organize the supposed independence. Nietzsche’s account of reactive valuation in the first essay of On the Genealogy of Morals helps identify this danger, though applying it to self-described Nietzscheans is an extension of the analysis rather than its stated target. Opposition can govern a life as thoroughly as obedience. A posture of disdain is therefore insufficient evidence of self-overcoming. On the Genealogy of Morals, I, §10.

Nor is novelty enough. In Beyond Good and Evil §211, Nietzsche’s philosophers create values and determine a direction for humanity; their role exceeds personal preference. That ambition raises questions about authority, consequences, and the people affected by someone else’s creation. A new valuation can be narrow, destructive, or dependent on self-deception. Calling it creative does not discharge the obligation to examine it. The risk in becoming includes the possibility that what emerges will be worse. Beyond Good and Evil, §211.

The position defended here thus preserves a problem that a comforting interpretation might wish to dissolve. Nietzsche can help us question whether inherited standards exhaust human possibilities without settling which departures deserve our allegiance. The overman opens an evaluative task; it cannot replace that task with the authority of a name. Where Nietzsche’s own valuations fail scrutiny, the demand to examine values should remain available against him. Otherwise his challenge would end by establishing the very dependence on a teacher that Zarathustra asks his disciples to overcome.

A future that has not been decided

The overman remains difficult because Nietzsche gives the figure several kinds of work. It challenges satisfaction with existing humanity, directs desire toward creation, and exposes the inadequacy of merely exceptional achievement within inherited standards. Its relation to recurrence tests whether that desire can survive without condemning existence. Its association with rank and cultivation creates dangers that interpretation must acknowledge. These functions do not assemble themselves into a description of someone we can confidently recognize, but neither do they amount to a proof that such transformation is impossible.

The horizon reading is strongest when it warns against possession: against declaring the work complete, installing a person as its final measure, or turning self-overcoming into an identity immune to challenge. It is weaker when that warning becomes a prohibition on realization itself. Achieving a different capacity need not end becoming. A figure can guide the present without being constitutionally barred from the future. Nietzsche’s texts leave room for these distinctions, and some of their most explicit statements about creation require us to take them seriously.

The alternative to the last man is therefore more demanding than perpetual dissatisfaction and less reassuring than a promised destination. It requires treating our current valuations as revisable, accepting that revision can fail, and asking whether what we create makes a fuller affirmation of life possible. This is the sense in which the overman names a risk of becoming. We are given neither a guarantee that humanity will surpass itself nor the reassurance that genuine transformation is forever out of reach. The future remains a task partly shaped by what we attempt, without becoming a result we can claim in advance.
