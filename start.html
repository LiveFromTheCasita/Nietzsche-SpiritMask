Your first reading: the madman

Also Reading 4 of The Gay Science — Essential Reading Path: What Does “God Is Dead” Mean?

Read: Book III, §§108 and 125, from the original 1882 edition.
Allow: About 40–55 minutes for both passages, this guide, rereading, and your notes. For a shorter first encounter, spend 20–30 minutes on §125 and return to §108 afterward.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): §108 · §125.

Find section 108, “New Struggles,” and read to the end of that short aphorism, stopping before 109. Then find section 125, “The Madman,” and stop before 126. The preface and verse prelude have separate numbering. The explanations below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

A man enters a marketplace carrying a lantern in daylight. Many of the people gathered there already do not believe in God. Read slowly enough to notice who is speaking and how the audience reacts.

Section 108 gives us a short preparation. What happens when a belief loses its authority, but the habits of thought formed around it continue? Keep that possibility in mind as you encounter the madman’s listeners.

The main question: Why would an announcement about God unsettle people who already consider themselves unbelievers?

If you are following the book’s reading path, bring forward Reading 3’s question about the history of our judgments. Here the issue becomes what happens when an inherited source of authority loses its hold.

Three things to notice

  1. In §108, what can a shadow preserve after its source is gone? What further struggle does the speaker announce?
  2. In §125, what does the lantern suggest about looking for something in an apparently well-lit world? Why does the madman describe something people have done?
  3. What could it mean for an event to have happened while people have not yet grasped its consequences? Follow the change from laughter to silence.

A way to read the passages

The shadow that remains: §108

Nietzsche begins with an image of Buddha’s shadow being shown long after Buddha’s death. He then moves to the death of God and imagines shadows that could remain for millennia. The passage distinguishes a disappearance from the end of its influence. Read §108

The shadow gives us a way to think about beliefs and the expectations built around them. Someone might cease to believe that the world has a divine purpose while continuing to expect that life must possess one final purpose, already established and waiting to be discovered. That expectation could survive the belief that formerly supported it.

This is an illustrative possibility, rather than a claim that every unbeliever thinks in this way. Section 108 does not supply a complete list of the shadows. Its last sentence announces the work of overcoming them, leaving us to investigate what might count as a continuing influence.

Keep the image in view when the madman arrives. His listeners may have given up a belief without understanding the extent of the change in which they have participated.

A listener who already disbelieves: §125

The guide reads the death of God as a crisis in the authority of inherited values. Giving up a religious belief may be easier than understanding how deeply a culture’s ideas of truth, duty, and purpose depend on it. On this reading, the audience’s laughter shows a gap between disbelief and understanding. Read §125

This is an interpretation to test against the images and dramatic action. The madman is a character in the passage; do not assume every sentence is a straightforward statement in Nietzsche’s own voice. His listeners initially treat the search for God as a joke. Their answers make God sound like someone who has wandered away or gone on a journey.

The madman answers by making them participants. His repeated language of “we” and “you and I” turns attention toward a shared deed and its consequences. He is not mainly trying to persuade believers with an argument against God’s existence. The unbelievers are already there; he is asking what their situation means.

Losing a horizon

The images enlarge that problem. Wiping away a horizon removes a boundary by which a world has been oriented. Unfastening the earth from its sun brings questions about direction, movement, warmth, and light. The loss is described through the conditions that have allowed people to find their bearings.

That helps explain the lantern in daylight. An apparently illuminated public world may still fail to understand what has happened within it. The image invites us to question the audience’s confidence in its own clear-sightedness.

The speech also asks about cleansing, consolation, and new sacred practices. Those questions expose the scale of the task as the madman experiences it. They are not a completed program for creating new values, and their urgency should not be mistaken for a demonstrated answer.

Why does the madman come too early?

The audience finally falls silent, but silence is not yet understanding. The madman describes events whose effects need time to arrive, using lightning, thunder, and distant starlight. An event and the comprehension of its meaning can be separated by a long interval.

Read this beside §108’s shadow. One passage imagines what persists after a disappearance; the other imagines the delay before a deed is understood. Together they make the change something that must unfold through thought, feeling, institutions, and practice.

The final visits to the churches sharpen the difficulty. The madman describes them as tombs and monuments of God: places whose continued existence may preserve a relation to what he declares dead. The passage ends in an image, leaving the reader to examine how survival and loss can occupy the same place.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does losing one foundation mean that every value collapses?

The madman’s imagery makes disorientation vivid. Vividness alone does not establish that every moral judgment depends on religious belief, or that no other justification could be developed. Those are further questions to investigate.

Suppose a community once justified a practice by divine command. Discovering that this justification has lost authority raises the question of what reasons remain. It does not, by itself, decide whether the practice should continue. Historical dependence, present justification, and practical consequences need to be distinguished.

Apply the same care to the shadow in §108. Calling an idea a religious inheritance is not a complete assessment of its truth or value. We must ask what has persisted, whether the supposed connection is well supported, and what follows from identifying it.

The challenge of these passages is to take the loss of an inherited horizon seriously while examining the conclusions drawn from it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does the audience matter?

Because its members already disbelieve. Their failure to understand suggests the passage concerns the consequences of a cultural change, rather than merely announcing atheism.

What does §108 add to the madman passage?

Its shadow image suggests that the influence of a belief can outlast belief itself. This helps explain why the madman’s listeners might still have work to do despite their disbelief. The passage leaves the particular shadows to be investigated.

Does the passage prove that nothing has value?

No. It raises the problem of what happens to inherited sources of value. Whether and how new valuations or justifications are possible requires further reading.

Why does the madman say that he has come too early?

He describes a gap between a deed and the understanding of its consequences. His listeners can participate in a historical change without grasping what it will unsettle. The images of delayed light and sound make that gap imaginable.

What should I write in my notes?

Write six to eight sentences explaining the difference between abandoning a belief and understanding its consequences.

  1. Use the shadow in §108 and one image from §125 to support your explanation.
  2. Give one possible example of an expectation that could survive a belief. Mark the example as your hypothesis.
  3. Explain what would still need to be shown before concluding that a particular value had lost its justification.
  4. Finish with one question the madman’s speech leaves open.

For the shorter §125-only reading, write two or three sentences identifying the gap between disbelief and understanding, one supporting image, and one remaining question.
