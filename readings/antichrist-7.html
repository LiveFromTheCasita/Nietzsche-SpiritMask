Before you read

Nietzsche has argued that Christianity reversed the nonresentful practice he attributes to Jesus. These sections test that thesis through textual examples. How does a reader move from what a passage says to what its speakers want, and from selected passages to a judgment about an entire collection?

Read §45's examples as Nietzsche presents them, not as independently verified biblical transcriptions. Attached notes sometimes clarify quotation boundaries; those notes are editorial. Distinguish the cited voices, Nietzsche's remarks, and his additional claim to explain the motives behind them.

The main question: What can a conflict between a text's declared virtues and its threatened consequences reveal, and what can it fail to prove?

Three things to notice

  1. Virtue grants authority In §44 Nietzsche asks who receives the power to judge when judgment is assigned to God. Track how the declaration of humility can accompany a claim to represent the sole legitimate standard.
  2. Reasons alter the action Section 45 selects threats, promises, and comparisons. Ask what happens to an apparently generous act when its stated reason invokes reward, reciprocal treatment, or superiority to outsiders.
  3. A verdict exceeds its examples Sections 46–47 move from selected passages to categorical judgments about Christians, God, and knowledge. Identify each additional inference rather than allowing the force of the rhetoric to supply it.

A way to read the passages

Reading virtue as an exercise of power

Section 44 treats the Gospels as evidence of an already transformed community. Nietzsche reads declarations of goodness as actions within a struggle over authority. If a group identifies itself with truth and places opponents outside truth, apparent self-effacement can accompany an immense claim to govern everyone's standards. Its moral vocabulary can establish an asymmetry without announcing a desire for power.

His central example pairs a refusal to judge with condemnation delegated to God. In the proposed mechanism, speakers perform the judgment while presenting themselves as its obedient messengers. Similarly, necessities imposed by a group's position can be elevated into virtues that everybody ought to possess. The interpretation asks how a description of one's own condition becomes a universal measure, and how that measure benefits those who already satisfy it.

But §44 also explains deception through inherited Jewish traits and uses openly anti-Jewish, racialized language. Those assertions are part of Nietzsche's argument here, not neutral historical findings. They do no evidential work simply by invoking inheritance: he would need to establish the alleged behavior and its transmission rather than treating group identity as an explanation. The more precise analysis of moral authority can be assessed without accepting his prejudicial generalization about a people.

The reason for an action can change its meaning

Section 45 is an adversarial selection. Nietzsche presents passages attributed to Mark, Matthew, Luke, and Paul, then adds terse comments. Examples of punishment, destruction, vindication, and reward challenge the identification of Christian teaching with the uncalculating, nonresentful practice described in §§29–35.

Several examples turn on motivation. An instruction to forgive can look different when it is supported by the prospect of receiving forgiveness oneself. An instruction to love beyond one's immediate circle can look different when Nietzsche reads its accompanying comparison as a demand for superior reward. His underlying question is whether the reason offered for an action reintroduces calculation or rivalry into conduct presented as generous.

His question is productive without making the sarcastic answers compulsory. A reason can appeal to existing motives while educating them, or describe a consequence without making it the action's sole purpose. Context might alter a warning's force. Distinguish the conduct requested, the reason Nietzsche attributes to the passage, and his conclusion that this reason governs those who act upon it.

Contradiction is evidence requiring interpretation

The clash between nonjudgment and threatened judgment supports Nietzsche's larger distinction between Jesus and later Christianity. Yet a collection containing different voices is not automatically a single speaker caught in hypocrisy. A tension might reflect different authors, occasions, audiences, or conceptions of judgment. Even within one discourse, a distinction between private vengeance and another kind of judgment could be relevant. Such possibilities require investigation rather than automatic acceptance or dismissal.

Nietzsche's earlier method also creates a special problem. In §31 he selected nonresentful traits as the original type and assigned militant features to later distortion. In §45 the militant and punitive material now helps establish that later corruption. If the evidence was already sorted using the conclusion, the argument risks becoming circular. He needs some reason, beyond agreement with his portrait, to assign the competing strands their historical places.

Yet plurality does not settle the practical question. A community may endorse diverse passages together and claim a unified moral authority for the collection. One can ask how that community reconciles forgiveness with its use of threats. This narrower inquiry concerns an identifiable interpretation and use of texts, rather than declaring that diversity proves either universal hypocrisy or perfect compatibility.

The reader's disgust becomes a criterion

Section 46 intensifies the indictment through images of dirt, bad smell, and contamination, including a degrading comparison involving Polish Jews. These are devices for producing distance and aversion, not evidence about literary meaning or human worth. Nietzsche contrasts the New Testament with a literary figure he experiences as healthy and cheerful. The contrast tells us how his evaluative sensibility organizes the reading, but an experienced aversion cannot by itself establish the motives of an author.

He then proposes an inverse criterion: what an early Christian attacks acquires value by being attacked. His discussion of scribes, Pharisees, worldly wisdom, and privilege follows this reversal. Yet being condemned by a mistaken opponent does not make every target admirable. The inference substitutes allegiance for assessment. It also returns to §43's defense of rank: Nietzsche treats hostility to privilege as a symptom against the critic, which reveals his own standard rather than independently proving the privilege deserved.

Pilate becomes the favored figure because Nietzsche reads his question about truth as aristocratic distance from religious claims. This is Nietzsche's appropriation of a literary scene, not a verified historical assessment of Pilate. It also strains the earlier sympathetic portrait of Jesus: §46's sweeping denial of sympathetic features in the New Testament is difficult to align with §§29–35 unless one distinguishes the type Nietzsche constructs from the writings he now condemns.

Rejecting a valuation differs from disproving an existence

Section 47 shifts from the texts to the value of the God they authorize. Nietzsche says the central issue is not an inability to locate God in nature or history. His objection concerns whether the being or ideal called God merits reverence. Even a demonstration of existence would not establish worthiness of worship. This separates an evidential question about what exists from an evaluative question about what deserves affirmation.

He interprets Paul's God as a projection of a will opposed to worldly wisdom. Philology and medicine matter because each replaces a sacred explanation with a disciplined inquiry into conditions: one investigates the production and interpretation of texts, the other bodily states. In Nietzsche's account, an unconditional command to believe protects religious authority from those investigations. Faith becomes an instruction about what inquiry must not be allowed to overturn.

His conclusion remains more absolute than the distinction requires. The claim that every competent philologist or physician must oppose Christianity assumes that these disciplines can yield only his verdict. Critical inquiry instead requires room for findings that unsettle the investigator's expectations as well as the believer's. This gives the lesson a final test: does Nietzsche apply to his own psychological and physiological claims the freedom from preassigned conclusions he demands from everyone else?

A difficulty to keep in view

Does interpreting a speaker's motives make it unnecessary to answer what the speaker says?

No. A claim may serve someone's interests and still be true; generosity can have mixed motives. A psychological interpretation might challenge an assertion of complete selflessness, explain persuasive appeal, or show how authority is distributed. None of those achievements automatically refutes every proposition a passage contains.

The strongest reading of §§44–45 treats the stated reasons and threatened consequences as material for testing a claimed ethos. The weakest treats disgust or attributed ancestry as sufficient to decide the matter. Section 47 raises the stakes by defending intellectual conscience: Nietzsche's own account must allow evidence to distinguish between these possibilities. Otherwise suspicion becomes an equally protected conviction, able to redescribe every response as further proof of concealed dishonesty.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

How can delegating judgment to God preserve human judgment in §44?

The community still identifies who stands condemned and claims authority to announce the divine verdict. Nietzsche argues that the posture of obedience can conceal the group's active role in assigning moral standing.

Why does Nietzsche attend to the reasons accompanying conduct in §45?

He thinks reward, reciprocity, and superiority can make an apparently generous action calculative or competitive. Assessing that inference requires distinguishing the action, the stated reason, and the actual motives of particular agents.

Why does disagreement among passages not by itself prove hypocrisy?

Different voices and contexts may explain a textual tension, whereas hypocrisy requires a more specific relation between profession and conduct or intention. Nietzsche must justify treating the collection as evidence of one shared deceptive orientation.

What is the problem with §46's inverse criterion of value?

An opponent's condemnation does not establish the worth of its target. Nietzsche's reversal can replace independent evaluation with group alignment, especially when it assumes that attacks on privilege already count against the attacker.

What distinction opens §47, and how does it lead toward intellectual conscience?

Nietzsche separates God's existence from the value of what is worshipped as God. He then attacks a command to believe that prevents investigation, while exposing his own account to the question of whether it permits contrary evidence.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 250–350 words answering: How much can Nietzsche establish through one example in §45?

  1. Choose an example involving judgment, forgiveness, reward, or the superiority of believers. Paraphrase its role in Nietzsche's selection and state a precise claim about what his comment reveals.
  2. Connect that evidence to one mechanism in §44, such as delegated judgment or a claim to exclusive moral authority. Explain the connection rather than assuming that a harsh tone proves the mechanism.
  3. Present a serious alternative reading based on context, mixed motives, or textual plurality. Answer it, and distinguish any criticism your example supports from a conclusion about all Christians or the entire New Testament.

End by naming the additional evidence that would most strengthen or weaken your interpretation.
