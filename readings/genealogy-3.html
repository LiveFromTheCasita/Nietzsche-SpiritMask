How Does Resentment Create Values?

On the Genealogy of Morals — Essential Reading Path · Reading 3 of 12

Read: Essay I §§8–13. Stop before §14.

Before you read

Imagine defining a good person by listing everything a hated rival is not. The rival seeks recognition, so goodness must be indifferent to recognition. The rival speaks confidently, so goodness must speak modestly. Your own qualities become admirable through this contrast.

You may have valid complaints against the rival. But notice a further question: does the rival’s conduct provide the evidence for particular criticisms, or has opposition become the organizing principle of your entire standard?

This example is ours. It introduces Nietzsche’s account of ressentiment, in which blocked hostility becomes creative by establishing values. His argument concerns more than feeling angry after an injury.

Reading 2 followed the proposed conflict between aristocratic self-affirmation and priestly reversal. These sections develop the psychological mechanism, confront the violence of the nobles, and ask what picture of agency lets weakness become moral merit.

The main question: What happens when the need to condemn an enemy supplies the first principle of goodness?

Read Essay I, §§8–13 in order. Section 9 contains a freethinker’s speech; its judgments must be distinguished from Nietzsche’s own interventions.

Three things to notice

  1. Love emerging from hatred. In I §8, what relationship does Nietzsche assert between Christian love and the earlier reversal of values?
  2. The order of naming. In I §§10–11, which comes first: affirming oneself or condemning an enemy? Why does good have a different meaning in each opposition?
  3. The agent behind the action. In I §13, follow the movement from lambs and predators to the grammar of lightning. What must Nietzsche establish beyond the animal comparison?

A way to read the passages

I §8: love is presented as the crown of revenge

Nietzsche continues the anti-Jewish narrative begun in I §7. He pictures Christian love growing from a tree rooted in hatred, then insists that the new growth advances the reversal of values rather than overcoming its source. Essay I, §8

The claim is not merely that something good can develop from an ugly beginning. He presents love as the successful crown of the same historical revenge. Jesus’s promise to the poor, sick, and sinful makes the new valuation attractive to people who might otherwise have rejected it.

The crucifixion becomes central to the proposed seduction. Nietzsche represents the rejection of Jesus and the spectacle of a crucified God as means through which the reversal conquers its opponents. The image of supreme sacrifice makes the inheritance more compelling.

This account moves into conspiratorial language: an entire people appears to calculate a strategy, including rejection and crucifixion, across an immense history. The passage supplies no evidence for such a collective plan. Its attribution of revenge and responsibility reproduces anti-Jewish accusations and must not be repeated as historical fact.

Nor should it become a diagnosis that every Christian’s love is consciously fraudulent. Nietzsche is describing the direction of a moral inheritance, though his strategic language repeatedly assigns intentions to that history. The relation between an outcome, its function, and anyone’s actual purpose remains something his account must establish.

I §9: a listener accepts the victory and welcomes it

The next section begins inside quotation marks. A freethinker responds to Nietzsche’s account: if ordinary people have defeated the masters, why lament the result? He accepts much of the proposed history while disputing the preference for aristocratic ideals. Essay I, §9

The distinction is instructive. Even someone who granted the account of origins might judge its outcome differently. Nietzsche’s history does not automatically compel his evaluation.

The listener also separates the Church from the morality that has spread through it. The institution may now hinder the very development it once promoted. Free thinkers can reject ecclesiastical authority while preserving the values Nietzsche attacks.

The speech shares the surrounding vocabulary of poison and racial mixture; changing speakers does not make those images neutral. Its particular intervention is to welcome the outcome despite that vocabulary.

At the end Nietzsche identifies the speaker as a democrat and an honorable person who has interrupted his silence. Nietzsche’s closing reservation does not settle the disagreement. Do not attribute the entire quoted celebration of emancipation from masters directly to him.

I §10: a blocked reaction becomes a source of values

Section 10 names the mechanism: ressentiment becomes creative. People deprived of a direct outlet for action seek compensation through imagined revenge. The enemy’s condemnation becomes the beginning from which they construct their own goodness. Essay I, §10

The French term names an enduring, organizing hostility in this argument. It is not equivalent to every moment of resentment, every protest against mistreatment, or every acknowledgment that someone has caused harm.

Nietzsche contrasts two orders of valuation. Noble valuation begins by affirming its own life and capacities. Its low or bad counterpart emerges afterward as a comparatively secondary contrast. Reactive valuation begins by identifying the evil other; the good self is then defined as that other’s opposite.

The difference concerns dependence. The reactive standard needs an external enemy in order to establish itself. Its apparent self-description remains organized around what it denies.

Return to the rival in our opening example. Confidence may be criticized for a precise reason, such as concealing uncertainty. But if every confidence becomes evidence of corruption simply because the rival exhibits it, opposition has begun producing the standard. The example illustrates a mechanism without proving that every excluded or injured person responds this way.

Calling this creative is essential. Ressentiment does not merely complain about existing values. It establishes new distinctions that can organize conduct, institutions, and historical victory. Its origin in blocked action does not mean that its effects remain powerless.

The portraits include happiness, memory, and enemies

The remainder of I §10 gives the contrast emotional depth. Noble happiness appears active and immediate, whereas the happiness Nietzsche attributes to the oppressed takes the form of respite, numbness, or relief from strain. He contrasts open confidence with watchfulness, concealment, and prolonged calculation.

He admits that nobles can experience resentment. His distinction is that their reaction rapidly discharges itself or disappears through a capacity to forget. Mirabeau supplies his example of someone whose forgetfulness makes sustained grievance impossible.

The noble can also respect a worthy enemy. Nietzsche imagines this respect becoming a bridge toward love. The reactive person instead fashions an evil enemy whose condemnation is necessary to the good self.

These are idealized, strongly unequal portraits. Nietzsche claims that noble contempt falsifies its object less radically than vindictive hatred does, although he admits that nobles misunderstand the people they disregard. Neither his preference nor his examples establish a universal psychological law about dominant and subordinated people.

I §11: the noble’s victims have encountered real violence

Section 11 clarifies the two moral oppositions. Good–bad begins with noble self-affirmation; good–evil begins with the condemned enemy. The word good therefore changes its meaning across the two systems. The noble who calls himself good becomes the evil person of the opposing valuation. Essay I, §11

Nietzsche then makes an admission that prevents the contrast from remaining flattering to the nobles. Their conduct toward equals is restrained by custom, mutual supervision, gratitude, and loyalty. Outside that circle they can behave as predators.

The text explicitly names murder, arson, rape, and torture, followed by a return to ordinary life without troubled conscience. Noble innocence here concerns freedom from guilty self-interpretation. It does not mean that nobody was injured or that the violence was harmless.

This gives victims grounds for fear and condemnation beyond imaginary caricature. The account of ressentiment must confront that fact. Nietzsche’s admission is stronger than saying that powerful people occasionally behave inconsiderately.

The image of the blond beast belongs to this predatory release. Nietzsche includes Roman, Arab, German, and Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, and Vikings. The list prevents limiting the image to one literal hair color or one modern national population.

But that breadth does not make the passage politically or racially innocent. The animal image expresses a social elite’s violent mastery, and the section also returns to racial claims about descendants of subordinated populations. Nietzsche’s disclaimer of close continuity between ancient and contemporary Germans does not erase the larger racial hierarchy.

Civilization’s success becomes Nietzsche’s objection

Nietzsche interprets opposed memories of the heroic past through the contrast between aristocratic descendants and descendants of their victims. His account of Hesiod’s ages makes one world appear magnificent from one side and crushingly brutal from another. That interpretation is part of his reconstruction, not independent proof of it.

He next provisionally accepts civilization’s aim as taming the predator. If that is the aim, reactive instincts have helped achieve it. But he refuses to identify the agents of that achievement with an admirable human outcome.

His preference is severe: he would rather face a frightening figure who can also inspire admiration than encounter a harmless humanity he finds diminished and repellent. Safety and successful domestication do not satisfy his standard.

This is a value judgment, not something established by the preceding history. It gives the defender of civilization a direct objection to make: why should preserving possibilities of greatness outweigh protection from the violence Nietzsche himself describes?

I §12: nihilism appears as exhaustion with humanity

Section 12 makes the evaluative stake personal. Nietzsche longs to see a fully realized, powerful human being who could restore hope in human existence. His account of decline concerns the loss of anything that invites a more demanding aspiration. Essay I, §12

In this setting nihilism is weariness with humanity: losing hope and the will to be human as people become safer, more comfortable, and less capable of inspiring admiration. The diagnosis concerns an inability to find a human future worth wanting.

The diagnosis remains hostile to leveling. Its list of supposedly diminished traits also includes a disparaging comparison with Chinese people. That racial stereotype belongs to Nietzsche’s picture of decline and supplies no evidence for it.

The connection with the Preface is now clear. A morality can count its effects as improvement while Nietzsche judges those effects a threat to life’s future. His standard of a humanity worth hoping for still requires defense against other accounts of what improvement means.

I §13: lambs construct the goodness of being unlike predators

Nietzsche returns to reactive goodness through an animal comparison. Lambs call birds of prey evil, then identify the opposite kind of being—the lamb—as good. Their perspective makes the order of valuation easy to see. Essay I, §13

The birds answer with an appetite that needs no corresponding hatred: they like lambs as food. This is a deliberately brutal contrast between moral condemnation and predatory enjoyment. It does not offer a reassuring account of relations between the strong and weak.

Nietzsche argues that requiring strength not to express itself as strength is as absurd as requiring weakness to act as strength. He describes strength through overpowering, mastering, and seeking opposition. That conception is itself substantive; the fable cannot establish that every human capacity must take those forms.

The next step attacks a picture of agency. Popular morality imagines a neutral subject behind the expression of strength, able to choose freely whether to manifest it. Such a subject would allow the predator to become a lamb while remaining, underneath, the same independent chooser.

Lightning exposes the supposed doer behind the deed

Nietzsche compares this picture with separating lightning from its flash and treating the flash as an additional action performed by lightning. Language encourages us to duplicate one event into a thing and what the thing does.

His claim is categorical: no independent being stands behind doing, acting, and becoming. The supposed doer is added to the deed. He extends the criticism to scientific talk of forces and subjects, showing that the target reaches beyond one moral habit.

The moral use of the imagined subject runs in two directions. It makes the strong responsible for choosing to be strong, and lets the weak interpret an inability to act otherwise as voluntary restraint. Necessary weakness becomes a freely chosen achievement deserving praise.

This is Nietzsche’s explanation of how the idea of a soul or free subject can support self-preservation and moral self-esteem. It does not demonstrate that every instance of restraint is disguised inability, or that all claims of agency depend on the neutral substratum he attacks.

People can learn, assess reasons, and develop capacities to inhibit an action. Those activities could belong to the doing rather than to a mysterious agent outside it. An adequate account must explain these capacities without treating them as proof of a neutral subject behind action. Rejecting that subject does not by itself settle which forms of agency remain possible.

Pause here: Separate three claims: moral judgment can misdescribe a motive; grammar can mislead us about agency; human conduct cannot meaningfully be assessed. Which does the text argue for, and which would require a further argument?

A difficulty to keep in view

Can the diagnosis of ressentiment become a way to dismiss justified criticism?

Yes, if the critic’s anger is treated as sufficient proof that their complaint is false. I §11 gives the danger a concrete basis: people called evil by their enemies have committed actual atrocities. An account of the enemies’ emotional investment cannot make those events disappear.

Nietzsche’s more searching question concerns what a response becomes. A justified objection might develop into an outlook requiring permanent enemies, or a portrait of oneself whose merit consists entirely in not being like them. Evidence of injury and evaluation of the resulting standard remain separate inquiries.

The same care applies to I §13. Rejecting a wholly independent chooser does not automatically abolish distinctions among intention, coercion, learning, and restraint. Nor does it settle how to protect people, repair harm, or assess conduct. These sections give no complete account of responsibility or institutions.

Keep Nietzsche’s own investment visible. He wants a human future capable of inspiring hope, and fears that prevailing morality blocks it. That concern can disclose costs others overlook. It can also tempt him to discount the claims of people harmed by the capacities he admires.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Does I §8 say Christian love simply overcame the revenge from which it arose?

No. Nietzsche presents love as the crown and vehicle of the same reversal of values. His collective, strategic account contains anti-Jewish accusations that the section does not establish as historical fact.

Who welcomes the defeat of the masters in I §9?

A quoted freethinker whom Nietzsche identifies afterward as a democrat. The listener accepts much of the proposed history but judges its outcome differently. His speech should not be mistaken for Nietzsche’s own endorsement.

What makes ressentiment creative in I §10?

Blocked hostility establishes a new ranking: the evil enemy comes first, and the good self emerges as its opposite. Noble valuation starts from self-affirmation and develops its bad counterpart second. The difference concerns the structure of the standards.

Why does I §11 prevent an innocent portrait of noble strength?

Nietzsche explicitly describes predation and atrocities outside the nobles’ own circle. Their lack of guilt does not mean lack of injury. His admiration for frightening greatness remains an evaluative preference that victims have grounds to question.

Does the lightning comparison prove that accountability is meaningless?

No. It attacks an independent subject supposedly standing behind action and choosing whether to express its strength. Distinctions involving reasons, restraint, coercion, and responses to harm need further consideration. The image does not settle all those questions.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to the value examined in your earlier notes. Write 200–300 words:

  1. Explain whether its definition begins with an admired activity or with something it condemns.
  2. Identify a possible injury that the condemnation recognizes. Separate known facts from imagined motives.
  3. Use I §10 to ask whether opposition has become necessary to the value’s identity.
  4. Use I §11 or §13 to state a limit on what your psychological explanation establishes.
  5. Give a reason to retain, revise, or reject the value without treating its emotional origin as the entire verdict.

A successful response does not diagnose every grievance as ressentiment or assume that confident strength justifies its own effects.
