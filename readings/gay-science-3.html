Where Do Our Truths and Values Come From?

The Gay Science — Essential Reading Path · Reading 3 of 10

Read: Book III, §110, then §116, from the original 1882 edition. Read both aphorisms in full, stopping before §111 and §117 respectively.
Allow: About 50–65 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): §110 · §116.

The free links open near the assigned aphorisms. Find the numbered headings 110 and 116 if the text is not highlighted. Section 110 is substantially longer; pause at its final question before turning to §116.

Before you read

Imagine being taught that a decent person always keeps a promise. At first, other people remind you. Eventually, the expectation speaks in your own voice. Breaking a promise can make you feel ashamed even when nobody else knows about it.

Now ask why this expectation took hold. Perhaps the people who taught you depended heavily on being able to trust one another. That would help explain their insistence. You could still ask whether a particular promise ought to be kept, and what reasons support that judgment.

The example distinguishes the history of a conviction from its present justification. Nietzsche makes this distinction difficult and urgent by asking how deeply our histories enter the things that seem obvious to us.

Reading 1 asked us to examine our reasons for believing. Reading 2 explored how art might sustain that honesty, then introduced a thinker who discovers the past working within his own experience. Here we investigate how convictions and moral expectations acquire their hold.

The main question: What changes when we discover that the judgments we trust have been shaped by the requirements of life?

Three things to notice

  1. The source of a conviction’s strength. In §110, what makes an assumption feel indispensable? Distinguish that strength from the evidence for its truth.
  2. The change within the history of knowledge. Find the point where questioning and doubt become needs themselves. How does this change the conflict at the end of §110?
  3. The community within the individual. In §116, what gets ranked, and by whose requirements? Notice how a standard for useful conduct becomes a measure of personal worth.

A way to read the passages

Convictions can become conditions of living

Section 110 begins with a sweeping history. Nietzsche proposes that certain errors helped living beings survive and reproduce. As they persisted, they became deeply incorporated into human functioning. Later thinkers encountered them as assumptions already built into how they perceived and judged. §110, the opening account

His examples reach beneath everyday opinions: enduring things, equal things, substances, free will, and the assumption that what benefits oneself is good absolutely. These belong to different kinds of judgment. By placing them together, Nietzsche asks how our basic ways of organizing experience acquired their apparent necessity.

Here incorporated means taken into the workings of a life. Something can guide perception, expectation, and action before we deliberately examine it. Changing a sentence in our notes may leave the habit of judging almost untouched.

Imagine sorting apples for sale by treating the members of one batch as equal. Their differences disappear for the purpose of counting and exchanging them. This illustrates how a practical activity can depend on treating different things alike. It does not establish Nietzsche’s much larger claim that our basic categories are errors. That claim requires its own argument.

The aphorism offers an ambitious reconstruction of how knowledge developed. It does not provide historical or biological evidence sufficient to establish its entire story. Follow what the explanation would change while keeping its evidential demands in view.

What feels unquestionable can have a history

Nietzsche’s next move raises the stakes. The inherited assumptions become standards by which other claims are judged true or false. Their authority reaches into the procedures that would ordinarily examine them. A doubter may therefore appear irrational for challenging something the community treats as a condition of rational judgment.

Notice the difference between a conviction’s grip and its truth. Nietzsche explains its grip through age, incorporation, and its role in sustaining life. A belief can feel unavoidable because so much of our activity depends on it. That feeling does not settle whether it describes things accurately.

He nevertheless calls the opening convictions errors. We should neither quietly soften that claim nor accept it merely because he supplies a possible history. An account of why an assumption survived and an argument showing where it fails are different achievements.

Return to Reading 1’s demand for intellectual conscience. We may need to examine the standards through which we recognize a reason, as well as the particular reasons we give. The investigation becomes more demanding when its own familiar tools acquire a history.

The investigator belongs inside the investigation

The passage then discusses the Eleatics, a group of early Greek thinkers. For this reading, focus on the portrait Nietzsche draws: a sage who believes that knowledge can become wholly impersonal and unchanging, free of the drives that shape ordinary thinking.

Nietzsche questions that self-description. The thinker may still be moved by a desire for repose, possession, or dominance while imagining reason to be independent of such desires. The supposedly exceptional investigator remains a living person with a history and an investment in the inquiry.

Recall the ending of §2 in Reading 1, where the critic notices something questionable in his own expectations. Here the demand for scrutiny again turns toward the person making it. Recognizing other people’s inherited assumptions does not establish our freedom from them.

This also reconnects with §54 in Reading 2. Awareness of the conditions of thought does not place the thinker outside those conditions. It gives the thinker something further to investigate.

How doubt becomes a need

The story now changes. Different judgments can coexist without immediately threatening life. People can compare their usefulness, play with possibilities, and become involved in intellectual conflicts. Inquiry acquires attractions of its own.

Nietzsche describes questioning becoming an occupation, a duty, a source of honor, and eventually a need. Distrust and contradiction can gain standing as activities that serve knowledge. Habits once regarded with suspicion acquire a place within an admired practice.

We can see a limited everyday analogy in learning to check our work. At first, we may check because someone requires it. With practice, an unanswered question can begin to trouble us independently. The demand becomes part of how we work. Nietzsche’s account concerns a far larger historical transformation, but the analogy helps us recognize what it means for inquiry to acquire force within a life.

This is why we must follow §110 all the way to its end. The drive to truth eventually becomes a power that sustains life. The thinker is now the place where that drive confronts older, deeply incorporated errors.

The experiment at the end of §110

The final conflict takes place between two forces within the same person. Both belong to life; both possess power. Reading the passage as a simple choice between useful illusion and destructive truth would miss the transformation Nietzsche has just described.

His closing question asks how far truth can be incorporated. What would it mean for an insight to enter our habits, perceptions, and conduct? How much of what we discover can become something we actually live?

Suppose you recognize that your confidence in a judgment outruns the evidence. Writing that recognition down is one step. Becoming able to hesitate, ask a better question, or revise your judgment under pressure would show the recognition entering your practice. This is a modest example of the problem, rather than an answer to Nietzsche’s wider experiment.

Pause here: In two sentences, explain why the drive to truth has a different position at the end of §110 from the one it had near the beginning. Then read §116.

Morality orders what a community needs

Section 116 shifts from convictions about the world to the ranking of human drives and activities. A morality gives some traits higher standing than others. Nietzsche asks what those rankings express: which requirements of a community make a particular trait seem admirable, shameful, or indispensable? §116, the account of moral ranking

His claim is strong. Wherever there is a morality, he says, its ordering expresses the needs of a community. Different conditions of preservation help explain why communities have had different moralities. Future changes in their conditions would likewise bring changes in their values.

Think again of promise-keeping. A community’s dependence on reliability could help explain the importance it gives the trait. Another question concerns its rank: when reliability conflicts with candor, mercy, or independent judgment, which should take priority? A moral order determines relationships among valued activities, as well as praising individual traits.

This example illustrates what it means to examine a ranking. The aphorism itself supplies no detailed history of promise-keeping, and its broad claim about every morality remains something to test.

The herd learns to speak through self-worth

Nietzsche’s sharper claim concerns the individual. Morality teaches a person to value themselves through the function they perform for the community. A social requirement becomes an inward measure: being useful in the prescribed way feels like being a worthwhile person.

That is why the closing reference to herd instinct reaches beyond copying a crowd. The community’s expectations can operate through an apparently private conscience. In our opening example, nobody needs to be watching for a broken promise to produce shame.

The word herd carries a criticism of this reduction of individual worth to collective function. We should feel that pressure in Nietzsche’s language. We should also resist treating the word as a ready-made verdict on other people. A dissenter might still judge themselves through the expectations of a community that rewards dissent.

The investigation therefore asks something more searching than whether we behave conventionally. Which needs speak through the standards by which we admire or condemn ourselves? What possibilities in a person disappear when those standards become the whole measure of their worth?

Reading the two accounts together

Both aphorisms unsettle the authority of what seems self-evident. Section 110 follows assumptions into the workings of knowing, then follows inquiry as it becomes a need itself. Section 116 follows community requirements into moral ranking and personal self-estimation.

The objects of examination differ. A belief about enduring things and a demand to keep promises call for different kinds of assessment. Their shared point is that we can investigate how a judgment acquired its hold without pretending that the person investigating stands outside history.

That is a beginning of the approach developed elsewhere on this site as genealogy: tracing the development and functions of our values so that their authority becomes available for examination.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does explaining where a judgment came from give us grounds to reject it?

If you learned to value keeping promises because your community needed reliability, that history would not by itself make reliability worthless. Trust might still provide a reason to keep a particular promise. We would need to examine the promise and the competing claims upon us.

But the history can change the argument. Suppose a value claims authority because it supposedly expresses an eternal standard, independent of every human requirement. Evidence that its ranking changes with a community’s conditions would put pressure on that claim to independence. The value might need a different defense.

Likewise, if confidence in a belief rests chiefly on its familiarity or usefulness, discovering that source of confidence can reveal a gap in its support. The investigation matters even when it stops short of demonstrating that the belief is false.

Keep three questions separate as you work: How did this judgment take hold? What has it enabled, and for whom? What reasons support accepting or revising it now? A historical explanation can alter the last question without automatically answering it.

Nietzsche owes us scrutiny too. What would count as evidence for his histories? Could his claim about every morality accommodate a value that repeatedly threatens the community in which it arose? These questions test the reach of the explanation while preserving what it has helped us notice.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

In §110, what explains the strength of inherited convictions?

Nietzsche points to their age, their incorporation into human functioning, and their role in sustaining life. These factors can explain why they feel necessary. Explaining that feeling is different from establishing their truth.

Why does Nietzsche question the supposedly impersonal sage?

The sage may mistake their own desires and drives for an activity of reason wholly independent of life. Nietzsche brings the investigator into the investigation: claims to disinterested knowledge also require scrutiny.

What changes when truth-seeking itself becomes a need?

Inquiry acquires force within life. The final struggle is between inherited errors and a drive to truth that has also become life-preserving, within the same thinker. The closing experiment asks how far truth can become incorporated into living.

Why does §116 concern more than pressure to conform?

A morality ranks drives and activities according to community needs, then teaches individuals to measure their own worth through their social function. Its standards can operate inwardly even when no one else is present.

Does a value’s social origin settle whether we should retain it?

No. Its history can expose interests, costs, or an unsupported claim to timeless authority. Those discoveries give us reasons to examine it further. We still need to assess the reasons for accepting, changing, or rejecting the value.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one ordinary expectation you inherited: keep your promises, never complain, always be productive, or another rule you recognize. Write about 180–250 words:

  1. State the expectation and identify who taught or reinforced it. Distinguish what you remember from what you are inferring.
  2. Explain which trait it ranks highly and what community need that ranking might serve. Treat this as a hypothesis to investigate.
  3. Describe how it enters your judgment of yourself. What makes following it feel like evidence of being a good or worthwhile person?
  4. Give one reason to retain the expectation and one situation that could require revising it. Explain what its history helps you see and what the history leaves unsettled.
  5. Connect your example to §110’s closing experiment: what would have to change in your conduct for your examination of the rule to become more than a written observation?

A successful response distinguishes origin, function, and justification, and uses both aphorisms. It treats your example as a small inquiry rather than proof of Nietzsche’s entire account.

The next session reads §§108 and 125. We turn back a little within Book III to ask what happens when a major source of inherited authority loses its hold, while the habits and expectations formed around it persist.

Sections 110 and 116 examine knowledge and morality broadly; they do not establish that every value depends on Christianity. They prepare a question for the madman’s listeners: How much of a way of judging can survive the loss of a belief that helped authorize it?

Keep the distinction between origin and justification beside you. Abandoning a belief and deciding what follows for a life are different tasks.
