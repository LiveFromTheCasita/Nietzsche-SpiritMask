What Does Education Train Us to Do?

Before you read

Bring forward the previous lesson's questions about human formation: what type is being cultivated, by whom, and at whose expense? Here Nietzsche offers his own educational preferences for examination.

Distinguish three standards that the chapter repeatedly separates: usefulness to institutions, accomplishment in a specialty, and the development of intellectual judgment. Success by one standard need not establish success by the others.

The main question: Can education cultivate independent judgment without making either the state or an exclusive social hierarchy its final authority?

Three things to notice

  1. Praise before accusation Section 1 acknowledges German capacities before arguing that political power can consume them. Sections 2–3 distinguish the production of scholars from the formation of independent minds.
  2. The end of education Sections 4–5 ask whether culture can flourish when national power and institutional usefulness define its purpose. Notice the additional claim that higher education should belong to a privileged few.
  3. Practices, not merely ideals Sections 6–7 turn toward learned capacities: delaying reaction, examining a case, mastering techniques of thought, and acquiring movement and precision in expression.

A way to read the passages

National success can conceal intellectual loss

‘What the Germans Lack’ §1 begins with a deliberately qualified assessment. Nietzsche credits Germany with accumulated capacity, persistence, dependable obligations, self-respect, and a willingness to obey without experiencing obedience as humiliation. The opening matters because his criticism is not that a nation he dislikes possesses no strengths. It is that real strengths can be expended in ways that impoverish another dimension of life.

He argues that political ambition has absorbed the seriousness once available for philosophy and culture. His joke that Bismarck must answer a question about German thinkers compresses the substitution: the achievement of state power stands where intellectual achievement should stand. Success in one sphere has become a defense against asking what happened in another.

That is a claim about direction, not an established national diagnosis. The chapter offers provocations and examples rather than a systematic comparison of cultural production. Its more transferable question is what our favored measure of success makes difficult to notice. If an institution celebrates status, expansion, or influence, does it still know how to recognize an improvement in judgment?

Scholarship is not identical with intellectual formation

Section 2 portrays alcohol, Christianity, and German music as forces that dull intellectual sensitivity. David Strauss serves as Nietzsche's example of a free spirit whose independence has become complacency. These sweeping causal accusations are not demonstrated by their forceful delivery. Their place in the argument is to challenge a learned person's self-description: acquiring knowledge does not guarantee protection against habits that narrow or tranquilize thought.

Section 3 makes the institutional version clearer. Vast scholarly demands can produce specialists whose concentrated expertise leaves little room for a fuller education. Scientific achievement therefore does not directly answer Nietzsche's criticism. He is asking whether institutions form people capable of intellectual depth, range, passion, and judgment, not whether those institutions can produce impressive specialized results.

The objection is serious: specialization can make rigorous understanding possible, while a taste for breadth can become dilettantism. Nietzsche needs a distinction between necessary concentration and the reduction of a person to their function. His closing appeal to the book's misunderstood cheerfulness also matters. The seriousness he wants can issue in lightness and experiment; a ponderous manner is not reliable evidence of intellectual depth.

Does culture compete with the state?

Section 4 develops an argument from limited resources. Energy directed toward politics, commerce, military activity, and administration is unavailable for other purposes. Nietzsche turns that observation into a strong opposition between state and culture, claiming that major cultural periods coincide with political decline. He interprets Germany's ascent as a political power and France's cultural importance as a displacement of intellectual gravity.

Two claims need separating. First, priorities have costs: an institution can exhaust resources on objectives that leave cultivation neglected. Second, state and culture are necessarily antagonists. The first does not prove the second. A state could finance education or protect spaces of inquiry, and those activities might support forms of culture that criticize the state itself.

That counterexample would not settle every concern about dependence. Funding can come with expectations that narrow what counts as worthwhile. The deeper issue is whether culture retains authority to define its ends when support is justified by national prestige or usefulness. Nietzsche's provocative historical generalization should become a question to investigate, rather than a law accepted because the examples have been arranged persuasively.

An educator forms more than an employee

Section 5 asks both who should educate and what education is for. Nietzsche distinguishes an educator whose conduct embodies a developed culture from a functionary who delivers information. He names Jacob Burckhardt as an admired exception. His complaint is that schools train people quickly for exploitable service while forgetting that cultivation itself, rather than the Empire, should supply the end.

The criticism of haste has an intelligible educational point. A timetable for obtaining a profession cannot establish when a person's judgment has matured. Nietzsche's higher type needs time, and becoming cultivated is not finished at the moment one becomes employable. This argument challenges the idea that every period of learning must be justified immediately by a marketable output.

But §5 also explicitly reserves higher education for an exceptional, privileged minority and attacks its democratization. That is more than a request for individualized teaching or high standards. A reader who values broad access must confront the exclusion rather than quietly translate it into modern educational advice. The defensible demand for time and excellent educators does not by itself prove that those conditions should be denied to most people.

Learning to see includes learning to wait

Section 6 supplies the most concrete positive practice in the chapter. Learning to see means acquiring patience, delaying a judgment, and considering a case from more than one side. The educated response has room between stimulus and action. This returns to ‘Morality as Anti-Nature’ §2: strength includes the capacity to refrain, rather than the compulsion to discharge every impulse.

Nietzsche's suspicion of indiscriminate openness complicates the practice. He does not praise a mind that makes itself available to every demand for attention. Selection and resistance help preserve the conditions under which careful attention is possible. Our example: before endorsing a forceful argument, a reader distinguishes its evidence, inference, and evaluative standard, then asks what would count against it. A delayed verdict becomes a way to improve responsiveness.

That is different from making suspicion permanent. If nothing can ever earn a hearing or alter a judgment, resistance becomes insulation. Nietzsche's practice should be tested by whether delay improves discrimination and makes revision possible. A posture of superiority that refuses evidence would reproduce the very unexamined reaction the practice is meant to interrupt.

Thinking and writing must acquire technique

Section 7 rejects the assumption that thought develops merely through possessing ideas. It has techniques, exercises, and disciplines that must be learned. Nietzsche compares its mastery to dancing: movement can become precise, responsive, and apparently light because capacities have been formed. The image does not mean replacing argument with whatever feels expressive. A practiced thinker should perceive distinctions that an awkward grasp misses.

The chapter's attack on Kant is a polemical judgment within that contrast, not a refutation of Kant's philosophy. The educational argument stands or falls on whether habits of thinking can be trained and what counts as improved performance. Nietzsche includes speaking and writing among the capacities needing formation, and ends by making the pen participate in the dance. Style becomes part of how thought handles its objects.

Our practical extension is to revise a sentence until its distinctions are explicit without becoming heavy: what happened, what you infer, and why you judge it valuable. Then ask a fair opponent to reconstruct the claim. Clarity of movement should make thought more exact and answerable, not merely more impressive. This extension applies Nietzsche's practice while leaving his exclusionary distribution of educational privilege open to challenge.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can we adopt Nietzsche's practices of education while rejecting his elitism?

We can distinguish arguments without pretending their author did so. Sections 6–7 describe capacities whose value can be considered independently: patience, controlled attention, technical practice, and expressive precision. Section 5 adds the claim that higher cultivation belongs to a privileged few. Rejecting that restriction requires an argument, especially if time and excellent teaching are scarce.

The strongest challenge is that wider provision may dilute the conditions of demanding education. A reply must show how access and depth could support one another rather than merely announcing that both are desirable. Conversely, scarcity does not establish that inherited advantage reliably identifies those capable of cultivation. The central question is whether educational arrangements discover and develop capacities, or merely preserve the advantages by which their favored students were selected.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why does the initial praise in §1 matter?

Nietzsche grants real capacities before questioning how they are spent. His claim is that political power can divert strengths from intellectual cultivation, not that Germany has no accomplishments.

Why does scientific success not directly answer §3?

He distinguishes specialized achievement from forming a whole person capable of depth, range, and judgment. The objection concerns what scholarship demands of and leaves undeveloped in its practitioners.

What inference in §4 requires additional support?

Limited resources make priorities consequential, but do not prove that culture and state must always be antagonists. That stronger claim needs evidence beyond the existence of competing demands.

What two claims should be separated in §5?

Cultivation requires time and capable educators; higher education should be a privilege of an exceptional minority. The first claim does not automatically establish the second.

What connects learning to see in §6 with dancing in §7?

Both require trained capacities rather than an ungoverned reaction. Patient discrimination and practiced intellectual movement make a response more selective, precise, and capable of handling distinctions.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 300–400 words proposing one change to an educational practice, using Nietzsche's argument while stating where you depart from it.

  1. Identify the practice and its current purpose. Use ‘What the Germans Lack’ §§3–5 to distinguish institutional usefulness, specialist achievement, and cultivation.
  2. Describe your change concretely through §§6–7: what would learners do differently to improve attention, thinking, speaking, or writing? Name an observable sign of improvement.
  3. State who would receive the opportunity and how it would be supported. Present the strongest objection about access, scarcity, or rigor, and explain whether your proposal accepts or rejects the restriction in §5.

End by identifying one sign that your proposed practice has become a new ritual of conformity rather than an education in judgment.
