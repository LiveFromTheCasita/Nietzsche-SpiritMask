Dream, Intoxication, and Art

Essential Reading Path · Reading 1 of 8

Read: §1 of The Birth of Tragedy

Before you read

Think of two experiences.

In the first, you are absorbed in a film. You care about its characters and feel the danger they face. Yet some awareness remains that you are watching a created world. You can undergo the experience because you are also held apart from it.

In the second, you are singing with a crowd. For a moment, you stop monitoring your own voice or wondering how you appear. Your attention belongs to the shared movement of the song.

Keep these examples nearby as starting points. They suggest a contrast we will test against Nietzsche’s text: an experience given a distinct form, and an experience in which the boundaries of the self become less secure.

Nietzsche names two artistic drives after the Greek gods Apollo and Dionysus. He begins with dreams and intoxication because he wants us to recognize experiences before we settle for definitions.

The main question: How might art draw its power both from giving experience a clear shape and from loosening our sense of being a separate self?

Read §1 once before continuing. If an unfamiliar name interrupts you, mark it and keep going. On this first pass, follow the images.

Three things to notice

  1. The dreamer’s double awareness. How can someone feel a dream intensely while still sensing that it is an appearance? Notice that the dream can contain frightening scenes.
  2. The person in the boat. What gives this person confidence in a dangerous sea? What might the boat suggest about the boundaries that make someone an individual?
  3. The change from artist to artwork. At the end, who seems to be doing the creating? What has happened to the person who might ordinarily stand apart and make something?

A way to read the passage

Apollo: the power of a distinct image

Nietzsche’s dreamer lives through a vivid world of figures and events while retaining a glimmer of awareness that it is a dream. This helps explain the Apollonian: the power through which experience acquires an image, an outline, a form we can contemplate. Apollo also stands for measure and limits. §1, discussion of dreams and Apollo

In the opening paragraph, Nietzsche associates the arts of shaping images with Apollo and music with Dionysus. The older translation’s phrase plastic art means art that gives form, such as sculpture. Keep this distinction in view when you encounter his examples.

Return to the film example. A frightening event becomes a scene with a particular shape: a face, a gesture, a sequence of actions. Ask yourself what difference that shaping makes. Does it merely reduce the fear, or can it also let you attend to something you would otherwise avoid?

This is a useful question to bring to the passage. An image can give us access to an experience as well as provide some distance from it.

The boat: a boundary we trust

Nietzsche borrows Schopenhauer’s image of a person trusting a fragile boat amid a storm. He connects it with the principium individuationis, the principle through which things appear as distinct individuals. Apollo expresses confidence in this separateness. §1, the boat image

For this reading, hold onto the question the image makes possible: What allows me to experience myself as a definite “someone” within a much larger world?

Try drawing the boat and sea in two simple shapes. Label the boat “a separate individual.” Then consider what the sea might represent. Your drawing need not solve Nietzsche’s metaphysics; its purpose is to make the relationship visible enough to think about.

Dionysus: when separateness gives way

With the Dionysian, the boundaries of individuality break down in a mixture of terror and ecstasy. Intoxication supplies an analogy; Nietzsche also invokes spring, singing, and dancing. His description expands into reconciliation among people and with nature. By the end, nature appears as an artist shaping human beings themselves. §1, the Dionysian experience

His phrase Primordial Unity names the underlying oneness he takes separate beings to express. This brings a claim about the nature of reality into his account of the experience.

Now return to the crowd singing. What changes when you stop listening to yourself as an individual performer? Does a shared rhythm alter your sense of who is acting?

This example offers a foothold. You should still ask how far it takes you. Briefly forgetting yourself in a song may help you imagine the passage, but does it establish that ordinary individuality is only an appearance? Keep that question open.

Why the opening points toward tragedy

The first paragraph describes the drives as rivals whose conflict stimulates artistic creation, with periodic moments of reconciliation. It announces tragedy as their union. The rest of the section begins to make that difficult claim imaginable. §1, opening paragraph

Our task at this stage is to understand the problem that the union poses. If one drive gives experience distinct boundaries and the other dissolves them, how could they work together in a single artwork?

That is a question to carry forward. Resist the urge to answer it with the word “balance.” What would the actual cooperation look like? Which features of an artwork would need explaining?

A difficulty to keep in view

An experience of unity and a claim about what reality ultimately is are different kinds of claim.

As you reread, use two marks in the margin:

  • E beside a description of an experience you can try to imagine.
  • R beside a claim about reality that would need an argument.

Where would you put both marks? Where does a vivid image make a larger claim feel persuasive?

This exercise lets you appreciate the writing while testing the work it asks its images to do. You can understand the proposed experience and still question the conclusion drawn from it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before reading each suggested response.

Why is “reason versus emotion” an inadequate explanation of the two drives?

Because the contrast we have followed concerns form and individuality. The dreamer can feel fear or delight; the presence of emotion alone does not tell us which drive is at work. Explain what happens to the boundaries of the experience and the self.

What does the dreamer retain that the Dionysian participant loses?

The dreamer retains some distance from the images, some awareness of appearance. In the Dionysian description, the participant’s sense of standing apart gives way. Support your answer with one detail from each part of §1.

Has this section shown how Greek tragedy combines the two drives?

It has announced the claim and introduced the experiences through which we can begin to understand it. We still need an account of how a theatrical work brings them together.

What should I write in my notes?

Write four to six sentences answering:

What is the difference between being absorbed in an image and losing your sense of being separate from an experience?

Use one image from Nietzsche for each side of your explanation. Then finish this sentence:

The part of Nietzsche’s argument I understand least—or most want him to defend—is…

A successful response will explain what the images help you see and identify a question they leave unanswered.
