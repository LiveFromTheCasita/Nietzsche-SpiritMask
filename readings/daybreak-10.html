How Far Can We Fly?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 10 of 10

Read: Book V, §547, then §550, then §575. Read each in full, stopping before §548 and §551 in the first two cases. Section 575 is the last aphorism; stop before the free edition’s footnotes. The final exercise also returns to §103 and one earlier selection.
Allow: About 65–85 minutes, including the passages, this guide, a return to earlier readings, and the final written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §547 · §550 · §575 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Look back at the rule about being a good person that you chose in Reading 1. You were asked to distinguish what moved you to follow it from what might justify it. You were also asked what would happen if your judgment changed while your feelings remained.

You have now met several possible explanations of those feelings: inherited custom, religious interpretation, competing drives, concern for others, and the demands of social life. You have also encountered reasons to question the confidence of the person offering these explanations. Understanding a revealing possibility has repeatedly fallen short of establishing a universal account.

What would count as finishing this inquiry? Perhaps you hope to replace the old rule with a better one. Perhaps you would like an explanation of morality that settles every remaining question. Perhaps you have become suspicious of the wish to reach that kind of destination.

The last reading turns these expectations into a problem. Nietzsche asks how inquiry might continue beyond the ambitions and limits of individual thinkers. He also asks what makes it enjoyable, and whether that enjoyment could compromise the honesty that gives it value.

The main question: How can we commit ourselves to inquiry without pretending that its destination, its value, or our own arrival is guaranteed?

Read §§547, 550, and 575 before continuing. In the final aphorism, notice the change between imagining other birds flying farther and asking where the flight is taking them.

Three things to notice

  1. The scale of a lifetime. In §547, why does the wish to finish knowledge within one human life encourage the ambition to explain everything at once?
  2. A pleasure that needs scrutiny. In §550, how can delight in knowing change the appearance of the world? What danger appears in the final sentence?
  3. Two different uncertainties. In §575, distinguish the limits of a particular thinker from the question of where inquiry itself is heading.

A way to read the passage

Why must the answer fit inside my life?

§547 begins with an apparently modest fact: a human life has a limited duration. Nietzsche argues that earlier thinkers treated this limit as a deadline for mastering knowledge. Methods promising a quick route to completion acquired a special attraction. Small questions and particular experiments seemed insufficiently important.

The ambition depends on a further assumption. If the world has been arranged for human needs, perhaps the task of understanding it has also been arranged to fit human capacities and lifespans. Nietzsche connects the demand for a complete explanation with this picture of our place in things. The fact that we want a conclusion does not establish that reality owes us one.

Think of someone researching a complicated family dispute. They may make real progress by establishing what happened during one conversation. But if they demand a single explanation of every relationship, the more modest finding begins to look disappointing. A theory that explains everything through jealousy or control can feel more satisfying precisely because it promises an end to the work. This is our illustration of the temptation, not evidence for Nietzsche's history of philosophy.

The aphorism presents that history boldly. Its portraits of thinkers and its claim about the state of inquiry in Nietzsche's own time require assessment. We can take its diagnostic question seriously without accepting that all earlier philosophy pursued one ambition or that such ambitions had disappeared by 1881.

A thinker who does not own the conclusion

The desire to solve everything also offers a reward to the solver. The person who supplies the final answer becomes the authority through whom everyone else's thinking must pass. Nietzsche compares this ambition to intellectual tyranny and explicitly includes Schopenhauer among those who imagined themselves occupying the privileged position.

That connection explains the turn toward motive at the end of §547. In Kennedy's translation, the future thinker's doorway bears the question “What do I matter?” The proposed change concerns the relation between the inquirer and the inquiry: knowledge can have a future that exceeds this person's success, reputation, and lifetime.

Do not make this into a command to regard every individual need as worthless. The paragraph gives us a criticism of possessive intellectual ambition. Elsewhere in the course, Nietzsche has questioned demands that people sacrifice their individuality to a collective purpose. A reader should ask how the commitment imagined here differs from the self-denial criticized in §132, rather than assume the difference has already been secured.

The earlier account of drives makes that question harder. In Reading 5, the wish to control an impulse had motives of its own. Similarly, a thinker can profess indifference to personal glory while pursuing another satisfaction: belonging to a grand undertaking, being unusually selfless, or seeing rivals corrected. Those are possibilities to investigate, not diagnoses we are entitled to impose.

Nietzsche's criticism also does not show that every broad theory is illegitimate. Its target is the ambition to bring all questions under one final authority. A theory that makes its evidence and limits available for examination differs from an explanation that treats every objection as confirmation of its own supremacy.

Learning to enjoy a small discovery

If we give up the fantasy of a final solution, what remains attractive about inquiry? §550 turns to the pleasure of making even a small, secure advance in understanding. Nietzsche challenges readers who reserve their strongest admiration for imaginative departures from reality and consequently find inquiry cold or disappointing.

His proposal is more interesting than the reassurance that facts sometimes happen to be pleasant. Something previously regarded as ugly can become an occasion of intellectual delight through being understood. The discovery does not require that its object was secretly comforting all along.

Consider learning how an error arose in a piece of work you care about. The error can remain frustrating. Yet understanding the overlooked assumption may have a satisfaction distinct from liking the result. This is an ordinary illustration of how the value of understanding and the appeal of what is understood can come apart.

Nietzsche then makes a stronger claim: repeated experiences of discovery may change the world as it appears to the knower. Things come to be encountered through the happiness associated with understanding them. Knowledge does not simply stand beside an unchanging experience of beauty; it can transform that experience.

This brings us back to §103's distinction between learning and feeling anew. We can read §550 as one possible instance of such a change. A person may come to take pleasure in what previously disappointed them. But this is a connection made by the guide, and the passage offers no guarantee that every reader will undergo the transformation.

Can the joy of knowing make us dishonest?

The end of §550 prevents its celebration from becoming uncomplicated. Nietzsche recalls philosophers, including Plato and Aristotle, who placed the highest happiness in intellectual activity. He takes the intensity of their delight seriously. Then he asks how great the danger was that their honesty might give way and they might become excessive celebrants of things.

A knower may begin by discovering something and end by praising reality because the discovery was pleasurable. The feeling that makes inquiry rewarding can put pressure on the account of what has been found. An unpleasant feature may be made to look justified, necessary in a comforting sense, or better than the evidence allows.

Keep that danger concrete. Understanding the arrangements that sustain exhausting work can be intellectually satisfying. It does not follow that the arrangements are good for the people doing it. In Reading 7, the question of whose independence matters could not be settled by the elegance of an explanation. That question remains even when the explanation makes its author happy.

The warning is not that pleasure automatically corrupts thought. Nietzsche has just described its importance. The problem is how to enjoy inquiry while allowing its findings to disappoint, unsettle, or contradict the person who enjoys it. His own attractive picture of intellectual life is subject to the same question.

A resting place is not the edge of the possible

§575 begins with birds that have flown until they can go no farther and must settle on an inadequate perch. The image separates exhaustion from completion. A bird's inability to continue does not show that there is no open space ahead.

Nietzsche applies the image to teachers and predecessors, then includes himself and the reader. Their stopping places may have been awkward or undignified. Our own limits will also arrive. Recognizing those limits should change how we judge the positions thinkers finally occupy: an ending can reveal the capacities of the traveler without marking the boundary of what can be thought.

This need not excuse an unsupported conclusion. Fatigue might help explain why someone settled for it, but explanation does not establish its truth. Nor should the image make us dismiss a finding simply because the thinker who reached it was finite. The point is to avoid confusing the limits of the person with the limits of inquiry.

Other birds may continue, and Nietzsche imagines our hopes following them beyond our own failures. Read beside §547, this makes intellectual life extend across people and generations. The future does not need to be our possession to matter to us.

The direction remains a question

The aphorism could end with that prospect. Instead, it asks where the flight is going. Why this direction? What passion sends the thinkers out over an expanse where no destination is visible? The image shifts from the relief that someone can go farther to uncertainty about what farther would mean.

Nietzsche invokes the possibility of sailing west in search of India and being wrecked on the infinite. The allusion recalls the desire for decisive discovery examined in §547, but now it belongs to a question about the inquirers themselves. Escaping one final system does not make their direction or deepest commitment self-justifying.

The ending therefore gives neither a demonstration that inquiry must fail nor a promise that future thinkers will arrive. It leaves an unfinished alternative before us. Confidence that our exhaustion is not the limit of everything can coexist with uncertainty about what lies beyond us.

Return to Reading 9. Experimental living made action possible while future ideals remained unsettled; it did not make uncertainty disappear. The final image puts that openness under pressure. How do we decide what to pursue when waiting for a guaranteed destination would prevent us from beginning?

A difficulty to keep in view

Why should inquiry deserve the devotion that Nietzsche asks us to reconsider elsewhere?

Daybreak has repeatedly challenged the authority of a feeling, an inherited practice, or an admired person. Yet the desire to know has acquired considerable authority in these last passages. It promises happiness, asks us to relinquish final ownership of the result, and carries hopes beyond the individual life.

There are reasons one might offer for pursuing it: correcting a harmful mistake, understanding a difficulty, finding an alternative that was previously invisible. But particular successes do not establish that inquiry should always outrank every other commitment. Its demands on attention, relationships, and other people still need consideration.

Section 550 places the knower's enjoyment under examination, and §575 questions the direction of the journey. These moves help prevent the value of inquiry from becoming wholly immune to the book's criticism. They leave us with work to do rather than a completed justification.

You can therefore finish the course with a commitment and a condition: name an inquiry worth pursuing, then identify what would make you change its direction, limit its demands, or revise the judgment that sustains it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why does §547 connect a human lifetime with the wish for a final explanation?

Nietzsche argues that the demand to master knowledge within one life favored methods promising a rapid and comprehensive solution. He also identifies the assumption that knowledge should be arranged for human needs and the personal ambition of becoming its final authority. His historical account remains something to assess.

Does the future thinker's modest place in inquiry mean individuals have no value?

The immediate target is the ambition to possess the final answer and govern others' thinking through it. Extending this into a demand for unlimited personal sacrifice would require a further argument. The criticism of self-denial elsewhere in the course gives us a reason to ask where the proposed modesty should stop.

How can knowledge of something ugly be beautiful in §550?

The activity of understanding can be pleasurable even when its object is unwelcome. Nietzsche also suggests that repeated delight in discovery changes how reality is experienced. Neither claim establishes that everything understood is therefore good or that every painful condition deserves approval.

Why does §550 end by questioning the honesty of delighted knowers?

The satisfaction of understanding can encourage excessive praise of what is understood. An attachment to the joy of knowledge may influence judgment about reality. The passage makes that a danger within intellectual pleasure, without claiming that pleasure always makes inquiry dishonest.

Does §575 guarantee that later thinkers will reach the destination we miss?

No. It distinguishes a particular thinker's exhaustion from the scope for further inquiry and imagines others going farther. Its closing questions reopen the direction and destination of that movement, including the possibility of shipwreck. Further flight and guaranteed arrival are different claims.

What should I write in my notes?

For the final exercise, write 300–400 words. Reopen §103 and your notes from Reading 1. Then revisit one earlier passage that challenged your judgment: §119 on self-knowledge, §133 on helping, or §206 on work and whose freedom counts.

  1. State your original rule about being a good person and explain what, if anything, you would now retain, revise, or reject. Distinguish an explanation of its origins or motives from a reason for your present judgment.
  2. Use the earlier passage you chose to complicate that judgment. Include a limit in Nietzsche's explanation or a person whose experience it does not adequately account for.
  3. Use §547, §550, or §575 to examine your own attraction to the conclusion. Are you seeking a final answer, enjoying a revealing diagnosis, or treating your present limit as the end of inquiry?
  4. Name one inquiry you will continue and a concrete action it requires. Say who bears its costs and what evidence, objection, or competing commitment would make you change course. Identify one feeling that has yet to catch up with your judgment.

A strong response can defend part of your original position. It should show what the course has made more precise, what remains difficult, and how you will hold your next conclusion open to examination.

Its account of moral honesty turning against morality gives a further question to put to the commitment to inquiry encountered here.
