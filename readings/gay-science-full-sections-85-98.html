Artists, Narcotics, and the Freedom of Brutus

Optional full-text route · Station 10 of 35 (proposed)

Review draft. This lesson belongs to a proposed full-text route that has not been published. Only some stations are drafted so far. For the complete published introduction, use the ten-session guided reading path.

Read: Book II, §§85–98, in full, from the original 1882 edition. Begin at §85 and stop before §99.
Allow: About 65–85 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. This is an editorial estimate, not a measured reading time. The fourteen sections total about 3,200 words in the free edition. If you need two sittings, stop after §92.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): begin at §85 · §87 · §98.

Quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation, including his section titles. Kaufmann’s wording differs, and none of it is quoted here. Where a word drives the interpretation, this page notes the German, from the Projekt Gutenberg-DE text.

Before you read

Station 9 ended with a warning that rhythm makes thoughts seem truer. These fourteen sections turn from art’s origins to its makers and its audiences: who artists really serve, what the theatre gives tired people, what a musician fails to see in himself, how writers lie about their lives, and why good prose needs poetry as an enemy. The last section honours Shakespeare’s Brutus, the man who killed Caesar.

The main question: How do artists’ ambitions shape what they say they see—and what kind of freedom does the station finally honour?

Use the marks from station 2. Many sections here make claims about named people’s motives; those are P and H.

What to notice

  1. The real valuers. §85 says artists are not the ones who decide what is valuable. Who does?
  2. A musician not named. §87 describes a composer who misunderstands his own gift. Notice what the section praises and what it regrets.
  3. Caesar again. Station 5’s §23 praised the age of the Caesar. §98 honours the man who killed one. Compare.

The real valuers

§85 says artists “glorify continually—they do nothing else.” They glorify what already has a reputation for making people feel good, great, or wise. They are not the valuers of happiness; they “press close to these valuers” and are only “faster and louder.” The section ends: “And who then are these?—They are the rich and the leisurely.” Mark it P and V. It is a sociological claim about who sets values, stated as a punchline. §85, the rich and the leisurely

§86, “The Theatre,” describes the audience with open contempt. Art that intoxicates is for “those men with commonplace souls,” who come home in the evening “like tired mules to whom life has rather too often applied the whip.” The speaker, who has had strong feelings of his own that day, wants none of it. Theatre and music are “the hashish-smoking and betel-chewing of Europeans,” and the history of narcotics “is almost the history of ‘culture.’” The German word for culture here is Bildung, education and self-cultivation, so the target is the educated public, not culture in general. §86, the theatre

Mark §86 V. Its ranking of “commonplace souls” is plain. Keep it beside station 3’s §7, which wanted a history of the passions, and station 4’s §14 on the “sequel” of love: the book often ranks the people it describes.

§89 puts the same complaint historically. Works of art were once shown “on the great festive path of humanity,” as monuments of high and happy moments. Now they lure “the exhausted and sickly” aside for a moment and furnish them “with a little ecstasy and insanity.” The festival is “that higher art” the section fears we have lost. §88 adds a warning about artists who take up a thinker’s cast-off opinions and feel “the profoundest earnestness for the truth”: the pathos itself may betray how little they have thought. §89, the art of the festival

A master of miniature

§87 describes an unnamed musician who “has the genius for discovering the tones peculiar to suffering, oppressed, tortured souls.” He knows the colours of late autumn and the “secret and weird midnights of the soul”; he is “the Orpheus of all secret misery.” His real masterpieces are very short, “often only one bar in length”: “he is the master of miniature.” But he loves “large walls and daring frescoes,” and does not know his own gift: “He is too conceited to know it.” §87, the master of miniature

The German title is Von der Eitelkeit der Künstler. Common renders Eitelkeit as “conceit”; “vanity” is the more usual English. The section names no one. In 1888 Nietzsche revised it as the chapter Wo ich bewundere (Where I Admire) in Nietzsche Contra Wagner, where the musician becomes Wagner and the ending changes. That identification is Nietzsche’s own, but it is later. Read the 1882 section first as a portrait of an artist who misjudges his gift.

Writers and their lives

§91 warns against believing autobiographies. Alfieri “told a great many falsehoods” about his life, forcing it into a rigid sublimity. The speaker would “give no credit” to a life of Plato written by himself, nor to Rousseau’s, nor to Dante’s Vita nuova. §91, autobiographies

§92 says the masters of prose have almost always been poets, because good prose is “an uninterrupted, polite warfare with poetry.” It ends: “Warfare is the father of all good things, it is also the father of good prose!” The phrase recalls Heraclitus, as the prelude’s poem 41, “Heraclitism,” did at station 1. Among the century’s masters of prose Nietzsche names Leopardi, Mérimée, Emerson, and Landor. §93 is a dialogue: A writes only because he has “no other means of getting rid of my thoughts.” Asked why he wants to be rid of them, he answers: “Do I really wish! I must.”

§94 is about thoughts whose standing grew after their author’s death. Fontenelle’s daring remarks on morals were taken as paradoxes in his own age; now “these thoughts become truths! Science proves them!” The section ends on an open question: “Rightly? Wrongly?” Leave that question open in your notes. §97 then sorts the loquacity of authors by its source: anger in Luther and Schopenhauer, formulas in Kant, delight in variation in Montaigne, fine words in Goethe’s prose, noise and confused feeling in Carlyle. §94, growth after death

Chamfort’s revenge

§95 explains why Chamfort, a keen judge of the multitude, sided with it rather than standing apart. Nietzsche’s answer is a motive: “the hatred against all noblesse of blood,” perhaps inherited from his mother, an instinct of revenge “from his boyhood.” Yet “the paternal blood in his veins” (German das väterliche Blut) had drawn him for years to consider himself equal to the nobility. At last he put on “the raiment of the populace as his special kind of hair-shirt”; “His bad conscience was the neglect of revenge.” §95, Chamfort

Mark the explanation P and H. It explains a political choice, and even the Revolution’s “sharpest sting,” by a motive of revenge and by inheritance through the blood. The section offers no evidence beyond the story it tells. Mark ? where it hedges: the mother’s hatred and the father’s blood each come with a “perhaps.” The framing sentence, that the speaker can explain Chamfort’s choice no other way, is not hedged. Compare station 29’s §359, which explains moral language by revenge, and keep origin and value apart as §345 asks. The section also praises Chamfort as “a thinker who found laughter necessary as the remedy of life.” §96, on two orators, makes a related point about temperament: one speaker thinks best when moved, the other only when he resists his own feeling.

Independence of soul

§98 is the station’s close. “The best thing I could say in honour of Shakespeare, the man, is that he believed in Brutus.” Shakespeare consecrated his best tragedy to Brutus, and it “is at present still called by a wrong name.” What it honours is “Independence of soul!” (German Unabhängigkeit der Seele). For that, “one must be able to sacrifice to it even one’s dearest friend, though he be also the grandest of men.” “The elevation in which he places Cæsar is the most exquisite honour he could confer upon Brutus.” §98, independence of soul

Then the section asks whether “political freedom” was “merely a symbol for something inexpressible”—some dark event in Shakespeare’s own soul. It notices that the play twice brings in a poet whom Brutus treats with contempt, “like the cry of self-contempt,” and suggests reading that contempt back into the poet who wrote it. Mark the guesses about Shakespeare’s own soul P, and add ? where the section says “perhaps,” as it does three times. The earlier claim that “it is thus that Shakespeare must have felt!” is not hedged. §98, the poet’s self-contempt

A difficulty

Caesar’s age or Brutus’s freedom?

At station 5, §23 described the age of the Caesar as the ripening time of the individual, and said the Caesar “understands the rights of the Individual even in his excesses.” §98 honours the man who killed the greatest of men for the sake of “Independence of soul,” and hints that political freedom may be only a symbol for it.

Several readings are possible.

  • The two sections describe different things: §23 a social condition, §98 an inner virtue.
  • They conflict, and the book leaves both standing.
  • §98 revises §23: the individual’s true freedom is not the Caesar’s licence but the independence that would sacrifice even a Caesar.

Decide which reading the texts best support, naming a sentence from each. Then ask whose freedom §98 praises. It is “the freedom of great souls”: not, on its face, a freedom for everyone.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

According to §85, who really values the things artists glorify?

“The rich and the leisurely.” Artists are only faster and louder than the real valuers, and often seem to be the first to praise what these valuers already esteem.

What does §87 say the musician fails to know about himself?

That his real mastery is in miniature: tiny works, “often only one bar in length,” that capture hidden suffering. He prefers large frescoes because he is “too conceited to know it.” The section names no one; Nietzsche later reused it about Wagner in Nietzsche Contra Wagner.

How does §95 explain Chamfort’s siding with the multitude?

By a motive of revenge against “all noblesse of blood,” perhaps inherited from his mother, delayed for years by “the paternal blood in his veins,” and finally indulged. It is a psychological and hereditary story, offered without evidence.

What does §98 say Shakespeare honoured in Brutus?

“Independence of soul,” for which one must be able to sacrifice even one’s dearest friend and the greatest of men. The section asks whether political freedom was only a symbol for something in Shakespeare himself.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Does §98’s praise of Brutus revise §23’s praise of the Caesar’s age?

  • Quote no more than one phrase from each section.
  • Say what kind of freedom each section values, and for whom.
  • Use one other section from this station (§85, §86, or §95) to test whose judgment of value the book trusts.

A successful response keeps the two sections’ contexts distinct, states the ranking in §§85–86 plainly, and treats the reading of Shakespeare’s motives as the hedged guess it is.
