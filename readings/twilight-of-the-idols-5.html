Can a Passion Be Transformed?

Before you read

Recall the previous lesson's removal of the opposition between a true world and an apparent world. The present chapter asks what happens to moral judgment when it can no longer appeal to a tribunal beyond life.

Keep three possibilities distinct: acting on an impulse immediately, suppressing it, and transforming its expression. Nietzsche's argument depends on their differences.

The main question: Can we cultivate our passions without turning one preferred kind of person into a law for everyone?

Three things to notice

  1. A changed problem In §§1–2 the question moves from whether passions can be dangerous to whether eliminating them is the only possible response. Notice what Nietzsche considers a sign of strength.
  2. Useful opposition In §3 love and transformed hostility are parallel examples. Follow the argument from public enemies to conflicting impulses within one person, then examine the changing meanings of inner peace.
  3. The evaluator belongs to life In §§4–6 Nietzsche continues making strong evaluations while denying an external position from which life as a whole could be condemned. Do these two commitments fit together?

A way to read the passages

Why eliminating the danger may eliminate a capacity

In ‘Morality as Anti-Nature’ §1, Nietzsche concedes that passions can be destructive in their undeveloped forms. His disagreement concerns the treatment. If the dangerous capacity can develop, removing it prevents cultivation. The chapter opens with a problem of education rather than a simple defense of instinct. Refusing every discipline does not answer it.

His image of a dentist who removes teeth to prevent future toothache makes the cost visible. A successful removal of suffering might also remove something worth preserving. Applied to desire, the alternative is what Nietzsche calls spiritualization: a passion acquires complexity, form, intelligence, and a different relation to its object. This does not mean escaping the body into a higher realm. The desire is transformed while remaining a source of life.

Nietzsche presents the Church as committed to extirpation rather than transformation. That sweeping institutional portrait is part of his polemic; the chapter does not establish it through a history of Christian practices. The philosophical distinction can nevertheless be assessed independently: does a proposed discipline develop a capacity, or secure obedience by disabling it? That question is more exact than deciding whether all restraint is good or all restraint is bad.

The strength to delay a response

Section 2 shifts attention from a rule’s stated nobility to the person who requires it. Nietzsche treats demands for total eradication as evidence of an inability to govern a response. His sharpest target is someone unable to achieve an ascetic ideal who compensates with increasingly vehement denunciations of desire.

The distinction between self-command and immediate reaction will return in ‘What the Germans Lack’ §6. Here it prevents a common mistake: Nietzsche's alternative to anti-natural morality is not compulsory spontaneity. Being unable to refrain is itself a kind of dependence. Cultivation includes the capacity to interrupt an impulse, change its direction, and decide what form its expression will take.

However, the inference from radical avoidance to weakness is not established simply by naming it. A temporary or lasting refusal can be deliberate, effective, and suited to a particular danger. Our example: a person might stop entering a destructive rivalry while continuing to welcome demanding criticism elsewhere. That case asks Nietzsche to distinguish the practical protection of a capacity from hatred of the capacity itself. The distinction is stronger than his sweeping diagnosis of those who choose abstention.

Love and enmity acquire a form

Section 3 names love as a transformation of sensuality, then gives the less comfortable example of hostility. An enemy can require distinctions, restraint, invention, and self-definition. Nietzsche even says his own opponents can benefit him by continuing to exist. Removing opposition altogether might deprive a position of the pressure through which it becomes articulate. This is why his spiritualization of enmity differs from both indiscriminate aggression and the demand to abolish all conflict.

The political examples extend the argument: parties can benefit from preserving an opposition, and a new state can define itself through an adversary. But an institution might also manufacture enemies to avoid confronting its failures. The fact that opposition strengthens an identity does not establish that the identity deserves strengthening.

Nietzsche then turns inward. Productivity can emerge from competing tendencies rather than their complete reconciliation. Our example: a writer's urge to risk an unfamiliar form may struggle with a demand for clarity. Neither need be annihilated; their friction might improve the work. The claim is not that every inner conflict is productive. It is that the absence of conflict cannot serve as the sole measure of a well-formed life.

A peaceful feeling does not interpret itself

The second half of §3 complicates its own apparent attack on peace. Nietzsche lists different conditions that might receive the same flattering name: exhaustion, satiety, relief after uncertainty, recovery, or mastery within a creative task. Some are signs of diminished activity; others can accompany ripeness and achievement. The point is not that calm is always suspect. It is that a shared description does not establish a shared cause or value.

Experience might be described as feeling untroubled; the inference might be that conflict has been resolved; the evaluation might be that resolution is the highest good. Recall lesson 1: those are three claims, not one. Nietzsche’s suggestion that this book might express a kind of peace prevents us from treating his hostility to peace as an absolute prohibition.

Section 4 then explicitly allows a life-serving morality to contain commands and prohibitions. The question is what they serve. Rules may remove obstacles to flourishing, or they may condemn the desires through which a life acts at all. Nietzsche's vocabulary of nature therefore cannot mean that whatever happens impulsively should be permitted. He distinguishes forms of organization within life from a moral campaign against its sources.

Can a living being pass judgment on life?

Section 5 returns to the problem encountered in ‘The Problem of Socrates’: an assessment of life's overall worth is made by someone already situated within it. Nietzsche argues that a competent external assessment would require both standing outside life and possessing the experience of those who have lived. We have no such position. A condemnation of life consequently becomes evidence about a particular form of life rather than the verdict of a disinterested judge.

This does not make the condemnation unreal. Someone really does evaluate, and the evaluation may profoundly organize conduct. Nietzsche asks which conditions express themselves through it. He interprets hostility to life as the valuation of an exhausted or declining life. Even the apparent revolt against life thus remains something life does through a particular evaluator.

But a difficulty remains: if every valuation belongs to life, on what grounds does Nietzsche rank ascending life above declining life? His argument removes a claim to transcendent authority; it does not automatically prove his own hierarchy. One possible answer is that he evaluates capacities from an acknowledged perspective within life. The harder question is whether he sometimes writes as though his perspective has become the universal tribunal he has just disqualified. Keep that question open.

A prescription has a history and a cost

In §6 the target becomes the moralist who specifies what every person should be. Nietzsche contrasts that demand with the variety of actual human types. The moralist's universal human ideal may turn out to be an enlarged self-portrait. More deeply, a person is not an isolated creation available for replacement: their disposition belongs to an interconnected past and future. An unconditional condemnation abstracts the individual from the conditions that made that individual possible.

This need not mean that learning, reform, or resistance is impossible. Those activities also occur within life and change its conditions. Nietzsche specifically distinguishes condemnation in itself from evaluations made for a living purpose. His affirmative alternative tries to understand what even an antagonistic type contributes to a larger economy. The priests he attacks have, among other effects, helped produce their opponent.

Affirmation nevertheless faces a test. Recognizing that an opponent helped form you does not establish that every action by that opponent was desirable. Understanding a destructive practice and opposing its continuation can belong to the same response. Ask what a discipline enables, what it diminishes, and whether its standard acknowledges the conditions of its own authority.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does spiritualizing a passion just give harmful conduct a more flattering name?

It could. A cultivated vocabulary, artistic presentation, or calm manner does not prove that an underlying practice has improved. The chapter's strongest distinction is between changing a passion's form and merely renaming its immediate discharge. To defend a case of spiritualization, identify an increased capacity for restraint, complexity, or productive relation to opposition, using §§1–3. Do not substitute admiration for that explanation.

An opponent can still ask whose capacities count and who bears the costs. Nietzsche's criterion does not supply an agreed rule for resolving conflicts between lives. Sections 5–6 make that problem harder by rejecting an external judge. A responsible interpretation should preserve both his challenge to blanket condemnation and the unresolved demand to assess particular injuries. Understanding how an impulse arises does not determine whether its expression should continue.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

What does §1 concede about the passions?

They can be destructive in undeveloped forms. Nietzsche disputes the inference that eliminating the underlying capacity is therefore the best response; he proposes its cultivation and transformation.

Why is immediate reaction not Nietzsche's ideal in §2?

An inability to refrain can indicate dependence on a stimulus. His distinction permits restraint and moderation while questioning the conversion of difficulty with a desire into universal hatred of it.

What joins love and hostility in §3?

Both can become more complex and disciplined without their motivating energies disappearing. An opponent can become a productive condition of development rather than simply an obstacle to annihilate.

Does §4 reject every moral prohibition?

No. Nietzsche allows commands and prohibitions that serve life. He opposes morality that condemns its basic sources, which makes the purpose and effects of a prohibition decisive questions.

What follows from §5's rejection of a position outside life?

Condemning life expresses a situated valuation rather than an external verdict. Nietzsche still ranks ways of living, leaving readers to examine whether his rankings acknowledge their own perspective.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 300–400 words answering: When does discipline transform a passion, and when does it impoverish a life?

  1. State a distinction drawn from ‘Morality as Anti-Nature’ §§1–2, and illustrate it with an example of your own, clearly identified as such.
  2. Use §3 to explain what a productive form of opposition would add to your example. Specify a condition under which that opposition would become destructive.
  3. Bring in §§5–6 to question the authority of your judgment. Give the strongest objection to your criterion and answer it without appealing to an unquestionable model of the ideal person.

End by stating what evidence would make you revise your assessment of the discipline.
