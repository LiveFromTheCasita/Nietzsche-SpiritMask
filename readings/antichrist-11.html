Before you read

Read §§58–61 with the account of rank and religious legislation in §57 beside you. These pages ask what kinds of culture deserve preservation and who supposedly destroyed them. Nietzsche’s answer depends on the hierarchy of ends established in the preceding lesson. The historical narrative does not begin from a neutral standard of improvement.

Make three headings in your notes: what Nietzsche admires, what he claims happened, and what he imagines might have happened. Keep them distinct. A civilization’s achievements, an explanation of its transformation, and a possible future allegedly lost through that transformation require different kinds of support.

The main question: How much does Nietzsche’s story of Europe establish about Christianity, and how much does it reveal about his own standards of cultural greatness?

Three things to notice

  1. The harvest that never arrives In §§58–61, civilizations become preparations for an abundant future that Christianity allegedly prevents. Notice how the loss of unrealized possibilities magnifies the accusation beyond the destruction of achievements that can actually be described.
  2. Admiration has selective conditions In §§59–60, Nietzsche praises Islam and Moorish culture through nobility, masculine strength, sensual refinement, and opposition to Christianity. This is a ranking of civilizations according to his preferences, not an argument for equal respect among religions.
  3. An imaginary pope In §61, Cesare Borgia as pope is a wished-for possibility. Identify its role in the argument before treating it as historical evidence: Nietzsche imagines Christianity’s governing center occupied by dispositions opposed to Christian valuation.

A way to read the passages

The fate of Rome becomes a test of ends: §58

The opening of §58 returns to the issue of deception. Nietzsche says that a falsehood’s purpose matters: does it preserve conditions for flourishing or destroy them? His contrast with §57 therefore does not condemn every religious use of authority by one uniform rule. The legislation he admires secures a ranked order; Christianity allegedly disintegrates one. Imperial Rome becomes the exemplary achievement of organization, a structure capable of surviving individual rulers and sustaining projects across generations. His standard is the capacity to prepare a great cultural future, rather than the equal standing or immediate happiness of everyone within the empire.

Nietzsche then attributes Rome’s destruction to Christian redirection of attachment. Immortality and judgment supposedly make earthly institutions seem unworthy of lasting loyalty. This is a proposed mechanism, not just an insult: change what people regard as ultimately real, and you change what they will maintain. But the force of the mechanism is not evidence that it explains the whole transformation. A historical investigation would have to weigh institutions, conflict, material conditions, and the different conduct of Christians. Calling their influence parasitic supplies a verdict before those comparisons occur. Notice also how conquest disappears into the image of preparing ground for culture.

Intellectual methods and an enlarged enemy: §§58–59

The story grows stranger when §58 describes the religious forces opposed by Epicurus as already Christian in tendency. Here Christianity means more than the historical church. It names a pattern of guilt, punishment, immortality, and hostility to worldly life that Nietzsche claims to recognize across different cults. Paul becomes the organizer who gathers these tendencies into a potent movement. Distinguish this interpretive category from evidence about Paul’s intentions. The account also employs an explicitly hostile Jewish stereotype and contemptuous caste language. Those devices make religious and social identity do argumentative work that a reconstruction of motives would need to establish independently.

Section 59 identifies something more definite that Nietzsche values: disciplined inquiry, careful reading, observation, patience, and continuity in transmitting methods. A culture loses more than conclusions when it loses the habits through which conclusions can be checked. That is a substantial question about the conditions of knowledge. Yet the claim that antiquity’s entire achievement vanished immediately is sweeping polemic. Within Nietzsche’s own image, what hurts most is an interrupted future, not merely a damaged archive. Ask separately whether a practice declined, what caused that decline, and how confidently anyone can predict the achievements an uninterrupted tradition would have produced.

What the praise of Islam does and does not show: §§59–60

Nietzsche’s favorable treatment of Islam disrupts any simple equation of his position with Christian Europe defending itself against an external religious enemy. Section 60 instead presents Moorish civilization as a bearer of sensuous refinement and cultural abundance, while Christian warriors appear as acquisitive destroyers. His appeal to Frederick II supplies a preferred figure of political and intellectual independence. These reversals are designed to make the familiar account of Christian cultural superiority lose its self-evidence. They ask the reader to imagine the crusading side as culturally inferior to the world it attacks.

Reversing a prejudice does not automatically produce an adequate history. The passage compresses diverse peoples, periods, motives, and conflicts into two opposing valuations. Its claim that the crusades amount simply to piracy would need evidence beyond the insistence with which it is stated. Nor does Nietzsche defend religious tolerance as a general principle. He makes admiration conditional on the qualities he prizes and pairs it with degrading comparisons involving Jews, Germans, and subordinate social types. Read the praise seriously without converting it into an inclusive political outlook. Selected admiration proves neither the accuracy of his historical account nor a commitment to freedom for every believer.

The Renaissance as an unfinished transvaluation: §61

Transvaluation means reconsidering and reversing the standards by which values receive their rank. In §61 Nietzsche interprets the Renaissance as a collective attempt to enthrone dispositions that Christianity condemns: worldly splendor, daring, cultivated appetite, and a confident attachment to life. The crucial theater is the papacy itself. A transformation at Christianity’s center would be more decisive than criticism from outside because the authority of the institution would remain while its operative standards changed. This explains the deliberately provocative fantasy of Cesare Borgia as pope. It dramatizes a desired reversal; it is not an event the reader should enter into a chronology.

The fantasy also exposes a problem in Nietzsche’s standard. A ruler’s beauty, daring, or capacity to command cannot by themselves justify everything done through that power. The text’s enthusiasm invites us to move rapidly from admiration of force and style to approval of cultural rule. Pause at that crossing. If Renaissance magnificence required coercion or placed others at the disposal of exceptional figures, those costs belong in the assessment. Calling the result an affirmation of life does not decide whose life is affirmed. Nietzsche’s celebration depends on a selective distribution of significance that the reader can identify and contest.

Luther and the logic of the lost opportunity: §61

Luther enters as the person who misreads the supposed victory of life as religious corruption. On Nietzsche’s account, attacking the papacy restores Christian seriousness and thereby rescues the valuation that Renaissance Rome was overcoming. This is the paradox: an institutional rebellion can strengthen the deeper ideal it claims to purify. Separate that intelligible possibility from the claim that Luther ruined Europe’s final great opportunity. The latter depends on a counterfactual, an account of what would have happened if the intervention had not occurred. Nietzsche treats the imagined continuation of the Renaissance as both desirable and sufficiently secure to count as a stolen future.

His final attack on Germans extends the pattern across philosophy and politics, making a national character responsible for repeated cultural frustration. The insults intensify his judgment without demonstrating this continuity. The lesson’s central question is now sharper: who gets to decide which possible future counts as Europe’s proper story? Nietzsche makes Christian success appear as catastrophe by adopting different standards of achievement. Readers can learn from that displacement without accepting the exclusive causal explanation or the preferred hierarchy. A useful reading preserves the distinction between exposing the values inside a historical narrative and proving that a replacement narrative is true.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does rejecting Nietzsche’s sweeping history mean that these sections have nothing to teach?

No. An overextended explanation can contain a mechanism worth investigating. Beliefs about another world may redirect attention away from some earthly commitments; religious reform may renew the authority of an ideal even while weakening its officials. Those possibilities can be examined without claiming that Christianity alone destroyed Rome or that Luther necessarily prevented a superior future. Say precisely which level of the argument you are accepting.

Keep the standards under examination too. Nietzsche’s account challenges a story in which religious expansion automatically counts as improvement. But his replacement privileges durable rule, exceptional achievement, cultivated taste, and rank. People harmed or subordinated by an admired civilization remain part of its history. The strongest response neither dismisses every question because of the polemic nor treats aesthetic admiration as sufficient justification for power.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why does §58 distinguish the purposes of religious deception?

Nietzsche judges means through their ends: he favors preservation of a ranked culture and condemns its disintegration. His argument is not a consistent prohibition of every religious falsehood.

What does §59 identify beyond buildings and political power?

It values the accumulated habits and methods of inquiry, including disciplined reading and attention to evidence. Nietzsche claims their interruption destroyed possibilities for future knowledge.

Why is the praise of Islam not enough to establish religious tolerance?

Sections 59–60 admire selected qualities and use Islam against Christianity. They do not defend equal religious standing and retain hostile hierarchies and stereotypes.

What status does Borgia as pope have in §61?

It is a desired counterfactual, illustrating a reversal of Christian values at the seat of Christian authority, rather than a reported historical event.

How does Luther supposedly restore what he attacks?

Nietzsche argues that criticism of papal corruption renews Christian moral seriousness. The attack changes institutional relationships while rescuing Christianity’s underlying standards.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 350–500 words assessing one lost opportunity in §§58–61. Use at least two assigned sections and distinguish admiration, historical explanation, and counterfactual expectation.

  1. Identify the cultural achievement Nietzsche values and explain the standard that makes it valuable to him.
  2. Reconstruct the mechanism by which Christianity or Christian reform allegedly interrupts its development.
  3. Name the evidence a historical assessment would require. Do not substitute Nietzsche’s confidence for that evidence.
  4. Offer one objection concerning causation or the costs of his preferred hierarchy, then consider the strongest response available from the text.

Finish by stating what remains worth investigating after the accusation has been made more precise. Preserve this distinction between a penetrating question and an established conclusion for the final lesson.
