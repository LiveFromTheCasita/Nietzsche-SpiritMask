Why Are We So Certain?

Human, All Too Human — Essential Reading Path · Reading 9 of 10

Read: I §483, then I §630 (1878), followed by AOM §5 (1879). Read each in full, stopping before I §484, I §631, and AOM §6 respectively.
Allow: About 50–65 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternatives: I §483 · I §630 (Helen Zimmern); AOM §5 (Paul V. Cohn).

I means Volume I; AOM means Assorted Opinions and Maxims (called Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions in the free edition); WS means The Wanderer and His Shadow. Numbers restart in each collection. Before you read

A colleague proposes changing a familiar routine. You are certain the change will fail. When asked why, you remember one unsuccessful attempt, then the years you have spent defending the existing arrangement. The conversation begins to feel like a challenge to your competence.

Several things have become entangled: evidence about a proposal, confidence in your judgment, and a desire to preserve your standing. Your investment does not establish that you are wrong. It does make the reasons for your certainty worth examining.

Reading 8 tested Nietzsche's claims about groups and political independence. We found places where sweeping judgments outran their support. This session gives us more precise tools for understanding that movement, and for noticing it in ourselves.

I §483 is extremely brief. Let its comparison trouble you, then read I §630 for an account of conviction's assumptions and consequences. AOM §5, from the 1879 supplement, turns to another route by which assurance grows: a limited observation expands into an explanation of everything.

The main question: How can we commit ourselves to a judgment without treating our attachment to it as evidence that it must be true?

Three things to notice

  1. The special meaning of conviction. I §630 defines it through a claim to possess absolute truth. Keep that definition beside I §483; ordinary confidence and this kind of certainty are not automatically the same.
  2. The work done by sacrifice. Why might willingness to suffer for a belief increase its authority without improving its evidence? Notice how the argument turns toward both persecutors and the persecuted.
  3. The change in a claim's reach. In AOM §5, follow an observation from a particular time and place into a supposedly necessary truth. Then follow the word will as its scope expands.

A way to read the passages

A provocation about what obstructs truth

I §483 ranks conviction as a greater danger to truth than lying. It is a compressed challenge, not a complete argument. Before accepting the ranking, ask what feature of conviction could make it so resistant to inquiry. I §483, the comparison of dangers

One possible explanation is that a deliberate liar recognizes a distinction between what happened and what they want others to believe. Someone certain of possessing truth may experience no comparable need to check the favored account. Sincerity can then make resistance to correction look like integrity.

That explanation is a way to develop the provocation, rather than something the one-sentence aphorism itself demonstrates. Lying can also corrupt evidence, organize institutions, and obstruct investigation. We should not turn Nietzsche's comparison into an excuse for deception or a proven ranking of every possible case.

The useful question is where a person places a belief beyond correction. I §630 supplies the more developed account. Read it before deciding that every strongly held opinion belongs to Nietzsche's target.

Three claims hidden inside certainty

I §630 defines conviction as believing oneself to possess absolute truth about a matter of knowledge. Nietzsche unpacks three assumptions: there are absolute truths; perfect methods for obtaining them have been found; and the convinced person has successfully used those methods. I §630, the definition and its consequences

These assumptions concern different things. A truth's existence would not establish our ability to reach it. A reliable procedure would not guarantee that a particular person had applied it correctly. The believer's assurance can jump across both gaps.

Consider the opening workplace disagreement. You might have good evidence that an earlier change failed. That does not establish that every similar change must fail, that your method of comparison overlooks nothing, or that you have applied it without error to this proposal. The example makes the steps visible; it does not prove a psychological theory about all workplace disputes.

Nietzsche calls the convinced person intellectually immature. His harshness attacks a failure to investigate the grounds of assurance. The alternative suggested by his appeal to scientific thought is a practice of inquiry whose claims remain answerable to methods, reasons, and correction.

Why sacrifice cannot certify a belief

The aphorism next grants conviction enormous historical power. People have endured losses and death for what they took to be truth. Nietzsche challenges the inference from that endurance to the truth of the doctrine defended.

A sacrifice can show the strength of an attachment. It cannot, merely by being costly, establish the accuracy of the belief. Two opponents might make equally terrible sacrifices for incompatible doctrines. The costs do not supply a method for deciding between them.

Nietzsche presses further, suggesting that people often fought to vindicate themselves and protect what they regarded as their salvation. The threat of losing a belief could become a threat to the meaning of their lives. Desire then directs the intellect's defense.

Keep the difference between his logical criticism and his psychological reconstruction. The first identifies why sacrifice is insufficient proof. The second proposes motives for historical actors, on a sweeping scale. Those motives need evidence; they are not established simply because we can imagine them.

His claim that probably no one has sacrificed themselves for truth is deliberately severe. Preserve the qualifying uncertainty and the scope of the challenge. We need not endorse that historical generalization to recognize the gap between devotion and justification.

When the threatened belief attacks inquiry

The believer's problem intensifies when counterarguments become strong. Nietzsche describes a defense that turns against intellect itself: if reasoning threatens the conviction, the believer may declare reasoning inadequate and make the very resistance to rational examination a mark of faith.

The crucial movement is a change in the rules. A person initially speaks as though their belief has reasons, then abandons the authority of reasons when those reasons cease to support the desired conclusion. The conviction survives by changing what can count against it.

Nietzsche consequently distinguishes conflict among opinions from conflict over the supposed invulnerability of opinions. People can disagree while sharing ways of testing claims. When each side identifies its survival or salvation with being unanswerably right, correction becomes harder to tolerate.

The energy that could have gone into examination

I §630 ends by imagining a different use of conviction's energy. If people had devoted even part of their strenuous defense to examining their entitlement to believe and the route by which they arrived there, Nietzsche thinks history would have contained more knowledge and less persecution. The aphorism proposes this alternative history without demonstrating it.

He makes the inquiry run in two directions. Persecutors might have questioned their authority to defend absolute truth; those condemned as heretics might have lost their attachment to inadequately grounded doctrines. Both are asked to examine, although their political positions and responsibility for persecution are plainly different.

Questioning a persecuted person's doctrine does not justify persecuting them. Nor does the presence of unexamined convictions on opposing sides make their actions or power equal. Keep those distinctions visible when reading Nietzsche's sweeping counterfactual.

Pause here: State one judgment you have good reason to trust. Add one possible discovery that would require you to qualify it. Having an answer does not weaken every reason for confidence; it identifies the conditions of that confidence.

A maxim has a scale

AOM §5 shifts attention from the believer's attachment to the making of philosophical claims. Its target is a recurrent appropriation: philosophers take observations from writers about human conduct and turn them into unconditional necessities. AOM §5, the criticism of generalization

Here moralists means observers of motives and behavior, including the writers of psychological maxims encountered earlier in the course. They need not be issuing moral commands. A remark can offer an approximate insight into people in a particular country, town, or decade.

The qualification is part of the insight. Suppose you notice that members of one committee defend proposals more fiercely after publicly endorsing them. That might justify investigating the role of pride in that setting. Turning it into a claim that every human judgment always serves vanity would add a reach the observation never earned.

Nietzsche accuses philosophers of imagining this enlargement makes their work superior. But the transformation can corrupt what was useful. A limited remark open to comparison becomes a law that dictates what every case must mean.

This is why merely doubting more is an incomplete response to the passage. We need to track a claim's subject, circumstances, strength, and exceptions. A smaller claim can be more informative because its relation to evidence is clearer.

Following the expansion of will

Schopenhauer provides Nietzsche's central example. The aphorism identifies three doctrines: the superiority of will over intellect, the unchangeability of character, and the negative character of pleasure. Nietzsche calls them errors in the sense Schopenhauer gives them and traces them to maxims of practical psychological observation.

The passage does not independently refute each doctrine. Its detailed example concerns the word will. Nietzsche says that Schopenhauer gathered several human conditions under that name, filling a gap in language. As a writer of psychological observations, he could benefit from the resulting freedom of expression.

That initial usefulness is significant. Nietzsche is not arguing that a new term becomes worthless merely because it groups different experiences. The problem is what happens when the grouping is made to explain more than the inquiry supports.

The next expansion gives will to everything in nature. Nietzsche calls this a poetic metaphor. Then the term becomes available to mystical accounts of a single universal will. The same word travels from describing human conditions to naming the supposed unity of existence.

To see the problem, imagine describing a plant as trying to reach the light. The phrasing may vividly describe its growth. It does not by itself establish that plant growth and human deliberation are expressions of one inner agency. Carrying a familiar word across cases cannot do all the explanatory work.

Nietzsche's final mockery targets the worshipful confidence attached to this universal explanation. The philosophical issue remains the unsupported expansion. A metaphor's vividness, a term's convenience, and a doctrine's popularity are different from grounds for its truth.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does the criticism of conviction require us to stop judging or acting?

The assigned passages do not supply a rulebook for every uncertain decision. They do, however, distinguish claims to unquestionable possession from examination of grounds. We can make judgments proportionate to available evidence, act on them, and remain willing to revise them.

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation to investigate. A supported judgment has survived relevant scrutiny to some degree. Conviction in I §630's defined sense adds an assurance of absolute possession. Treating these as interchangeable would erase the very work of investigation Nietzsche recommends.

Universal suspicion could itself become an unexamined identity: I am the person who never trusts anything. That stance might resist correction as stubbornly as the beliefs it attacks. The point is to improve discrimination among claims, including our reasons for doubt.

The test returns to Nietzsche. I §630 offers a large historical explanation; AOM §5 speaks broadly about philosophers across time. Their force does not excuse them from their own demand for qualification. Ask which smaller claims they illuminate, and where their language outruns the argument.

This is also a way to carry Reading 8 forward. Recognizing the danger of generalization gives us a reason to scrutinize Nietzsche's claims about women and peoples. It does not automatically prove any complete alternative account. We must do the work of giving reasons for our own judgments.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why should I §483 be read beside I §630?

The short aphorism supplies a provocative comparison but no developed argument. I §630 specifies conviction as belief in possessing absolute truth and examines its assumptions. That definition helps prevent the comparison from becoming a blanket attack on every degree of confidence.

What are the three assumptions inside conviction?

Absolute truths exist, perfect methods for obtaining them have been found, and the believer has used those methods successfully. Each step requires support. Confidence in the conclusion does not establish the reliability of the route.

What can sacrifice establish, and what can it leave unsettled?

It can reveal the intensity of attachment to a belief. Its cost does not establish the belief's truth. Nietzsche also proposes psychological motives for historical sacrifices, but those explanations require evidence beyond the fact of sacrifice itself.

What gets lost when a maxim becomes an unconditional law?

Its original limits: the people, circumstances, period, and approximate character of the observation. A philosopher may then treat possible exceptions as appearances to explain away, instead of evidence that should qualify the claim.

Why does AOM §5 follow the changing use of the word will?

It shows a term moving from several human conditions to all natural things and finally to one universal reality. Nietzsche challenges the inference across those scales. A useful psychological expression or poetic metaphor does not establish a metaphysical explanation.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one claim about human behavior that you find persuasive. Write 180–250 words:

  1. State the claim and the observations that support it. Mark what you directly know and what you have inferred.
  2. Use AOM §5 to limit its reach. Which people and circumstances does your evidence actually cover? Rewrite the claim with those limits visible.
  3. Use I §630 to examine your attachment. Would abandoning the claim threaten your pride, loyalties, or picture of yourself? Treat your proposed motive as something to investigate.
  4. Name one counterexample or finding that would change your judgment. Explain what you could responsibly conclude now, before that inquiry is complete.
  5. Return to I §483: where might sincere certainty obstruct this investigation more effectively than an obvious falsehood?

A successful response distinguishes a vivid example from proof, and a justified degree of confidence from a demand to be beyond correction. The goal is a more exact judgment.
