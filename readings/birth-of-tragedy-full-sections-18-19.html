Alexandrian Culture and Its Art

Optional full-text route · Station 14 of 19

Read: §18 and §19 of The Birth of Tragedy, both in full.
Allow: About 65–95 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response. §19 is one of the longest sections in the book, so you may want to read §18 and §19 in separate sittings.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Haussmann): §18 · §19.

Before you read

§17 ended with the cheerful theoretical person who says to life, “it is worth while to know thee.” §18 asks what that cheerfulness rests on. Nietzsche’s answer moves in three steps: from the psychology of individuals, to a type of culture, to a claim about the social order such a culture requires. §19 then names the art that belongs to this culture, opera, and turns at its end to the hope that German music and philosophy are reversing the whole process.

These two sections contain some of the book’s most explicit social and political claims. This guide states them plainly as Nietzsche’s claims, so that you can examine them.

The main question: How does Nietzsche move from a psychology of the illusions that keep us living to a diagnosis of modern culture, and what social order does that diagnosis assume?

Read §18 once, then §19. In §18, mark the sentence where Nietzsche moves from culture to society. In §19, mark where the account of opera turns into a claim about the present.

What to notice

  1. Three illusions. At the start of §18, which three “illusions” bind people to life? Which one is the comfort the book itself has been describing?
  2. What optimism hides. What does Nietzsche say Alexandrian culture requires but will not admit? How does he describe the people it depends on?
  3. Opera’s origin. In §19, what need does Nietzsche say gave rise to opera, and why does he call it an “unæsthetic” need?

Close reading

Three illusions

§18 begins with what Nietzsche calls an eternal phenomenon. The insatiable will, which Haussmann calls “the avidious will,” can always keep its creatures living by spreading an illusion over things. There are three illusions:

  • the Socratic love of knowledge, with its hope “to heal the eternal wound of existence”;
  • “art’s seductive veil of beauty”;
  • the metaphysical comfort that eternal life flows on beneath the flux of appearances.

These are, he says, for “the more nobly endowed natures.” §18, the three illusions

Notice the third item. The metaphysical comfort the book has offered since §7, and stated most fully in §17, appears here as one of the will’s illusions. The book does not stop to comment on this. Ask what it means for the comfort to be both the goal of tragic art and an illusion that keeps us alive.

From these three illusions Nietzsche derives three kinds of culture: Socratic, artistic, and tragic. Historically, he says, they are “an Alexandrine or a Hellenic or a Buddhistic culture.” The modern world, he says, is “entangled in the meshes of Alexandrine culture.” Its ideal is the theorist, and Socrates is that theorist’s archetype. Education serves this ideal, and the cultured person has long taken the form of the scholar. Faust, storming through all the faculties, shows the modern person beginning to sense the limits of the Socratic love of knowledge. §18, Alexandrine culture

What optimism hides

At the heart of this culture, Nietzsche says, lies “Optimism, deeming itself absolute!” He then makes a claim about society, and it should be stated as plainly as he states it.

Nietzsche claims that “the Alexandrine culture requires a slave class, to be able to exist permanently.” Its optimism, he says, denies that such a class is necessary. When “the belief in the earthly happiness of all” becomes a “threatening demand,” the culture drifts toward “a dreadful destination.” He calls the talk of “the ‘dignity of man’ and the ‘dignity of labour’” “beautifully seductive and tranquillising utterances.” And he writes: “There is nothing more terrible than a barbaric slave class, who have learned to regard their existence as an injustice,” and who prepare to take vengeance. §18, the slave class

Keep the kinds of claim apart as you reread:

  • Nietzsche’s textual claim: a high culture of the Alexandrian kind depends on a subordinate labouring class. The demand that everyone share in earthly happiness threatens that culture. So does the belief of those who labour that their condition is unjust.
  • The site’s interpretation: the passage treats the labourers’ sense of injustice as a danger to be feared, not as a claim to be weighed. It treats the language of human dignity as a soothing illusion, not as a principle.
  • What the passage does not do: it offers no evidence about any actual society. It is a diagnosis, stated with confidence.
  • Evaluation: whether a culture can or should rest on such a class is your question to answer. The passage gives you Nietzsche’s answer, not an argument that settles it.

Do not soften this into a remark about inward nobility. Nietzsche is making a claim about social order. Do not inflate it either. The passage is a diagnosis of a culture’s hidden premise, not a political program.

Nietzsche adds that the “pale and exhausted religions” of the present cannot meet this danger, because myth, which every religion needs, has been paralysed by the same optimism. §18, religion and myth

Kant, Schopenhauer, and a tragic culture

Against this optimism, Nietzsche credits Kant and Schopenhauer with “extraordinary courage and wisdom.” They won “the victory over the optimism hidden in the essence of logic.” They did so by showing that space, time, and causality govern only appearances and cannot reach the essence of things. From this he says a new culture begins, “a tragic culture.” Its mark is that “wisdom takes the place of science as the highest end,” a wisdom that seeks to apprehend the world’s eternal suffering as its own, “with sympathetic feelings of love.” §18, a tragic culture

He then imagines a rising generation of “dragon-slayers.” They would turn their backs on optimism to live “resolutely” in the whole. Would such a person not need a new art, “the art of metaphysical comfort,” namely tragedy? Nietzsche closes the thought with two lines from Goethe’s Faust, printed in German. §18, the rising generation

This is the passage Nietzsche would quote back in 1886. The route’s first lesson asked you to note it in your ledger; the retrospect checkpoint below returns to it.

The hungry critic

§18 ends with the theoretical person afraid of his own conclusions. He “runs timidly up and down the bank” of existence, unwilling to enter the stream. Modern art, collecting the styles of every period, cannot help him. The Alexandrian person remains “the eternal hungerer,” the joyless “critic,” “a librarian and corrector of proofs,” going blind among books and misprints. §18, the Alexandrian critic

Opera: the art of Socratic culture

§19 names the art of this culture: “the culture of the opera.” Nietzsche begins from the origins of the stilo rappresentativo, the half-sung style of recitative, and contrasts it with the sacred music of Palestrina from the same age. The recitative, he argues, served a listener who wanted to hear the words: half speech, half song, a “superficial mosaic conglutination” of epic and lyric delivery. Its inventors believed they had recovered the music of the ancient Greeks. §19, the recitative

Beneath the style Nietzsche finds a need, and he calls it “unæsthetic”: “the yearning for the idyll, the belief in the prehistoric existence of the artistic, good man.” Opera, he says, became “the oppositional dogma of the good man,” the humanists’ answer to the church’s picture of humanity as corrupt. §19, the idyll of the good man

Treat this as Nietzsche’s cultural interpretation of opera’s origins. He names circles in Florence, but he offers no music history beyond what the interpretation needs, and this guide does not certify his history.

The “good primitive man” wants his rights

Nietzsche then makes a second political claim that parallels §18. The principle of opera, the belief in the naturally good and artistic human being, “has gradually changed into a threatening and terrible demand, which, in face of the socialistic movements of the present time, we can no longer ignore.” And: “The ‘good primitive man’ wants his rights: what paradisiac prospects!” §19, the demand

Read this with §18. In both sections, an optimistic belief about human beings, earthly happiness for all or natural goodness, becomes a social demand. Nietzsche presents that demand as a threat. He names the socialist movements of his own time. Again, state what he says; the evaluation belongs to you.

The layman’s art

Opera, Nietzsche says, “is the birth of the theoretical man, of the critical layman, not of the artist.” It rests on “the idyllic belief that every sentient man is an artist.” It makes the words master and the music servant. He fears that under its influence art’s serious task will degenerate into pastime. That task, in his words, is to free the eye from its glance into the horrors of night and to heal through appearance. Music will lose its dignity as the “Dionysian mirror of the world” and become again, as in the New Dithyramb, a slave of phenomena. §19, the layman’s art

The reverse process

Then the tone changes. Against all this, Nietzsche says, the most trustworthy signs promise “the reverse process”: the awakening of the Dionysian spirit in the modern world. Out of the Dionysian root of the German spirit has come German music, “from Bach to Beethoven, from Beethoven to Wagner.” He mocks aesthetes who chase it with a net of “beauty,” naming the scholar Otto Jahn. He calls German music “the only genuine, pure and purifying fire-spirit” of the culture, and invokes “Heraclitus of Ephesus.” §19, German music

German philosophy, through Kant and Schopenhauer, belongs with it: “Dionysian wisdom comprised in concepts.” Nietzsche suggests that the modern world is passing through the Greek epochs “in reverse order,” from the Alexandrian back to the tragic. He describes this as a return of the German spirit to itself after long servitude to foreign forms, freed from “the leading-strings of a Romanic civilisation” if it learns from the Greeks. The section ends: “we experience a re-birth of tragedy.” §19, the rebirth

Compare the opening of §16 (station 13). There Nietzsche promised to name “the forces … which seem to me to guarantee a re-birth of tragedy.” The rebirth was a hope whose guarantors were still to come. At the end of §19 he speaks of it as something “we experience.” The earlier hope has become his claim about present experience. Do his examples of German music and philosophy establish that such a rebirth is under way? This is also where a national framing begins to carry the argument. §§20–23 develop both.

A difficulty

Is Nietzsche’s claim about the slave class a description, a warning, or a judgment, and does the rest of his argument need it?

Read the key sentences of §18 again. The word “requires” sounds like a description of how such a culture works. The words “terrible” and “dreadful destination” sound like a warning. Calling the language of human dignity “tranquillising” sounds like a judgment about the value of that language. The passage slides between the three.

Now ask what the rest of the book needs:

  • The account of three illusions, and of Kant and Schopenhauer as limiting knowledge, does not obviously depend on the claim about labour.
  • The diagnosis of Alexandrian culture as optimistic might.
  • The attack on opera in §19 repeats the same pattern with the “good primitive man.”

Write two or three sentences on whether you could accept Nietzsche’s criticism of theoretical optimism while rejecting his account of the social order it supposedly requires. Then write one sentence on what you would have to give up by doing so.

A second difficulty concerns the book itself. In §18, metaphysical comfort is listed among the will’s illusions. In the same section, a coming tragic culture is said to need “the art of metaphysical comfort.” Can the book call its central consolation an illusion and still recommend it?

Retrospect checkpoint: an 1886 comparison

If you are following the chronological alternate, skip this until after the 1886 preface lesson.

The self-criticism’s §7 quotes the passage you have just read about a rising generation that would desire “the art of metaphysical comfort.” It answers that such readers are likely to end “metaphysically comforted,” like Romantics, and should learn “the art of earthly comfort” first.

  • Does the 1886 objection answer the argument of §18, or only its tone?
  • Notice also that §18 already listed metaphysical comfort among the will’s illusions. How much of the later objection was already in the text?

Two further comparisons:

  • Self-criticism §6 regrets the book’s hopes for German music and the “spirit of Teutonism.” Compare that with the end of §19.
  • Self-criticism §4 asks whether democracy is a symptom of declining vigour. That is a different claim from §18’s fear of a slave class, and §19’s fear of the “good primitive man,” made in a different vocabulary. Do not merge them. Record where they differ.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What does Nietzsche claim Alexandrian culture requires, and why is its optimism a danger to it?

He claims it requires a slave class to exist permanently, while its optimism denies that such a class is necessary. When the belief in earthly happiness for all becomes a demand, and when those who labour come to regard their existence as an injustice, the culture faces what he calls a dreadful destination. This is Nietzsche’s claim, stated as a diagnosis, without evidence about any actual society.

What need does Nietzsche say gave rise to opera?

An “unæsthetic” need: the longing for an idyll, and the belief in a naturally good and artistic primitive human being. Opera, on his account, made words master and music servant for listeners who wanted to understand. He calls it the art of the theoretical person and the critical layman. The history is his interpretation.

Where does the rebirth of tragedy come from, according to the end of §19?

From German music, from Bach through Beethoven to Wagner, and from German philosophy in Kant and Schopenhauer. Nietzsche presents these as a reverse movement from the Alexandrian back toward the tragic. He also presents them as the German spirit’s return to itself, free of “a Romanic civilisation.” What §16 offered as a hope, to be guaranteed by forces not yet named, §19 now states as something “we experience.”

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

What does Nietzsche think optimism conceals, and what would it cost to accept his diagnosis?

  • Start with the three illusions at the opening of §18.
  • State Nietzsche’s claim about the slave class in his own terms, with one short quotation.
  • Connect it to the “good primitive man” in §19.
  • Then separate what Nietzsche says, what you take the passage to imply, and your own evaluation, in three distinct sentences.

A successful response states the social claim accurately, without softening or exaggerating it. It identifies the step where the argument moves from culture to society, and it reaches an evaluation that is clearly marked as the writer’s own.
