Forty Miniatures: Courtesy, Punishment, and the Spectator’s View

Optional full-text route · Station 18 of 35 (proposed)

Review draft. This lesson belongs to a proposed full-text route that has not been published. Only some stations are drafted so far. For the complete published introduction, use the ten-session guided reading path.

Read: Book III, §§202–241, in full, from the original 1882 edition. Begin at §202 and stop before §242.
Allow: About 45–65 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. This is an editorial estimate, not a measured reading time. The primary text is short, about 1,400 words in the free edition, but it contains 40 separate judgments.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): begin at §202 · §219 · §233.

Quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation, including his section titles. Kaufmann’s wording differs, and none of it is quoted here. Many of these aphorisms share a printed page, so a link may land a few lines above the one named; count the section numbers down from there.

Before you read

This is the second of three stations of very short aphorisms that close Book III (stations 17–19). It has no famous passage. That makes it a good test of how to read a run of miniatures: not by skimming, and not by treating each line as a free-standing slogan.

The main question: What patterns connect kindness, punishment, and perspective in these forty judgments—and whose point of view do they take?

How to read a run of miniatures

The same method serves all three miniature stations, 17–19.

  1. First pass, straight through. Read all 40 without stopping to interpret. Put a mark beside each: P (psychological observation), H (historical claim), V (valuation or ranking), or ? (where Nietzsche himself hedges, as with “perhaps”). Note who speaks: an “I,” a “we,” a dialogue, a named speaker, or an impersonal maxim.
  2. Second pass, with a pencil. Star three sections: one you disagree with, one you do not understand, and one you think is simply true.
  3. Group, then close-read. Use the groups below or make your own.

You do not need to write about every section. You do need to have read every one.

What to notice

  1. Reasons. §209 describes a way of asking for reasons. Compare it with the demand for reasons in §2.
  2. Victims and spectators. Read §220 twice. Then look for sections in this run that speak about people from outside.
  3. The laughing animal. §224 imagines how animals see us. Which earlier section does it recall?

Close reading

One way to group the run

These groups are this guide’s, not Nietzsche’s. The book prints the sections in one numbered sequence; many have their own titles, but none is printed under group headings like these. A section may belong to more than one group.

  • Kindness, courtesy, and the poor: §§204, 206, 211, 214, 237, 238, 239.
  • Punishment, causes, and reasons: §§205, 209, 217, 219, 220, 229.
  • Perspective and the observer: §§223, 224, 228, 232, 233, 234, 240.
  • Greatness, style, and the multitude: §§202, 203, 207, 208, 210, 212, 213, 215, 216, 218, 222, 225, 226, 230, 231, 235, 236, 241.
  • The sexes and families: §§221, 227.

The largest group is the loosest. Test it against your own marks; several of its sections are really about style, and several about vanity.

A tyrannical way of asking for reasons

Station 2 met a speaker who found it contemptible not to ask for “the ultimate and surest reasons for and against” a belief (§2). §209 adds a qualification. There is “a mode of asking for our reasons” that makes us forget our best reasons and arouses “a spite and repugnance against reason generally”—“properly an artifice of tyrannical men!” §209, asking for reasons

The demand for reasons can itself be used as a weapon. That does not cancel §2. It says that the manner of questioning matters, and that a questioner can make reasons harder to find. Mark it P.

Punishment and sacrifice

Two adjacent sections deserve slow reading. §219: “The object of punishment is to improve him who punishes,—that is the ultimate appeal of those who justify punishment.” The claim is about justification: when defenders of punishment run out of other reasons, their last appeal is to the good it does the punisher. It does not say what punishment ought to be for. §219, punishment

§220: “The victims think otherwise than the spectators about sacrifice and sacrificing: but they have never been allowed to express their opinion.” Mark it P and V. It points to a gap in every account of sacrifice written by those who watched it. §220, victims and spectators

Around them, two short sections about causes keep the question of standpoint open. §205 says that need, supposed to cause things, is often “only the effect of the things themselves.” §217 says: “Before the effect one believes in other causes than after the effect.” What we take as the cause depends on when, and from where, we look. Book III made a related point about cause and effect in §112 (station 13).

The poor, and the sexes

Several sections judge groups from outside. §206, “During the Rain,” pictures poor people crowded together with their cares, “ready and anxious to give pain to one another,” and ends: “This, this only, is the poverty of the poor!” It is a generalization about a class of people, offered as an observation from someone watching the rain. Mark it P and V, and state it plainly: it locates the poverty of the poor in their conduct toward one another. §206, the poor

Two sections concern the sexes. §221 generalizes: “Fathers and sons are much more considerate of one another than mothers and daughters.” §227, “Fallacy, Fallacy,” describes a woman who thinks a man who cannot rule himself will be easy to rule, and ends: “the poor creature, who in a short time will be his slave.” The fallacy is hers; the prediction is the speaker’s. Both sections carry a generalization about women that the text does not support. §227, a fallacy

Perspective and the observer

§224 imagines how animals see us: perhaps as “the absurd animal, the laughing animal, the crying animal, the unfortunate animal.” That recalls station 2, where §1 made laughter the mark of a future wisdom. Here it is one of four ways of seeming absurd. §224, the laughing animal

§233, “The most Dangerous Point of View,” asks for an enormous change of scale. What I now do or neglect is “as important for all that is to come, as the greatest event of the past: in this immense perspective of effects all actions are equally great and small.” Why is this dangerous? Perhaps because it removes every difference of importance at once. Keep it for Book IV, where the demon of §341 asks how one would bear the weight of every action if life recurred (station 25 and Reading 7). §233, a dangerous point of view

§228 takes the opposite line about sameness. Someone who mediates between two decided thinkers “has not an eye for seeing the unique; similarising and equalising are signs of weak eyes.” Mark it V. The same judgment against “equalising” appeared in social form at station 4, where §18 spoke of our “doctrine of the equality of men.” §228, mediators

Greatness and the multitude

The largest group mixes jokes about vanity with a suspicion of public performance. §236 asks whether anyone who wants to move the multitude must first “give a stage representation of himself,” translated into “the grotesquely obvious.” That recalls station 3, where §5 found an “excessive pathos” in those who need unconditional duties. §236, the multitude

§213 is a small dialogue. A sage asks a fool the way to happiness. The fool answers: “Admire yourself, and live on the street!” The sage says that admiring oneself is enough. The fool replies: “But how can one constantly admire without constantly despising?” The fool has the last word, and the better argument. Who speaks, and who is wise, are separate questions. §213, the sage and the fool

A difficulty

Whose point of view?

§220 says that victims think otherwise than spectators and “have never been allowed to express their opinion.” Apply that test to the run itself. §206 speaks about the poor while watching them from a distance. §227 speaks about a woman’s fallacy from outside. §221 generalizes about mothers and daughters. In each case, the people described do not speak.

There are several ways to take this. Perhaps §220 is a principle the collection fails to meet. Perhaps it is a point about sacrifice only, with no wider scope. Or perhaps the run deliberately sets observers’ judgments beside a warning about observers. Notice too that §228 scorns “equalising” as a sign of weak eyes. Does taking the victims’ view seriously require some of the equalizing attention §228 dismisses? Decide which reading the sequence best supports, and name the section that tips it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

How does §209 qualify the demand for reasons in §2?

It describes a way of asking for reasons that makes people forget their best reasons and turn against reason itself, an “artifice of tyrannical men.” The demand for reasons stands; the manner of asking can defeat it.

What does §219 claim about punishment?

That the final justification offered for punishment is that it improves the one who punishes. It is a claim about how punishment is justified, not a theory of what punishment should achieve.

Why is §233’s point of view called dangerous?

The section does not say. One reading: if every action is as consequential as the greatest past event, then all actions become “equally great and small,” and differences of importance vanish. The thought anticipates the weight of every action in §341.

In §213, who gives the better answer, the sage or the fool?

The fool. His last question, how anyone can constantly admire without constantly despising, exposes the sage’s simpler view. The section separates who speaks from who is wise.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

Use §220 to test one other section in this station. Does that section give the view of the people it describes?

  • Choose §206, §221, §227, or one of your starred sections.
  • Quote no more than one phrase from each section.
  • Say whether §228’s scorn for “equalising” helps or hinders your test.

A successful response states the chosen section’s generalization plainly, notices who speaks, and does not treat one aphorism as the whole collection’s position.
