Is Compassion Always Good?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 6 of 10

Read: Book II, §§133–134 in full, stopping before §135.
Allow: About 60–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §133 · §134 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

A friend is distressed. You feel upset too, and want to do something immediately. Perhaps you listen carefully. Perhaps you offer reassurance before understanding what happened. Perhaps your urgency makes the friend responsible for calming you down. A response can feel compassionate while leaving its usefulness unsettled.

These possibilities help us approach Nietzsche's question without deciding it in advance. He examines what moves a person confronted with suffering, then asks whether suffering along with someone necessarily helps. His claims are often stronger than these preliminary questions, and we will need to assess that strength.

The German term Mitleid can be rendered as pity, compassion, or sympathetic suffering. Kennedy's free translation often uses pity. Do not assume that the argument concerns only condescending pity, or that every form of understanding and assistance involves the emotional experience Nietzsche attacks.

Reading 5 examined competing drives in §109. Here a seemingly unified moral feeling becomes a meeting point of different motives. Keep Reading 1's distinction nearby: explaining why we act and justifying what we do are separate tasks.

The main question: What changes when we distinguish another person's suffering, our response to it, and the assistance we might provide?

Three things to notice

  1. The difference between conscious and unconscious concern. In §133, why does not consciously thinking of oneself fail to establish complete selflessness?
  2. The variety under one name. Identify several motives and pleasures Nietzsche gathers under pity. Does finding a personal interest settle an action's worth?
  3. The restricted viewpoint in §134. Nietzsche considers pity insofar as it creates suffering. Watch how he moves from that restriction to much broader judgments about its consequences.

A way to read the passages

An action can involve us before we reflect on ourselves

Section 133 begins with rescue and the sight of someone suffering physically. The conventional explanation says we have stopped thinking of ourselves and act solely for the other person. Nietzsche challenges what that explanation assumes. The absence of conscious self-reference does not demonstrate the absence of personal motives. §133, the opening examples

His comparison is with regaining our balance after slipping. We make coordinated movements without first explaining them to ourselves. Similarly, an immediate response to suffering may involve learned sensitivities and self-protective reactions before any deliberate calculation occurs.

This matters because the argument need not accuse the rescuer of secretly calculating a reward. Nietzsche's alternative is that a person's concerns can operate without being consciously represented. The rescuer may sincerely describe being absorbed in the emergency.

The analogy establishes a possibility worth investigating. It does not prove that every rescue follows the same motivational pattern. We still need evidence about what moved this person in this situation.

One moral word can hide different responses

Nietzsche offers a series of possibilities. Another person's misfortune may expose our inability to help, threaten our pride, remind us of our vulnerability, or offend our sense that something should have been prevented. Helping may remove an uncomfortable feeling of our own.

These explanations differ. Fear of suffering a similar misfortune is not the same as wanting praise. Anger at an injustice is not identical with boredom relieved by an opportunity to intervene. The long list makes the familiar label less informative: naming a response compassion does not finish explaining it.

Consider helping someone whose bicycle has broken down. You might dislike seeing them stranded, enjoy solving a practical problem, remember a time you needed help, and welcome a break from your own routine. Several motives could operate together. Their combination would require closer examination than either calling you purely selfless or dismissing you as merely selfish.

Nietzsche also notices how differently we behave when we can help and when we can only witness distress. Avoidance may reveal that we are trying to regulate our own suffering. It is evidence to consider, though it cannot establish the same explanation for every instance of avoidance.

The useful reading habit is to ask which motive a proposed explanation identifies, and what observation would support it. A persuasive list of possibilities does not show that every listed motive is present in every case.

Relief and pleasure can belong to the same action

The argument becomes more complex when Nietzsche insists that we do not act from a single motive. Compassionate action both relieves discomfort and offers pleasures: gratitude, a sense of power, successful intervention, or the feeling of opposing an injustice.

Notice the pleasure of something gradually succeeding. A person may enjoy watching their assistance work. That enjoyment need not compete with the recipient's improvement; the improvement can be precisely what makes the action satisfying.

This distinction protects us from an easy but unsupported inference. Finding pleasure in helping does not establish that the help is worthless. A wish for applause might distort assistance, especially if it makes the helper prefer conspicuous gestures to useful ones. Enjoyment of successful help might support patient attention. The consequences depend on how the motive operates.

Nietzsche himself makes a stronger claim than the modest view that motives are mixed. He says that compassionate action removes our own misery and denies that its emotional experience is identical with the sufferer's. We should keep that claim visible while asking whether his examples establish it.

Feeling something intensely is not the same as knowing the other person

Nietzsche distinguishes the distress I feel at your suffering from the suffering you experience. My alarm, helplessness, or shame may be absent from your experience altogether. The fact that you occasion my feeling does not make the two feelings identical.

Return to the distressed friend. They may be worried about an uncertain future while you are frightened by your inability to reassure them. Treating your distress as direct knowledge of theirs could lead you to answer the wrong concern.

This explains why Nietzsche challenges the claim that pity gives especially penetrating knowledge of another person's suffering. Emotional intensity is not automatically accuracy. Listening and asking can reveal that our first interpretation was mistaken.

His named opponent is Schopenhauer, whose account Nietzsche represents as making pity the source of all moral action. Nietzsche attacks both its description of the experience and its privilege as a moral foundation. Read this as his polemical account of the dispute, not as a complete presentation of Schopenhauer's philosophy.

A difficult question remains: does my feeling's being mine imply that its concern is only for me? Grief about another person's loss remains my grief, yet its object may be their loss. Distinguishing the two experiences does not by itself establish that all concern is directed back toward the observer.

The person without pity is examined too

Near the end of §133, Nietzsche asks what distinguishes the compassionate person from someone who lacks pity. He describes differences in sensitivity to danger, vulnerability of pride, experience of pain, and attitudes toward emotional softness.

The unpitying person also has investments. They may value self-sufficiency, protect their composure, or regard tears as a threat to masculine courage. Their refusal of pity can serve a preferred image of themselves, just as helping can serve another person's self-image.

Nietzsche calls the two groups different kinds of egoists. He questions the moral fashion that makes one good and the other evil, while observing that an opposing valuation has also prevailed. This is not a simple transfer of praise from compassionate people to the hardhearted.

The contrasting portraits are nevertheless broad generalizations. Habitual exposure to pain does not, without further argument, establish what any particular person will think about another's suffering. The examples unsettle a ranking; they do not provide a reliable classification of every person.

What happens when suffering spreads?

Section 134 changes emphasis. Nietzsche explicitly considers pity insofar as it produces suffering in the observer. On that restricted view, the feeling adds distress to the world. Someone's suffering becomes an occasion for further suffering without necessarily reducing the original burden. §134

We might call this emotional infection: distress spreads between people. That description concerns a possibility in the passage, not a modern psychological finding being supplied to validate it. It is compatible with §133's distinction between experiences; the observer's new suffering need not duplicate the first person's.

Nietzsche acknowledges that compassionate action can reduce suffering, then dismisses such benefits as comparatively insignificant. He even claims that pity prevailing for a day would ruin humanity. These are sweeping assessments, not conclusions established by the examples.

His imagined experiment is to attend repeatedly to every nearby opportunity for pity and to picture the suffering involved. The resulting burden, he claims, would make someone ill. The question is whether attending to suffering must take this absorbing form. Attention might also guide a limited, effective response.

He then imagines someone serving humanity as a physician. Uncontrolled sympathetic suffering could undermine judgment and the steadiness needed to help. The image makes his practical concern clear: another person's need may require capacities that our distress can interfere with. It is an illustration of the argument, not medical guidance.

Why moral approval can make a feeling harder to question

Section 134 also asks how pity acquires a good conscience. When a community praises it, people feel entitled to cultivate and acknowledge it. When it is judged dangerous, they regard it as weakness or seek ways to limit it.

This reconnects with the question of learning to feel differently in Reading 1. A feeling's moral reputation can determine whether we indulge, resist, or confess it. Feeling good about having a response is therefore another thing to examine, alongside the response itself.

The inherited reputation is not proof of benefit. But discovering its history does not prove the opposite either. We still need to ask what the feeling enables, what it obstructs, and whose situation improves or worsens.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does Nietzsche adequately distinguish being moved by suffering from being overwhelmed by it?

His examples reveal why intense distress can interfere with useful help. They do not establish that every compassionate response becomes incapacitating. A manageable response might draw attention to something that would otherwise be ignored and sustain an effort to address it.

Section 134's restricted viewpoint creates a further problem. If we consider only the suffering a feeling produces, then announce that it is harmful overall, we have left out the benefits needed for that assessment. Nietzsche acknowledges such benefits but does not demonstrate that they are insignificant.

There is a parallel difficulty in §133. Relief following assistance might be a result of helping rather than its governing purpose. Likewise, the fact that my feeling occurs in me does not settle whether I care only about myself. An account of motive must distinguish these possibilities.

These objections preserve the reading's central distinctions. Understanding someone, suffering sympathetically, wanting to help, and helping effectively can come apart. We can examine their connections without either praising every response called compassionate or condemning assistance because its motives include personal satisfaction.

Ask what evidence would establish the stronger claims: how often does shared distress obstruct help, compared with motivating it? Under what conditions? The assigned passages pose important possibilities; they do not provide that comparative evidence.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does unconscious action matter in §133?

A person may respond without consciously considering themselves while personal concerns still shape the response. Nietzsche's balance-recovery analogy illustrates that possibility. It does not require a hidden, deliberate calculation before every helpful action.

Does enjoying successful assistance discredit it?

No. Pleasure can accompany or motivate useful help. We need to examine how a particular motive affects attention, conduct, and results. Discovering an interest does not by itself determine the action's worth.

Why separate the observer's distress from the sufferer's?

They may have different contents and causes. My helplessness need not be what troubles you. Nietzsche uses this distinction against the claim that pity gives immediate access to another's experience. Feeling intensely does not guarantee understanding accurately.

Does §133 simply praise people without pity?

No. Nietzsche also connects their response with pride, habits, and investments in an image of themselves. He treats compassionate and unpitying people as different kinds of egoists while questioning the moral ranking that makes one group automatically good.

What remains unproved in §134?

Nietzsche does not establish that pity's helpful effects are generally insignificant or that sympathetic suffering invariably incapacitates people. His focus on added suffering identifies a possible cost; assessing the response overall requires examining benefits and different circumstances too.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose an ordinary, real or imagined occasion for helping someone. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Distinguish the person's difficulty from what the observer feels about it.
  2. Identify two possible motives for helping, including one personal satisfaction. Mark your explanations as hypotheses where necessary.
  3. Explain how the emotional response could either support or obstruct useful assistance.
  4. State what the helper would need to learn from the person affected before deciding what to do.
  5. Use one point from each aphorism, then identify a claim whose strength exceeds the evidence your example supplies.

A successful response evaluates what the help does without assuming that a mixed motive either vindicates or ruins it.
