What Remains After Belief?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 3 of 10

Read: Book I, §76, then Book II, §132. Read each in full, stopping before §77 and §133 respectively.
Allow: About 55–70 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §76 · §132 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Someone rejects the belief that a good person must always sacrifice their own wishes. Yet they still feel ashamed whenever they choose something for themselves. They praise other people most readily for being useful, undemanding, and willing to go without.

An explicit belief has lost its authority, while a pattern of feeling and admiration continues. The person may even demand greater sacrifice to demonstrate that they remain good.

This example is ours. It brings together two movements in the assigned passages: the suffering produced by guilty interpretations of ordinary feelings, and the survival of moral expectations beyond the beliefs that helped sustain them.

Reading 2 examined how custom can shape sincere conscience. Here Nietzsche investigates particular Christian inheritances. His inquiry concerns what a judgment does to our experience and what becomes of its authority when religious doctrine recedes.

The main question: What must change for the loss of a belief to become more than a change in what we say we believe?

Read Book I, §76 and Book II, §132 in full. Keep the criticism of sexual guilt distinct from the later account of morality organized around service to others.

Three things to notice

  1. Restraint and condemnation. In §76, why does needing to regulate a feeling not establish that the feeling itself is evil?
  2. The reversal produced by suppression. Follow the passage from secret misery to exaggerated public fascination with love stories.
  3. A changing inheritance. In §132, notice how personal salvation gives way to social benevolence, then how benevolence becomes a demand that individuals serve a collective whole.

A way to read the passages

A judgment can make ordinary feelings sources of misery

Section 76 begins with a claim about what condemnation produces. Nietzsche argues that interpreting passions through a hostile moral gaze can make them malign. His central case is Christianity’s transformation of sexual feeling into an occasion for bad conscience. Book I, §76

The names Eros and Aphrodite evoke powers of desire capable of elevated representation. Nietzsche contrasts that possibility with a religious interpretation that makes their recurrence frightening and shameful. His subject is not simply a prohibition of particular conduct; it is the inward suffering attached to having the impulse.

Ordinary sensations return. If their return repeatedly triggers guilty self-condemnation, suffering also becomes regular. Nietzsche locates a mechanism here: the moral interpretation helps make an unavoidable feature of embodied life into a recurring accusation against the person.

Secrecy deepens the trouble. A person who cannot speak about the feeling loses opportunities to find that others share it or to examine its supposed meaning. Nietzsche invokes Shakespeare’s sonnets as an unusual public expression of what he interprets as Christian gloom about sexuality.

Needing a limit does not prove a feeling evil

The next questions separate regulation from demonization. Nietzsche allows that a feeling may require restraint, limitation, or, in some circumstances, complete exclusion from one’s mind. He disputes the inference that whatever must be resisted is therefore evil.

Consider anger during a difficult conversation. You may need to pause before speaking because an immediate response would injure someone. That does not establish that the bare occurrence of anger proves a corrupt nature. Assessing what to do and condemning the capacity to feel are different acts.

The anger example is ours; §76’s principal subject remains sexual desire. The distinction matters there because rejecting guilty condemnation does not itself settle which conduct is acceptable. Another person’s wishes and the effects of an action still require attention.

Nietzsche then emphasizes a possibility within sexual relations: one person’s enjoyment can give another person enjoyment. He explicitly includes pity and adoration among feelings with this characteristic. Remember that positive association when we later examine his criticism of pity.

He neither claims that every encounter is mutually gratifying nor establishes that pleasure alone justifies conduct. His immediate target is the bad conscience that corrupts a possibility of shared delight before the circumstances have been examined.

The forbidden object becomes disproportionately fascinating

The final part of §76 turns bitterly comic. According to Nietzsche, demonizing desire has not made it unimportant. Religious mystery surrounding erotic life has helped make love stories a fascination shared across social classes.

He describes a culture in which imaginative work and thinking repeatedly return to the love story as the principal matter of existence. Condemnation and obsession can support one another: a forbidden object attracts the attention that the prohibition appears to deny it.

This changes the scope of the criticism. Bad conscience injures the individual’s experience, while the organization of attention narrows a culture. Removing a formal belief may leave people captivated by the same objects and inherited patterns of significance.

Nietzsche imagines future generations laughing at this disproportion and judging the culture that produced it. That anticipated verdict is a rhetorical challenge, not evidence from the future. The passage also gives no sufficient historical demonstration that Christianity caused the cultural pattern he describes.

The hypothesis is nevertheless sharper than a simple complaint about repression. A judgment can strengthen a passion’s cultural prominence even while teaching people to distrust the passion itself.

What modern morality treats as obvious

Section 132 opens with the contemporary assumption that goodness must contain pity. Nietzsche then broadens the picture: the moral person is identified through social, sympathetic, disinterested, and benevolent conduct. He asks how this identification became so widely accepted. Book II, §132

His question concerns the authority of a standard. Why should service to others define the moral person? Which other forms of human activity become subordinate or suspicious when that definition seems self-evident?

Recall §103 from Reading 1. Nietzsche can question the beliefs governing sincere benevolence without claiming that every kind act secretly seeks personal gain. Section 132 investigates a morality’s development and social direction. It does not need to expose each helpful person as a hypocrite.

The historical thesis is that this moral identification may be Christianity’s most extensive transformation of Europe, perhaps contrary to its own central teaching. That surprising qualification drives the account. Nietzsche describes an inheritance that changes its priorities as its doctrinal support weakens.

Personal salvation recedes; benevolence moves forward

Nietzsche presents the pursuit of eternal personal salvation as a thoroughly self-concerned priority. Love of one’s neighbor and charitable practice initially occupy a supporting place beside it. As belief in salvation and its accompanying dogmas recedes, the supporting commitments become more prominent.

This is not a simple story in which Christianity always meant selflessness and secular people merely keep the same instruction. The former center loses authority. Features of the tradition acquire a different position within the surviving moral life.

Nietzsche adds a motive for intensification. People who separate from Christian doctrine can seek moral justification for leaving by displaying exceptional love of humanity. They wish to equal or exceed the benevolence associated with the religion they reject.

He places French free thinkers from Voltaire to Auguste Comte in this story. Comte’s injunction to live for others becomes, for Nietzsche, an especially concentrated expression of the tendency. A critic of dogma can make Christian self-denial more absolute than it had previously been.

The claims are sweeping. Nietzsche offers a proposed history and an interpretation of these thinkers’ motivations, not a demonstrated account of every intellectual departure from religion. The possibility he identifies deserves investigation without being turned into a universal diagnosis of unbelievers.

Different doctrines can converge on one demand

Nietzsche next brings Schopenhauer and John Stuart Mill into the picture through pity and usefulness to others. His grouping does not make their philosophies identical. It identifies what he regards as a convergence in the standard used to praise an action.

The closing parenthesis makes the distinction clearer by treating Kant as an exception. In Nietzsche’s pointed sketch, Kant separates moral worth from sympathetic feeling, while Schopenhauer attacks that separation. Read this as Nietzsche’s positioning of the dispute, not a complete exposition of either philosopher.

The main argument moves toward social organization. Nietzsche links the moral prominence of sympathetic feelings with projects that adapt individuals to the requirements of a collective whole. He includes socialistic principles within the trend but describes a wider agreement than any single political doctrine.

The proposed whole could be an existing state, a future state, a nation, an international association, or a small economic community. Disagreement about the institution can coexist with agreement about the individual’s task: become useful to the whole.

Pause here: Explain why the disappearance of a religious doctrine need not weaken a moral demand descended from it. Identify one way Nietzsche thinks the demand can instead grow stronger.

When a person becomes an instrument of the whole

Nietzsche’s criticism now becomes uncompromising. He sees the social demand aiming at the individual’s transformation, weakening, and suppression. Personal life is judged wasteful, expensive, dangerous, or luxurious when it resists convenient administration.

The imagined advantages are cheaper, safer, more uniform, and more harmonious social arrangements. The individual regains a defined sphere of rights and duties through adaptation to the whole, but has become something different in the process.

Imagine an organization that praises members only for measurable contributions to shared targets. A person’s independent project becomes suspect because it takes time, creates uncertainty, and has no immediate use for everyone else. The organization need not be malicious for its standard to narrow the life it permits.

This example is ours. It illustrates how social usefulness can become a comprehensive measure of worth. It does not establish that all cooperative organizations suppress individuality or that collective needs are unreal.

The passage’s force comes from asking what the attractive language of benevolence authorizes. When goodness means self-denial for the whole, disagreement can become evidence of moral failure before anyone considers the value of what the individual wants to do.

Two forms of moral survival

The passages now illuminate one another. Section 76 follows a judgment into bodily feeling, secrecy, and cultural fascination. Section 132 follows a religious inheritance into a morality that can continue—and intensify—among critics of its dogmas.

In both, changing a declared belief does not settle what happens to attention, conscience, or admiration. These patterns have acquired forms of their own. A person can reject an authority while still experiencing its categories as obvious.

Nietzsche does not establish that every moral practice depends on Christianity, or that every surviving practice must disappear. He makes the demand from §103 concrete: learning anew must reach what we condemn, what we praise, and why those judgments carry force.

A difficulty to keep in view

Could a demand for cooperation protect individuality rather than suppress it?

People require one another’s help to acquire and exercise capacities. Shared work can provide time, education, care, and security for individual projects. Asking someone to contribute need not reduce them to an instrument; the contribution might sustain conditions of independence for several people.

Nietzsche’s sweeping account of social morality therefore needs distinctions. Which demands impose uniformity? Which make room for different lives? Who benefits, who bears the cost, and how can a participant question the arrangement?

This objection does not erase his criticism. Cooperation becomes vulnerable to it when collective usefulness claims complete authority over a person’s worth, or when self-sacrifice counts as good regardless of what is being sacrificed and why.

The historical question remains separate. Showing that benevolence developed through Christian practices would not prove it worthless. If an inherited standard is to be retained, however, it needs reasons beyond the claim that everyone already recognizes it as morality.

Section 76 requires the same care. An inherited condemnation can be harmful without every limitation being oppressive. The task is to explain particular constraints through their grounds and effects, while refusing to make the mere occurrence of a feeling an automatic accusation.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What is §76’s central criticism of Christian sexual judgment?

Nietzsche argues that bad conscience makes regularly recurring sensations into regularly recurring sources of inward misery. Secrecy can deepen that suffering. His target includes the interpretation of the impulse itself, not only prohibitions of particular conduct.

Why does restraint not establish that a feeling is evil?

A feeling may need regulation because of a situation and its possible consequences. That gives a reason to assess conduct, not automatically to condemn the capacity for the feeling. Nietzsche explicitly permits limits while disputing demonization.

How does §132 describe Christianity’s changing inheritance?

As faith in personal salvation and its dogmas recedes, love of humanity and charitable expectations become more prominent. Critics can seek justification for their departure by exceeding Christian benevolence, strengthening a surviving demand for self-denial.

Does Nietzsche claim that every kind act conceals selfishness?

No. Here he examines the history and authority of a standard identifying goodness with service to others. His argument concerns sincere moral expectations too. The distinction between motivation and justification from §103 remains essential.

What worries Nietzsche about different collective projects?

They can disagree about the desired institution while sharing a demand that individuals become useful instruments of the whole. He fears the suppression of individuality under ideals of economical, safe, uniform social organization.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to the rule and feeling you examined in Readings 1 and 2. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Identify a belief you have questioned and one judgment or feeling that may have survived it.
  2. Use §76’s distinction between regulating a feeling and condemning it. An ordinary, non-intimate example is sufficient.
  3. Use §132 to examine whether your rule makes usefulness to others a complete measure of goodness.
  4. Give one reason for a limit or cooperative demand you would retain, and one reason to question its scope.
  5. Identify what you would need to observe before accepting your explanation of yourself.

A successful response does not invent a religious history for your rule. It distinguishes what the passages help you ask from what your evidence establishes.
