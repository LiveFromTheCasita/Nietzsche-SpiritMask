Can Art Overwhelm Our Judgment?

Essential Reading Path · Reading 9 of 10

Read: A continuous selection from §7 of Richard Wagner in Bayreuth, followed by §11 in full
Allow: About 45–60 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Open the selection in Richard Wagner in Bayreuth, §7

Then open Richard Wagner in Bayreuth, §11

The free text uses Anthony M. Ludovici’s translation in Thoughts Out of Season, Part I. In Richard Wagner in Bayreuth VII, use the page 150 link and find “However the development of the born dramatist may be pictured”. Read continuously through “how much more keenly must the creator realise this contrast!”, shortly after the page 153 marker. Stop before “There he stands amid all the clamorous appeals”. This selection is about 840 words. We are leaving aside the earlier discussion of Wagner’s development and the remaining account of the creator’s experience.

Then jump to XI, near the end of the essay. The page 198 link lands in the closing discussion of §10; continue to XI, beginning “May blessed reason preserve us”. Read the entire section through the essay’s final words, “but the interpreter and clarifier of the past.” We are passing over the intervening sections, not reading continuously from VII to XI.

In another edition, use Untimely Meditations IV §§7 and 11; wording will vary. The site’s preferred translation is R. J. Hollingdale’s, edited by Daniel Breazeale. The opera descriptions in §11 provide enough context for this lesson; listening to the works is optional.

Before you read

During a film, a character’s choice seems inevitable. The music gathers, the camera stays close, and you want them to take the risk. After the credits, you begin asking questions you did not ask in the scene. Why accept that sacrifice? What happened to the people the story left outside its frame?

The experience was powerful. Your later questions need not make it worthless. They do make the relation between being carried away and making a judgment worth examining.

In Reading 8, Nietzsche denied that tragic heroes give practical instructions. Now he describes an artist whose power can suspend the spectator’s familiar standards. He welcomes this unsettling experience because he hopes it can loosen the authority of a culture he considers damaged.

The main question: Can surrender to an artwork help us judge more freely afterward, and how would we know?

These passages belong to Nietzsche’s supportive 1876 essay on Wagner. Read the hope and the danger together before deciding what you think. His later criticism of Wagner does not settle the argument made here.

What to notice

  1. The spectator’s missing standard. What happens when music, drama, and visible action work together? Why can the loss of familiar bearings feel frightening and welcome?
  2. The journey back. Nietzsche describes renewed sympathy and security, then warns about taking life too easily. What distinguishes a changed relationship to life from preferring the performance to life?
  3. The audience that does not yet exist. Why does §11 deny that the future will be perfectly happy or good? What evidence does Nietzsche use to imagine its people?

Close reading

A whole experience can unsettle our standards

The selection begins with Wagner joining the arts. Sound, visible action, and dramatic expression communicate together, producing an experience no explanation of the parts can replace. Nietzsche then describes a spectator who loses familiar bearings: fixed things tremble and acquire new meanings. §7, the gathered arts and their effect

This includes a power that “cancels both resistance and reason”. We should let the severity register. The artist’s success reaches the capacities by which an audience might assess what it encounters. Nietzsche admires a transformative power while describing something a critic could find alarming.

Being unsettled may make a rigid assumption available for reconsideration. It does not itself prove that the artist’s alternative is sound. That distinction is our question to the passage, rather than an answer Nietzsche fully supplies here.

Why welcome an artist whom Plato would send away?

Nietzsche invokes Plato to imagine honoring the extraordinary imitator and nevertheless asking him to leave the community. The artist can make an experience compelling enough to disturb an established order. The same power that invites admiration invites concern about its authority. §7, Plato and the charmer

Nietzsche’s response depends on the condition of his own society. Because he judges its prevailing reason and power harmful, he wants the charmer to unsettle them. A society able to do without such an artist remains an audacious possibility, not an achievement he believes the present can claim.

His defense is therefore specific: our need for art arises within conditions he wants changed. This gives us two things to evaluate—the diagnosis of those conditions and the claim that artistic disruption can improve our relation to them.

The return is part of the experience’s value

Nietzsche leads his spectators up to an extraordinary height of feeling and then back to familiar life. From that height, human struggles and defeats acquire a significance they lacked under ordinary pressure. He imagines more benevolent relations with others and renewed security in the face of danger. §7, the return to life

Then he admits a risk: we may take life too easily because we were so serious within art. The performance can dwarf ordinary demands instead of preparing us to face them. The warning belongs to this enthusiastic essay itself.

Nietzsche describes a return of comfort and sympathy; he does not explicitly describe the recovery of critical judgment. We can use that gap to distinguish surrender during a performance from the judgment we exercise afterward. Temporarily allowing a work to reshape attention can be valuable; deciding what deserves lasting authority is another task. Ask what remains after the intensity fades: more attentive relationships, a newly questionable assumption, or impatience with everything outside the work? §7, the danger and the contrast with waking life

A future audience would still face suffering and evil

§11 immediately rejects a final ideal order of permanent happiness. Nietzsche’s hope concerns people whose present barriers to art have changed. Their lives would still contain suffering, conflict, and the possibility of evil. A future that needs tragedy has not outgrown the terrible. §11, the rejection of a golden age

He even imagines this generation seeming more evil because its passions would be more direct. He prizes freedom and honesty over hypocritical obedience, and insists that people must become free through themselves. Those priorities help explain the break with conventional approval. They do not demonstrate that straightforward wrongdoing is harmless or admirable.

Nor does his opposition between nature and what is contrary to nature settle the issue. He describes the natural as seeking transformation through love. The label carries a valuation that the reader still has to assess. This future is Nietzsche’s challenging hope, with its own unresolved moral questions. §11, freedom, nature, and transformation

Wotan’s loss of power and the problem of prediction

Nietzsche turns to Wagner’s plots to make the hoped-for transformation visible. His longest example follows Wotan in the Ring: the god seeks power, becomes entangled in obligations, and cannot bring about the freedom he desires. He eventually finds joy in the triumph and suffering of the hero who breaks his power. Love frees him from his attachment to himself. §11, Wotan’s bondage and the free hero

Recall Reading 8: we need not copy a character’s decisions to investigate the change a drama presents. Here the question is whether losing control can become an enlargement of feeling. Nietzsche asks where his contemporaries show a comparable capacity to grow through retreat. Their apparent absence turns his gaze toward a future audience. §11, the questions addressed to the present

The final sentence makes the relation to time especially strange. To us, Wagner appears to anticipate people not yet present. To those imagined people, his work would clarify a past they could recognize as their own. Art’s present promise would become an account of a transformation they had lived.

That is a conditional vision. The vividness of a work does not establish that the audience Nietzsche imagines will emerge. His closing question asks us to consider a possibility; our response can examine the grounds of his confidence. §11, the future audience and its past

A difficulty

Can the artwork supply all the evidence for the audience it promises?

Nietzsche reads Wagner’s dramas as signs of a freer future, then appeals to that future to explain their importance. There is a risk of a circle: the imagined audience validates the art, while the art is what lets him imagine the audience.

The argument need not collapse into that circle. A work might disclose capacities people already possess imperfectly or make a change conceivable enough to attempt. But we would still need evidence beyond our absorption in it: ways people act, judgments they can defend, relationships they can sustain.

Test the hope on its own terms. What change outside the performance would count in its favor? What would make you revise it? Later disagreements between Nietzsche and Wagner cannot answer those questions in our place.

Check your understanding

Why does Nietzsche welcome the artist’s unsettling power?

He believes the present society’s standards and uses of power need to be challenged. The dramatic artist can disturb their apparent necessity. This defense relies on a diagnosis of existing culture; the strength of an artistic effect alone does not establish that its direction is good.

What danger appears before the end of the selection from §7?

We may take ordinary life too easily because the artistic experience seems more serious and real. That would compromise the hoped-for return to life with greater sympathy and strength. The passage therefore contains a way to question what happens after enchantment.

Why is the future audience in §11 different from a utopia?

Nietzsche denies permanent happiness and moral perfection. He imagines people with more direct passions and greater freedom who still need art amid suffering. The hope is for a changed relationship to existence, not the elimination of conflict, and its fulfillment remains uncertain.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering: What would show that an artwork had strengthened your judgment after temporarily overwhelming it?

Use one detail from §7 to describe what happens during or after a performance. Then use either Wotan’s change or the imagined audience in §11 to identify a possibility the artwork makes visible. Give one observable sign that this possibility is taking shape in life, and one sign that the experience has become a substitute for engaging with life.

A successful response preserves the difference between being deeply moved, seeing a new possibility, and having grounds to trust it. Keep that distinction for the final session, when we return to history and the task of making knowledge serve a life.
