What Does Relief Cost the Sufferer?

On the Genealogy of Morals — Essential Reading Path · Reading 11 of 12

Read: Essay III §§19–23. Stop before §24.
Allow: About 105–135 minutes, including the assigned text, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. You can divide the lesson across two sittings.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternative: Essay III §§19–23 (Horace B. Samuel).

Before you read

A person feels trapped in an exhausting, uneventful distress. Then an accusation gives the distress a dramatic explanation: a failure in their own character has caused everything. Searching their past becomes urgent. Fear and hope replace dullness, and the person feels that something decisive is finally happening.

This is our imagined case. The new experience may be intensely painful while also offering relief from the previous condition. That possibility makes it difficult to judge the intervention simply by asking whether the person feels better or worse at one moment.

Reading 10 distinguished preserving life, relieving suffering, and healing its cause. Nietzsche now examines a more dangerous form of relief: emotional excess organized through guilt and sin. He credits its power and insists that the priest may sincerely believe in it, while accusing it of causing profound damage.

The main question: How can an interpretation relieve one form of suffering by producing another, and what would justify calling the result an improvement?

Read Essay III, §§19–23. The final section changes the question: after the criticisms of the priest's effects, why has the ideal retained such power, and does modern science actually oppose it?

Three things to notice

  1. The terms of evaluation. Nietzsche calls methods innocent or guilty according to modern standards. Notice what consequences motivate that distinction and why it need not describe the priest's intentions.
  2. The interpretation of pain. In §20, trace the change from an unexplained condition to suffering understood as punishment. What does the new account make the person notice and want?
  3. The missing opponent. In §23, distinguish useful scientific work from an alternative ideal. Why might scholarship itself resemble a method of deadening distress?

A way to read the passages

§19: changing the kind of relief

Nietzsche first recaps the methods already examined: reducing vitality, mechanical activity, small pleasures, and belonging to a community. He calls these innocent by modern standards before turning to methods he labels guilty. The quotation-like status of those labels matters: he is borrowing a contrast while investigating how moral language shapes judgment. Return to Essay III, §19

The new methods deliberately produce emotional excess. Instead of making consciousness quieter or filling it with regular work, they arouse an intensity that overwhelms a persistent, dull distress. Nietzsche treats that intensity as a powerful anesthetic against the earlier pain.

It can sound paradoxical to call agitation an anesthetic. Imagine someone becoming absorbed in an angry dispute and temporarily ceasing to notice a different worry. That ordinary example illustrates a displacement of attention. It does not establish Nietzsche's larger physiological theory or justify provoking the conflict.

These comparative labels do not make the earlier methods harmless. Reading 10 encountered impaired activity and compliant routine among their possible costs.

Why the section detours through intellectual honesty

Nietzsche considers describing emotional excess in more pleasing terms, then refuses. He argues that respectable language can conceal what a practice does. Calling an intervention enthusiasm rather than an overwhelming stimulation would protect its attractive appearance before the effects had been examined.

His attack turns toward people who sincerely regard themselves as good. They may be unable to notice their own distortions precisely because dishonesty seems incompatible with their identity. The target includes innocent self-deception, not only conscious concealment.

The biographical examples concern reputations protected through destruction, omission, or restraint in writing. Nietzsche recounts what he takes to be evidence of an unwillingness to expose admired lives. The examples do not by themselves establish his general verdict about modern people, and some are presented as reports rather than demonstrated cases.

His own prose also loads the judgment. He associates evasive idealism with effeminacy and a feminized culture. Those gendered associations do no evidential work; their contempt should be examined alongside his demand to strip moral decoration from description.

The opening of §20 prevents the critic from claiming an easy exemption. Nietzsche says that he and his fellow psychologists may remain too attached to goodness for their task. Their first impulses require suspicion too. The demand for honesty therefore reaches the person offering the diagnosis, even when his confidence elsewhere outruns it.

§20: a painful intensity can defeat dull pain

Nietzsche names a range of emotions the priest can mobilize: rage, fear, desire, revenge, hope, triumph, despair, and cruelty. Their common function is to interrupt prolonged melancholy through a sudden and powerful discharge. Religious interpretation authorizes the experience and explains its significance. Return to Essay III, §20

This makes the distinction from ordinary consolation precise. The person need not become calm or comfortable. They can become terrified, ecstatic, or urgently preoccupied. What disappears for a time is the earlier undifferentiated heaviness.

Nietzsche insists that the excess carries a later cost: the sick become sicker. This is why the treatment earns the guilty label under the standards he invokes. The criticism depends on what happens to the sufferer beyond the immediate change of feeling.

Yet he immediately demands fairness toward the priest. The treatment can be prescribed with complete conviction that it is necessary and beneficial. The priest may himself nearly collapse before the pain he creates. Sincere intention and injurious consequences coexist within Nietzsche's account.

The intended immediate effect may also occur. A method aimed at suppressing felt depression can achieve that end while damaging the person more broadly. Calling the priest simply unsuccessful misses the distinction on which the criticism depends.

Guilt becomes the organizing explanation

The priest's principal instrument is the feeling of guilt. Nietzsche explicitly returns to Essay II's explanation of inward cruelty, then distinguishes that existing condition from its priestly interpretation as sin. The priest gives a new meaning to a capacity he did not originally create.

The sufferer asks why they feel this way. The priest directs the search toward the sufferer's own guilt, perhaps an episode in the past, and explains the suffering as punishment. Pain is now evidence within a moral and religious story.

This is the development of Reading 10's redirected resentment. Naming oneself as the responsible party becomes a continuing way to examine one's life. The person looks backward for offenses, interprets conduct suspiciously, fears retribution, and seeks redemption.

Nietzsche compares the resulting confinement to an animal held inside a drawn boundary. The explanation narrows the field of possible causes: whatever happens, the gaze returns to guilt. A powerful account can become difficult to escape because it supplies the terms in which every new experience is interpreted.

For an invented analogy, someone may read every setback as proof of insufficient devotion to a demanding ideal. Increased effort produces exhaustion, and exhaustion becomes further evidence that their devotion is defective. This illustrates how an interpretation could renew itself through its own effects; it is not an account of every religious commitment.

The patient begins to desire the treatment's pain

Nietzsche's description now intensifies. Contrition, fear, punishment, and moments of ecstatic relief make life urgent again. The exhausted person can become sleepless and consumed without returning to the previous dullness. The contrast is between kinds of suffering, not simply suffering and its absence.

He presents the resulting desire for more pain as the priest's triumph. Pain acquires a role in overcoming the earlier misery and pursuing salvation. The person can seek what injures them because the injury now belongs to a meaningful and absorbing undertaking.

That claim should not be softened into a warning that guilt sometimes feels unpleasant. Nietzsche argues that an entire machinery of interpretation and emotion can make additional suffering desirable. It renews engagement while organizing that engagement against the sufferer.

The priest's kingdom is consequently visible within this world, whatever otherworldly language expresses its purpose. The closing question about that kingdom exposes the contrast between a declared destination and an accomplished earthly power.

§21: what does being improved mean?

The next section disputes the standard of success. Nietzsche accepts that the treatment may have reformed people, then redescribes reform as taming, weakening, discouraging, and refining them. A person more manageable under a moral standard may be more damaged under the standard he uses. Return to Essay III, §21

His vocabulary makes that standard contestable. The language of emasculation links diminished strength with a gendered ideal. We should not quietly translate it into a neutral measure of well-being. What he prizes and what he regards as injury both need examination.

He makes sweeping causal claims about shattered nervous systems, collective outbreaks of distress, penitential movements, and religious ecstasies. The historical examples range across places and periods. Their accumulation gives the argument force, but the section supplies no method for distinguishing the influence of religious practices from other conditions.

These are Nietzsche's historical and physiological claims, not established diagnoses of the people described. His comparison with the spread of alcohol through German influence adds racial and national explanations to the argument. Its confidence exceeds the evidence presented.

What changes when a practice is called useful? Compliance cannot silently stand in for health, and relief at one moment cannot settle the effect over time. An assessment must identify the outcome it values.

§22: the judgment moves from health to taste

Nietzsche next claims that damaging the soul's health damages artistic and literary taste. He acknowledges that the inference needs support, then explicitly declines to provide a proof. Instead he offers a provocative comparison of Christian literature with the ancient writing it displaced or disparaged. Return to Essay III, §22

The contrast between the Old and New Testaments organizes his response. He honors the former for powerful figures, an heroic landscape, and the presence of a people. He attacks the latter as the writing of small sectarian worlds whose private troubles claim cosmic importance.

His objection therefore concerns scale and self-estimation as well as style. Ordinary personal distress becomes something God and eternity must attend to. For Nietzsche, the disproportion expresses the same emotionally inflated world the priestly treatment produces.

But his own alternative relies on rank. References to poor breeding, provincial people, and Luther's peasant manners turn social distinctions into standards of spiritual and literary worth. The passage also uses a derogatory characterization of Jewish religious intimacy. Praise of the Old Testament does not remove that judgment or establish the fairness of his contrasts.

The claims belong to the argument rather than to incidental bad manners we can subtract without consequence. If impressive scale or aristocratic restraint establishes superior taste, we must ask why. Personal suffering does not become aesthetically or ethically insignificant merely because its bearer lacks the stature Nietzsche admires.

Measure, intimacy, and the critic's own style

The comparison with restrained worship in East Asia introduces a preference for distance and silence. Luther's direct address to God appears, by contrast, as intrusive familiarity. Nietzsche briefly values priestly etiquette against the reformer's manner even while attacking the ascetic ideal.

This complication prevents the chapter from becoming a simple opposition between religion and unbelief. Different religious forms receive different judgments. The closing target is the ideal's lack of measure, which can undermine even the manners a priestly tradition cultivates.

Nietzsche declares independence from two thousand years of literary approval and answers excess with exuberant denunciation. Does his style disclose the disproportion he identifies, or substitute the force of his disgust for the missing argument?

§23: why has no rival displaced the ideal?

Nietzsche now says that cataloguing damage was never his final task. He wants the meaning and basis of the ideal's extraordinary power. What permits it to organize so much life, and where is a will strong enough to offer an alternative? Return to Essay III, §23

His answer begins with the ideal's unity. It interprets peoples, periods, activities, and individuals through one governing aim. Other interests acquire their significance and permission to exist through their place within its undertaking. The power lies in an encompassing account, not merely a collection of prohibitions.

Nietzsche then introduces an objection: modern science has already overcome this ideal through independence from God and another world. Keep the proposed answer distinct from his response. He refuses to accept those outward differences as proof of a competing foundation.

Where science remains passionately committed, he calls it the ideal's latest and noblest form. At this point he asserts that connection; the following sections still need to explain why the commitment to truth continues the ideal. He also describes scientific work as a refuge in which someone avoids confronting a lack of purpose. These are different explanations: a commitment may inherit an ideal, while continuous activity may conceal the absence of one.

He does not deny that researchers do useful work or find real satisfaction in it. He explicitly values their work. But usefulness and contented workers do not demonstrate that science supplies its own overarching aim. Reading 12 will examine the valuation that allows the pursuit of truth to claim authority.

Scholarship can repeat a method already described

The final portrayal returns to incessant labor, now in learned life. Mastery of a specialized task may occupy someone so fully that an unwelcome question remains unseen. Nietzsche calls this self-anesthesia.

The connection with mechanical activity in §18 is important. Leaving priestly institutions does not necessarily remove the patterns of relief they used. The critic, scholar, or student can organize a life around avoiding a question while regarding continuous work as evidence of intellectual courage.

Intensity of work does not itself disclose concealed distress. This is a hypothesis about scholars' motives, not evidence about a particular researcher.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can Nietzsche distinguish injury from transformation without simply imposing his preferred kind of person?

The passages make a strong case for asking what an improvement changes, who defines it, and what its effects cost. They also classify people through health, strength, breeding, and taste without supplying stable measures. His diagnosis can inherit the very danger he finds in the priest: an encompassing interpretation that judges every case through its own preferred standard.

This objection does not make all standards equally defensible. We can compare changes in a person's capacities, continuing distress, dependence, and ability to question the interpretation. These are possible grounds for assessment, not a replacement doctrine Nietzsche establishes here.

Section 20 gives an additional discipline: do not infer malicious intention from a damaging result. Nor should sincere intentions prevent us from recognizing damage. The combination makes responsibility more demanding than assigning a simple villain.

Finally, §23 turns the inquiry toward us. Reading a revealing explanation can be satisfying and useful without establishing the value of every commitment it encourages. The next question concerns the standard under which truth itself becomes an overriding aim.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Does calling a method guilty mean that the priest knowingly intends harm?

No. The label concerns its damaging effects under the standards Nietzsche invokes. Section 20 expressly allows sincere conviction in the method's necessity, even anguish at its consequences. Good conscience does not guarantee a beneficial result.

How does the interpretation as sin change suffering?

It directs the sufferer toward personal guilt and treats distress as punishment. An existing capacity for inward cruelty gains a religious meaning. The interpretation organizes attention, emotion, and the search for redemption; it is not the initial origin of bad conscience.

How can the treatment succeed while making someone worse?

Intense emotion can interrupt dull distress while producing further injury. Likewise, moral reform may mean greater compliance rather than greater vitality. The judgment depends on distinguishing the particular outcome achieved from the broader condition of the person.

What remains unproved in §22's account of taste?

Nietzsche declines to demonstrate the inference from impaired spiritual health to corrupted taste. His scriptural comparisons express contested standards of scale, restraint, and rank, alongside class and ethnic judgments. Strong distaste does not establish their authority.

Does §23 accept science as the ideal's victorious opponent?

No. Nietzsche rejects that proposed answer. He distinguishes valuable scientific work from an independent governing ideal, calls passionate science a continuation of ascetic commitment, and also examines work as self-anesthesia. The grounds of truth's authority become the next problem.

What should I write in my notes?

Use an ordinary or invented case in which an explanation makes distress more manageable while possibly increasing dependence or self-accusation. Write 200–300 words:

  1. Describe the original difficulty and the change the explanation produces.
  2. Use §20 to distinguish the interpreter's intention, the immediate effect, and a possible later cost.
  3. Specify what calling the result an improvement would mean. Identify evidence needed for that judgment.
  4. State one limit in applying Nietzsche's account to the case.
  5. Use §23 to ask whether your own investigation pursues a question or could also help you avoid one.

A strong response examines real effects without treating suffering as proof of guilt, or confident diagnosis as proof of knowledge.

Reading 12: Can the Will to Truth Question Itself? →

The final session reads Essay III, §§24–28. Nietzsche examines the commitments of supposedly free thinkers and asks whether the will to truth can question the value it grants truth.

Carry this question forward: If rejecting a religious explanation leaves our governing commitment intact, what would it mean to investigate that commitment's value rather than only the truths it helps us discover?
