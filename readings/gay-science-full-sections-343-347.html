We Fearless Ones: The Open Sea and the Will to Truth

Optional full-text route · Station 26 of 35 (proposed)

Before you read

This station begins the 1887 addition. The book you have read so far ended in 1882 with §342. Five years later Nietzsche published an expanded edition. It added Book V, “We Fearless Ones” (§§343–383), an appendix of fourteen songs, a title-page motto, and a preface dated autumn 1886. Book V was written after Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–85) and Beyond Good and Evil (1886), in the same period as On the Genealogy of Morality (1887). Read it as a later author returning to his book, not as the voice that wrote Books I–IV.

§§343–344 are the assignments of Reading 8, which reads them closely. This station reads them as Book V’s opening and gives most of its time to §§345–347.

The main question: What new horizon opens in 1887—and what still authorizes truth?

Keep using the marks: P, H, V, and ? for Nietzsche’s own hedge only. In Book V, also note when a section looks back at the 1882 book.

What to notice

  1. The report. §343 hears “the report that the ‘old God is dead’” as a new dawn. Compare the madman’s audience in §125 (station 14).
  2. The sea again. §343 ends with “the sea, our sea,” lying open. Compare §124 (station 14) and §289 (station 20).
  3. Belief. §344 finds a belief underneath science; §347 makes the need of belief a measure of weakness. Keep both claims in view.

The epigraph and the news

Book V opens with a French epigraph attributed to Turenne: “Carcasse, tu trembles? Tu tremblerais bien davantage, si tu savais, où je te mène.” In this guide’s translation: Carcass, you tremble? You would tremble far more if you knew where I am taking you. Turenne was a French marshal of the seventeenth century; this guide has not checked the source of the anecdote. The words address the speaker’s own body as it trembles before danger. The book of “fearless ones” opens with fear, and with a will that goes on regardless. the Book V epigraph

§343, “What our Cheerfulness Signifies,” is Reading 8’s first section. It begins with “The most important of more recent events—that ‘God is dead,’ that the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief.” Its shadows are only beginning to fall over Europe, and for most people “the event itself is far too great, too remote” to have reached them. That was the madman’s point in §125, now stated in the book’s own voice. What is new is the response of “we philosophers and ‘free spirits’”: “our hearts overflow with gratitude, astonishment, presentiment and expectation.” “At last the horizon seems open once more, granting even that it is not bright,” and the section ends: “perhaps never before did such an ‘open sea’ exist” (German vielleicht; mark ?). In §125 the madman asked whether the deed was “too great for us.” In §343 the same report is a dawn. §343, what our cheerfulness signifies

The will to truth

§344, “To what Extent even We are still Pious,” is Reading 8’s second section, and that lesson follows its argument step by step. On this route, notice where it leads. Science admits convictions only as hypotheses, yet science itself rests on one conviction: “there is nothing more necessary than truth.” That conviction cannot come from usefulness, since truth and untruth have both proved useful. So the will to truth means “I will not deceive, not even myself”: “and thus we have reached the realm of morality.” It “might be a concealed Will to Death” (German könnte; mark ?). The conclusion: “even we knowing ones of to-day, the godless and anti-metaphysical, still take our fire from the conflagration kindled by a belief a millennium old, the Christian belief, which was also the belief of Plato, that God is truth, that the truth is divine.” The last question: “what if God himself turns out to be our most persistent lie?” The German is unsre längste Lüge, our longest lie. §344, the will to truth

Two further details. The section says that life has shown its great type on the side of “the most unscrupulous πολύτροποι.” The Greek word means of many turns, or of many wiles: Homer’s epithet for Odysseus. The German text used here garbles the Greek, so this reading follows Common, and the identification is this guide’s. And §344 is 1887’s reply to §§110–112 and §121 (stations 13–14), where truth was one condition of life among useful errors. Station 29’s §357 continues the argument: Christian truthfulness itself defeated the Christian God.

Morality as a problem

§345, “Morality as a Problem,” begins with the person, not the argument: “A defect in personality revenges itself everywhere.” Great problems “do not let themselves be held by toads and weaklings: that has ever been their taste—a taste also which they share with all high-spirited women.” State the aside plainly; the German is mit allen wackern Weiblein, a diminutive, little women. Then the claim: no one has yet stood to morality “as one who knew morality as a problem.” The historians of morality, “especially Englishmen,” are still under “the popular superstition of Christian Europe, that the characteristic of moral action consists in abnegation, self-denial, self-sacrifice, or in fellow-feeling.” The section says that “In an individual case” it tried to encourage such a history, in vain. A biographical reading identifies the unnamed person as Nietzsche’s former friend Paul Rée; the section names no one. §345, morality as a problem

The section’s most important sentence is about method. Historians who show that a people’s moral opinions are foolish think “that just by so doing they have criticised the morality itself.” But “the worth of a precept, ‘Thou shalt,’ is still fundamentally different from and independent of such opinions about it.” “A morality could even have grown out of an error: but with this knowledge the problem of its worth would not even be touched.” Hold every origin story in the book to this rule, including the conjectural histories you marked H in Books I–IV. The section ends: “no one has hitherto tested the value of that most celebrated of all medicines, called morality: for which purpose it is first of all necessary for one—to call it in question. Well, that is just our work.” §345, origin and worth

§346, “Our Note of Interrogation,” asks who “we” are, and refuses the old names: “atheists, unbelievers, or even immoralists.” “We” no longer have the bitterness of one who makes “a martyrdom out of his unbelief.” The world is “ungodly, immoral, and ‘inhuman,’” and it now seems “absolutely ridiculous when man claims to devise values to surpass the values of the actual world.” The attitude of “man versus the world,” man “as judge of the world,” is dismissed with laughter. But the laughter turns on itself: “Have we not in our very laughing just made a further step in despising mankind?” The section foresees a terrible alternative: “Either do away with your venerations, or—with yourselves!” “The latter would be Nihilism—but would not the former also be Nihilism? This is our note of interrogation.” In the German text used here, Nihilismus appears only in §§346 and 347. Mark it V, and notice that the section ends with a question. §346, our note of interrogation

The need of belief

§347, “Believers and their Need of Belief,” measures a person’s power by how little “fixed opinion” he needs. “Most people in old Europe, as it seems to me, still need Christianity at present.” A dogma refuted a thousand times will be accepted again as “true” if people need it. The same need shows in positivism, in “Vaterländerei (so I designate Jingoism, called chauvinisme in France, and ‘deutsch’ in Germany),” in Parisian naturalism, and in “Nihilism in the St Petersburg style (that is to say, in the belief in unbelief, even to martyrdom for it).” The principle: “Belief is always most desired, most pressingly needed where there is a lack of will.” §347, the need of belief

Buddhism and Christianity, the section says, arose from “an extraordinary malady of the will.” Notice the order. It first hedges, “perhaps it could be inferred” (German Woraus vielleicht abzunehmen wäre; mark ?), and then asserts without a hedge: “And in truth it has been so.” Mark that assertion H, a historical claim about two world religions offered without evidence. The section ends with the opposite type: a spirit who could “bid farewell to every belief, to every wish for certainty,” and “dance even on the verge of abysses.” “Such a spirit would be the free spirit par excellence.”

A difficulty

Can the free spirit do without belief?

§344 says that even “the godless and anti-metaphysical” still take their fire from the belief “that the truth is divine,” and that science rests on the conviction that nothing is more necessary than truth. §347 imagines a spirit who bids farewell “to every belief, to every wish for certainty,” and calls it “the free spirit par excellence.”

Three readings are possible.

  • An unreachable ideal. If §344 is right, the free spirit still believes in truth, so §347’s farewell to every belief describes an ideal no inquirer can reach.
  • A different way of holding. §347’s free spirit supports himself “on slender cords and possibilities”: he holds truth as a question, not a certainty. §344’s closing “what if” is that kind of holding.
  • An open problem. §344 names a difficulty that Book V does not solve, and §346’s note of interrogation marks where it stands.

Decide which the text best supports. Name one sentence from §344 and one from §347.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

How does §343 differ from §125 on the news of God’s death?

Both say that most people have not yet grasped the event. But §125’s madman asks whether the deed is too great for us, while in §343 “we” free spirits feel the report as a new dawn and an open sea.

According to §344, why can the will to truth not rest on usefulness?

Because truth and untruth have both proved useful. The unconditional will to truth must therefore be a moral commitment: not to deceive, not even oneself.

What does §345 say about a morality that grew out of an error?

That its origin in error would not touch the question of its worth. Criticizing people’s opinions about a morality is not yet criticizing the morality.

What alternative does §346 fear, and what does it call each side?

“Either do away with your venerations, or—with yourselves!” It calls the second Nihilism, and asks whether the first would not be Nihilism too.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Choose §343 or §344. What does it add to, or change in, the 1882 book?

  • Start from your answer to station 25’s pause: what had the 1882 book claimed?
  • Quote no more than two short phrases.
  • Keep the 1887 “we” distinct from the voice of Books I–IV.

A successful response names a specific 1882 section, states the 1887 section’s claim plainly, and says whether the later section extends, revises, or contradicts the earlier one.
