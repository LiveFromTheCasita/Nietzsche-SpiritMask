What Is Work Taking from Us?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 7 of 10

Read: Book III, §173, then §206. Read each in full, stopping before §174 and §207 respectively.
Allow: About 60–80 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §173 · §206 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Imagine finishing a demanding workday with enough energy to eat and sleep, but little left to think about how you want to live. The routine may provide income and satisfaction. It may also make questioning the routine almost impossible.

Section 173 asks whether this exhaustion helps explain the praise of work. Section 206 asks whether better pay would resolve a worker's loss of independence, then proposes an escape whose costs fall beyond the European workers it addresses.

Read both passages through their endings. The second includes attacks on socialism, a call for European emigration and colonial expansion, and a proposal involving Chinese workers. These belong to its argument about freedom. They cannot be omitted while presenting the passage as an uncomplicated defense of individual dignity.

Reading 6 examined the motives and consequences hidden by a favorable moral description. Here the description is productive, respectable work. As in Reading 1, questioning a familiar reason for valuing something leaves us with the task of finding better reasons.

The main question: What kind of independence should work permit, and whose independence does Nietzsche's proposed alternative actually protect?

Three things to notice

  1. The kind of work under discussion. In §173, Nietzsche specifies exhausting toil from morning to night. Trace how it can serve social control without assuming that every activity called work has identical effects.
  2. The difference between income and independence. In §206, ask what higher wages leave unchanged, and why Nietzsche distrusts a reorganized society that retains impersonality.
  3. Who appears as an individual. Follow the proposal from European workers to colonized places and Chinese replacement labor. Whose purposes receive attention, and whose are left out?

A way to read the passages

The praise of work can have a political purpose

Section 173 opens with the people who glorify work. Nietzsche suspects that behind this praise lies fear of the individual. Exhausting labor keeps people occupied and makes the development of independent purposes harder. Work can perform a policing function without a police officer issuing commands. §173

The mechanism is the expenditure of energy. Toil withdraws strength from reflection, imagination, care, love, hatred, and the desire for independence. Small, regular goals provide satisfaction while leaving limited capacity for questioning the organization of life.

Consider a schedule that leaves someone nominally free each evening but too depleted to study, maintain friendships, or consider another occupation. Their freedom on paper coexists with practical restrictions on what they can do. This illustrates Nietzsche's mechanism; it does not establish that every demanding schedule has that purpose.

Nor is his list simply a collection of admirable capacities. He includes greed and hatred alongside reflection and love. What is suppressed is a range of energies from which individual projects and disturbances might arise. A secure society may value their containment precisely because their results are unpredictable.

When security becomes the final standard

Nietzsche connects constant work with social security, then says that security has become an object of worship. His objection concerns its supreme position: if keeping people predictable becomes the highest aim, opportunities for independent development can appear mainly as threats.

The accusation needs testing. Praise for work might express appreciation of skill, a contribution to shared needs, or relief from material insecurity. Demonstrating that exhaustion serves control would not establish that every person praising work secretly intends control.

The aphorism ends by mocking alarm at workers themselves becoming dangerous individuals. Those whom industrious discipline was supposed to contain have become a source of unrest. The ending exposes an instability in the expectation that labor will reliably secure obedience.

This is not a developed account of labor organization or a claim that every worker's resistance is emancipatory. It is an ironic reversal of the opening confidence in work as social discipline.

Why more pay may leave the essential relation unchanged

Section 206 begins by distinguishing combinations easily run together. Poverty can coexist with cheerfulness and independence; it can also coexist with cheerfulness and servitude. Contentment alone does not establish that a person directs their own life. §206, the opening distinctions

Nietzsche calls factory workers enslaved because they are used as interchangeable components of production. His language identifies impersonality as an essential injury: a person becomes useful primarily as something that fills a place in a mechanism.

Higher wages may improve someone's circumstances without changing who determines their activity. This is the core of his objection to treating compensation as a sufficient solution. A larger payment does not automatically restore control over time, capacities, or the direction of a life.

The word slavery is his polemical description of industrial dependence. The passage does not establish that every employment relation is identical to ownership of a person. We can examine its claim about subordination without erasing differences among forms of coercion.

Equally, the argument that pay is insufficient does not establish that pay is unimportant. For someone unable to meet basic needs, increased income could make practical independence more attainable. Nietzsche's impatience risks passing too quickly over that possibility.

A society can grow richer while impoverishing people's lives

Nietzsche next attacks the national ambition to produce and accumulate as much as possible. He asks what inner capacities are spent in pursuit of that external goal. The question concerns the opportunity cost of production: what becomes of the people whose energies make it possible?

He portrays workers caught in comparison, envy, newspaper opinion, and fluctuating fortunes. They may lose confidence in a life with few needs and come to measure their prospects through the very standards that keep them dependent.

His alternative includes voluntary poverty, especially for intellectually inclined people, without the usual professional or marital commitments. This is a concrete and demanding proposal, not a general invitation to relax after work.

It also raises questions the passage does not settle. Who can afford to have few needs? Who depends on their earnings or care? A voluntary simplification available to one person may be unavailable to another. Existing responsibilities cannot simply be made irrelevant by describing them as obstacles to independence.

Why Nietzsche also distrusts socialist promises

The criticism does not stop at employers. Nietzsche opposes a projected new society if it intensifies the impersonality of its mechanisms. His objection reaches a vision of welfare that counts greater provision and organized production as success while leaving little room for individual direction.

He then attacks socialist organizers as offering hopes that keep workers waiting for liberation from outside. In his account, this waiting produces frustration and fury while daily dependence continues. Replacing obedience to an employer with obedience to a state or revolutionary party would leave the central problem unresolved.

His rhetoric is contemptuous, and the accusation is broad. The passage does not examine different socialist proposals or show that collective organization must exclude individual initiative. Working together to shorten working hours, for example, could aim to enlarge the independence of each participant.

That example tests Nietzsche's generalization rather than supplying a history of any particular movement. We need to distinguish his objection to a specific kind of dependence from his unsupported treatment of alternative political projects as inevitably reproducing it.

Emigration is the proposed practical break

Nietzsche calls for a mass departure of European workers. They should make their condition untenable as a class by leaving it, rather than accepting a higher price for continuing in it. He imagines unprecedented emigration as a protest against machines, capital, state dependence, and party dependence.

His imagined worker is willing to change location whenever servitude threatens, accept adventure or war, and risk death. The standard is severe: independence receives priority over the security criticized in §173.

Part of the proposal concerns mastery of oneself. But the passage also calls for becoming masters in countries it labels uncivilized. It subsequently names large colonial undertakings and celebrates European virtues spreading abroad. These details prevent us from reducing the departure to a private journey of self-discovery.

The recommendation has therefore changed the scale of the problem. We must ask what freedom means when one population's escape is imagined through expansion into places where other people already live.

Colonial freedom leaves other people's purposes outside the frame

Nietzsche describes the emigrants' fairness, rationality, and suspicion as European virtues that can flourish elsewhere. Energies regarded as dangerous at home might become heroic abroad. He presents this transformation largely from the viewpoint of the emigrating Europeans and the Europe relieved by their departure.

The people in the destination countries scarcely appear as people with purposes, claims, or institutions of their own. The argument does not ask whether they want newcomers seeking mastery or what the newcomers' adventures and wars might cost them.

This omission bears directly on his standard of independence. If being treated as an instrument for someone else's project degrades factory workers, that same concern should apply to people whose lands and lives become the setting for the workers' liberation.

The passage does not describe a detailed colonial administration, so we should not invent one. Its own language of colonization, mastery, war, and the export of European virtues is sufficient to make the problem substantive. The proposed escape threatens to move domination rather than overcome it.

Who will perform the remaining labor?

Near the end Nietzsche addresses the shortage of workers that emigration might create. One possibility is to reduce wants that Europeans acquired because satisfying them had become easy. This follows his earlier concern that multiplying needs sustains dependence.

Another possibility is to bring Chinese workers to Europe. He describes their supposed habits through an image of industrious ants and attributes calmness, contemplation, and stability to an undifferentiated Asian character.

Some of these qualities are praised, and Nietzsche imagines Europeans learning from newcomers. That does not remove the racial generalization. Chinese individuals are assigned a collective temperament and a place in the labor system from which European individuals are urged to escape.

The text does not explicitly propose forcing Chinese people to migrate. Its problem is already visible without that additional claim: it does not extend the same inquiry into their desired independence, their working conditions, or their refusal of interchangeable labor.

The closing proposal makes the contradiction concrete. A critique of reducing people to components ends by imagining replacement labor through a stereotype of group suitability. Ask whether the demand for individuality survives when applied to everyone affected by the proposed solution.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can Nietzsche defend independence while distributing its opportunities so unequally?

Section 173 gives a reason to scrutinize arrangements that consume people's capacity to form purposes of their own. Section 206 applies this concern forcefully to European workers, then offers an alternative that neglects the purposes of colonized populations and stereotypes Chinese laborers.

The difficulty is internal to the argument. Its standard of independence creates a question the proposal does not answer: why should the costs imposed on these other people count for less? A defense of workers as persons loses force if its solution treats others chiefly as available territory or manpower.

There are practical objections too. Collective protections, higher pay, and shared services might enable independence for people who cannot simply leave. Nietzsche's suspicion of externally organized solutions needs to engage that possibility, especially where dependence arises from arrangements one person cannot alter alone.

His challenge nevertheless identifies a question that higher output and better provision do not automatically answer: do people gain meaningful control over their lives? We can retain that question while rejecting the colonial and racial assumptions built into his response.

The exercise is to improve the argument's consistency, not to rewrite Nietzsche as though he had already offered the improved version.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What makes work function like policing in §173?

Exhausting toil consumes energies that could support reflection, independent desires, and disruptive projects. It supplies routine goals and satisfactions. Nietzsche connects praise for this arrangement with a social preference for security and predictability.

Does §173 establish that every kind of work is bad?

No. Its specified case is severe toil from morning to night, and its target is the glorification of work as discipline. Other activities may develop skills or independence. Claims about their effects require attention to their particular conditions.

Why are higher wages insufficient in §206?

They can leave impersonal subordination intact: the worker still functions as a replaceable component. Nietzsche asks about control and individuality beyond compensation. This does not establish that income cannot help people achieve practical independence.

Why must the colonial proposal remain part of the reading?

Nietzsche's escape includes mastery, colonial expansion, and the possible use of war. The purposes of existing populations receive little attention. These features test whether the independence he demands for European workers is extended to others affected by their liberation.

What is troubling about the proposal involving Chinese workers?

It assigns a supposedly uniform temperament to a population and imagines its suitability for remaining labor. Even its positive stereotypes diminish individual differences. Nietzsche does not examine those workers' independence with the care he demands for European workers.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose an imagined proposal for making people's working lives freer. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Identify a restriction on independence using §173's account of exhausted time and energy.
  2. Explain what your proposal changes beyond income, while acknowledging how income might matter.
  3. Name everyone whose work, resources, or opportunities make the change possible. Ask whether a burden is being shifted onto them.
  4. Use §206's colonial and racial assumptions to identify a failure your proposal must avoid.
  5. State one tradeoff or unresolved question that prevents you from calling the proposal complete.

A successful response applies the same demand for independence to all affected people. Distinguish your revised proposal from the alternative Nietzsche actually presents.
