What Does It Mean to Think Honestly?

The Gay Science — Essential Reading Path · Reading 1 of 10

Read: Book I, §2, then §12, from the original 1882 edition. Read both aphorisms in full, stopping before §3 and §13 respectively.
Allow: About 45–60 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): §2 · §12.

Before you read

Suppose a project has gone badly. You believe the main problem was that other people refused to listen. That explanation may be accurate. It may also make the disappointment easier to bear.

Someone asks what supports your account. Did people reject a clear proposal, or did you leave important things unexplained? What would someone who disagreed with you say? Which evidence would lead you to revise your judgment?

You can sincerely believe your explanation and still avoid these questions. You can also feel threatened by them without their being unfair. The distinction between having a conviction and examining its grounds is where this reading begins.

Nietzsche then adds a complication. What if careful inquiry makes you more attentive, but also more vulnerable to disappointment? Would the discomfort show that something had gone wrong with the inquiry, or would its value require another measure?

The main question: What makes a belief intellectually responsible—and why might responsible inquiry be uncomfortable?

Read §§2 and 12 once before continuing. In §2, follow the speaker’s judgment of other people all the way to his judgment of himself. In §12, notice the questions and possibilities before treating the passage as a settled claim.

Three things to notice

  1. The unexamined weights. People use scales to call things good and bad. What would it mean to question the weights themselves?
  2. The last sentence of §2. Why does the speaker end an accusation against others by acknowledging an injustice of his own?
  3. The two possible goals in §12. What changes if increasing our capacity for enjoyment also increases our capacity for pain? What can inquiry do for either way of living?

A way to read the passages

What are we weighing with?

Section 2 begins with people who keep judging while remaining uninterested in questions about their judgments. The scales suggest an ordinary activity: assessing an action, a person, or a way of life. The weights suggest the standards by which the assessment is made. Nietzsche’s complaint reaches beneath the answer to the grounds on which someone has arrived at it. §2, the opening image

Imagine calling someone successful because they have acquired wealth. One question is whether they really possess as much as people suppose. Another is why wealth should determine the assessment. Investigating the first claim leaves the second standard available for examination.

His broader demand is that people concern themselves with the reasons for and against what they believe. A familiar standard should not escape examination merely because everyone nearby uses it.

Kindness, intelligence, refinement, and sincerity do not automatically meet this demand. Someone can possess admirable qualities while taking very little trouble over the support for their beliefs. The point is unsettling because we often trust a judgment partly through our affection for the person making it.

Neither does disagreeing with the majority establish intellectual conscience. A contrary belief can be inherited from a different group, adopted for the pleasure of being unusual, or protected from scrutiny in exactly the same way. The question concerns the work of examining reasons.

Inquiry is a passion here

Nietzsche’s tone is severe. He speaks of contempt, shame, and a distinction between higher and lower people. The passage makes intellectual conscience into a demanding standard of human worth. Understanding the demand includes noticing this severity, rather than making the speaker sound more accommodating than he is.

Thomas Common translates one of the speaker’s central demands as a “longing for certainty.” We should keep that wording in view. The passage does not simply announce that certainty is impossible or that every confident judgment is mistaken. It describes a deep need to examine a belief’s strongest grounds while standing amid an existence experienced as uncertain and ambiguous.

Wanting assurance and possessing it are different conditions. The speaker’s passion drives questioning; it does not give him an unquestionable answer to every question. Notice the desire and delight he finds in inquiry itself. Intellectual conscience is a way of caring about reasons, with an emotional intensity of its own.

This helps explain an initially puzzling preference. The speaker responds more favorably to some religious people who hate reason than to people who remain indifferent to questioning. Their hostility at least reveals that they feel its challenge. He is not endorsing either their beliefs or their hatred of reason. He is provoking us to notice how completely the indifferent person can avoid experiencing a problem.

The accusation turns back toward its author

At the end of §2, the speaker admits that he repeatedly expects everyone, simply by being human, to share his feeling about inquiry. He calls this expectation his own “special kind of unrighteousness.” The final sentence changes the position from which we hear the preceding judgments. §2, the closing admission

A person can be deeply committed to an activity and find it difficult to understand why others do not feel the same necessity. An investigator may experience an unanswered question as intolerable while someone else gives their attention to a different concern. The speaker recognizes his tendency to make his passion the measure of everyone.

The admission does not plainly withdraw his demand for intellectual conscience. Nor does it settle whether that demand can be justified more broadly. It makes the critic part of the problem: how fair is his judgment of people whose attachments differ from his own?

That is something to carry into your reading of Nietzsche. We can ask whether an accusation reveals a real failure, whether its severity is warranted, and what the accuser’s own investment contributes to it. Acknowledging the last question need not erase the first two.

Must knowledge make us more comfortable?

Section 12 begins by questioning an assumed goal: should science produce the greatest possible pleasure and the least possible pain? In this book, the pursuit of knowledge extends beyond laboratory science. The question concerns what we expect inquiry to accomplish in human life. §12, the opening questions

Nietzsche asks what would follow if our capacities for intense pleasure and intense pain were closely connected. Perhaps reducing our exposure to one would also reduce our capacity for the other. Perhaps unfamiliar enjoyments would require a susceptibility to discomfort that a quieter life could avoid.

Someone learning to listen carefully to music may discover pleasures that were previously inaudible to them. The same attention may make a careless performance more grating. This is an everyday analogy for a capacity acquiring both rewarding and unwelcome consequences. It does not demonstrate that every increase in suffering creates a corresponding enjoyment.

The wording matters. Nietzsche begins with a possibility, inserts “perhaps,” and ends with another speculative prospect. He also presents the choice in stark terms. A close reader should preserve both features: the hypothesis remains open, while the rhetoric presses us toward a demanding decision.

His discussion of the Stoics belongs to this argument. He characterizes their position as accepting less pleasure in order to experience less pain. This is Nietzsche’s characterization of Stoicism, rather than a complete account of its philosophy.

Inquiry can serve more than one kind of life

An important sentence in §12 says that science can advance either goal. Inquiry might contribute to a life with diminished susceptibility to both pain and pleasure. It might also open a more intense existence, with new delights and new sources of disturbance. The passage does not give science one inevitable emotional result.

Nietzsche observes that it has so far been associated with cooling and diminishing enjoyment. He then imagines a different possibility: knowledge as a source of great pain whose counteracting power could disclose unfamiliar worlds of enjoyment. The future remains something to investigate.

This interrupts an easy promise we might bring to §2. Examining a belief’s grounds need not make us feel better immediately. It might unsettle a comforting explanation, disclose a difficulty we had missed, or make us dissatisfied with something we previously accepted.

Discomfort would not, by itself, establish that the inquiry had failed. It would not establish its success either. A painful conclusion still needs evidence. A comforting conclusion can be true. The emotional effect of a belief and the support for it remain questions we must distinguish.

Why begin a joyful book here?

Together, the passages connect inquiry with a passionate way of living. Section 2 asks what it means to take the examination of belief seriously. Section 12 questions the expectation that knowledge should be valuable chiefly because it removes discomfort.

The book’s joy can now become a question. What pleasures belong to discovering, understanding, revising, or becoming attentive? What vulnerabilities accompany them? The title’s promise becomes more demanding when we allow the costs to remain visible.

These two aphorisms do not settle how inquiry and the love of life can coexist. They give us a reason to follow the question into the next reading, where art and laughter become part of the response.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does a passion for inquiry justify judging everyone by it?

Nietzsche’s speaker treats indifference to a belief’s grounds as contemptible. We might agree that people should examine consequential judgments while still questioning the scale of his condemnation. Does intellectual conscience require the same intensity from everyone? What other demands on a person’s attention matter?

The final admission in §2 supplies a point from which to raise these questions. It also prevents us from adopting the speaker’s contempt as though it were itself evidence of intellectual independence. Feeling superior to unreflective people can become another belief whose appeal we have failed to examine.

Section 12 adds a related difficulty. Even if some demanding activities enlarge both pleasure and pain, must every worthwhile life favor greater intensity? What would support that judgment? Nietzsche’s comparison asks readers to reconsider comfort as an unquestioned goal, but it does not establish that all suffering is necessary or beneficial.

A useful response separates three matters: the reasons for a belief, the emotional consequences of examining it, and the value we place on that examination. Evidence bears on the first. The second calls for attention to what actually happens. The third remains a philosophical question that cannot be settled simply by praising either comfort or hardship.

Keep this question open: What would make an inquiry worth pursuing if it could not promise to make you happier?

Check your understanding

Try answering before reading each suggested response.

Why is sincere belief not enough to satisfy intellectual conscience?

Sincerity describes someone’s relation to what they believe. It does not show that they have examined the reasons for and against it. Nietzsche’s demand concerns that examination, even when the believer is intelligent, kind, or personally admirable.

Does the ending of §2 cancel its criticism of unexamined belief?

It does not clearly do so. The speaker acknowledges the injustice of expecting everyone to share his passion for inquiry. That complicates his judgment of others while leaving the demand for intellectual conscience in view. The relationship between the demand and his severity remains open to assessment.

Does §12 claim that science must make life more painful?

No. Nietzsche explicitly says it can serve either of the goals he describes. He explores the possibility that greater capacities for enjoyment and pain develop together, but does not establish an inevitable outcome of inquiry or a universal law about suffering.

If a conclusion is painful to accept, is that evidence that it is true?

No. A conclusion’s emotional effect does not establish its truth. Both welcome and unwelcome claims need examination. This distinction helps us apply the demand for intellectual conscience without turning discomfort into a substitute for evidence.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one ordinary belief you are willing to examine. Write six to eight sentences that do four things:

  1. State the belief clearly and give your strongest reason for holding it.
  2. Identify a serious objection and one kind of evidence that could change your mind.
  3. Explain what makes the belief comfortable, uncomfortable, or emotionally useful to you, and whether that affects how you weigh the evidence.
  4. Say what examining it could make possible, even if the result were less reassuring than you hoped.

Finish with this sentence:

The question I have been too quick to set aside is…

A successful response distinguishes support from attachment and gives an objection a fair hearing. You may finish with a revised belief, a better-supported belief, or a clearer account of what you still do not know.
