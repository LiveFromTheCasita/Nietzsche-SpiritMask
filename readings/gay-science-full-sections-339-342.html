The Heaviest Burden and the End of 1882

Optional full-text route · Station 25 of 35 (proposed)

Review draft. This lesson belongs to a proposed full-text route that has not been published. Only some stations are drafted so far. For the complete published introduction, use the ten-session guided reading path.

Read: Book IV, §§339–342, in full, from the original 1882 edition. Begin at §339 and stop at the end of §342, before the Book V title. Do not read on into Book V yet.
Allow: About 45–65 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, the pause at the end of 1882, and a written response. This is an editorial estimate, not a measured reading time. The four sections total about 1,000 words in the free edition.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): begin at §339 · §340 · §341 · §342.

Quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation, including his section titles. Kaufmann’s wording differs, and none of it is quoted here; this draft has not been compared with Kaufmann’s text. Where a word drives the interpretation, this page notes the German, from the Projekt Gutenberg-DE text.

Before you read

These four sections end the book as it was published in 1882. They move from life as a veiled beauty to the death of Socrates, to the thought of living one’s life again, and to a short scene in which Zarathustra goes down from his mountain. After §342 the free edition prints Book V, which Nietzsche added in 1887. This station stops before it.

§341 is the second assignment of Reading 7 (with §276, station 20), which reads it closely. The route’s checkpoint for Reading 7 comes at the end of this station.

The main question: Why end the original book with a dramatic departure—and what had the 1882 book claimed by the time it ended?

Keep using the marks: P, H, V, and ? for Nietzsche’s own hedge only.

What to notice

  1. Veils. §339 calls life “a gold-embroidered veil of lovely potentialities.” Compare §§59–60 (station 8), where images of women are made from a distance.
  2. Last words and first words. §340 reads Socrates’ last words; §342 ends with Zarathustra’s first. Compare what each speaker says about life.
  3. The evening sun. §342’s sun “givest light also to the nether-world.” Compare the evening sun of §337 (station 24).

Close reading

Life as a woman

§339, “Vita femina,” says that to see the “ultimate beauties” of a work, a deed, a person, or nature needs more than knowledge and good-will; it needs “the rarest, good chance.” “I am inclined to believe” (German ich glauben möchte; mark ?) that the highest summit of all that is good has stayed hidden from most people, “and even for the best.” The world “is overfull of beautiful things, but it is nevertheless poor, very poor, in beautiful moments.” Then, hedged again with “perhaps” (vielleicht; mark ?): “this is the greatest charm of life: it puts a gold-embroidered veil of lovely potentialities over itself, promising, resisting, modest, mocking, sympathetic, seductive. Yes, life is a woman!” §339, vita femina

State the figure plainly: the section personifies life as a woman defined by veiling and seduction. Book II’s §§59–60 (station 8) treated such images of women as projections seen from a distance; ask whether §339 is one. The 1887 preface (§4, station 35) later asks, “Perhaps truth is a woman who has reasons for not showing her reasons?” That question belongs to the retrospective of 1887, not to the 1882 book.

The dying Socrates

§340 admires Socrates, “This mocking and amorous demon and rat-catcher of Athens,” for what he did, said, “and did not say.” It wishes that he had been silent at the end too: “perhaps he might then have belonged to a still higher order of intellects” (vielleicht; mark ?). His last words, “O Crito, I owe a cock to Asclepios,” are read as meaning “O Crito, life is a long sickness!”: a thank-offering to the god of healing for being cured of life. Common adds “long”; the German says das Leben ist eine Krankheit, life is a sickness. So Socrates, who had lived cheerfully and “to all appearance as a soldier,” “was a pessimist!” He “took his revenge” with “that veiled, fearful, pious, and blasphemous phrase!” The section ends: “Ah, my friends! We must surpass even the Greeks!” §340, the dying Socrates

Mark the reading H and V. It is the section’s interpretation, offered “For him who has ears.” The last words are in Plato’s Phaedo, and they have been read in other ways. Benjamin Jowett’s introduction to his English translation calls the request a puzzle to later ages. He reads it chiefly as Socrates’ ironic remembrance of a small religious duty, while allowing that Socrates may have meant he was now restored to health. Nietzsche returned to these last words in Twilight of the Idols (1888); do not read that later treatment back into this one.

The heaviest burden

§341 is Reading 7’s second section; read that lesson for the full analysis. On this route, notice three things. First, it is a question, not a doctrine: “What if a demon crept after thee into thy loneliest loneliness”? The demon’s words are in quotation marks, and they say that “every pain and every joy and every thought and every sigh” must return “in the same series and sequence.” Second, the section gives the thought no name. The phrase “eternal recurrence” appeared only in §285 (station 20), inside a quoted accusation. Third, it is hedged: the thought would transform you “and perhaps crush thee” (vielleicht; mark ?). The German title, Das grösste Schwergewicht, means the greatest weight. §341, the heaviest burden

Incipit Tragœdia

§342, “Incipit Tragœdia” (the tragedy begins), ends the book with a story. “When Zarathustra was thirty years old, he left his home and the Lake of Urmi, and went into the mountains.” After ten years he speaks to the sun: “Thou great star! What would be thy happiness if thou hadst not those for whom thou shinest!” He is “weary of my wisdom, like the bee that hath gathered too much honey,” and he must “descend into the deep, as thou doest in the evening.” The last words are: “Thus began Zarathustra’s down-going.” The German is Untergang, which also means going under, as the sun does, and decline. §342, Zarathustra’s down-going

The same scene opens the Prologue of Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883), nearly word for word. The main change is the lake: there Zarathustra leaves den See seiner Heimat, the lake of his home, not the Lake of Urmi. This guide checked the two German texts against each other in their Project Gutenberg editions. Read §342 as the 1882 book’s ending, not as a summary of Zarathustra. Compare §281 (station 20), on masters who know “how to find the end,” and §153 (station 16), whose poet asked what to do with his fifth act. The 1887 preface (§1) later jokes about this title: “incipit parodia.”

The end of the 1882 book

Stop here. §342 is the last section of the book as published in 1882. Book V, “We Fearless Ones,” with its Turenne epigraph and §§343–383, together with the appended songs, the title-page motto, and the preface dated autumn 1886, belongs to the expanded edition of 1887. The route marks the hinge with this pause, so that you can say what the 1882 book claimed before Book V reopens its questions. The writing prompt below is the pause’s task.

Optional, and not part of the route. Thus Spoke Zarathustra begins where §342 leaves off, and Beyond Good and Evil (1886) was written between the two editions of this book. If you want to compare, see the site’s guides to Thus Spoke Zarathustra and Beyond Good and Evil. Neither is assigned here, and nothing on the route depends on them.

A difficulty

Why end with a departure?

The 1882 book could have ended with §341’s question, or with §340’s call to surpass the Greeks. It ends instead with a story in which someone else, Zarathustra, goes down from a mountain to give his wisdom away.

Three readings are possible.

  • A handing-on. The book’s questions are too large for aphorisms, and §342 announces the form, a figure and a story, that will take them up.
  • A tragedy beginning. The title matters. §153’s poet killed the gods in his fourth act; §342 begins the fifth, and §341’s question is its burden.
  • A self-portrait at a distance. Zarathustra’s weariness with his wisdom and his need of “hands outstretched to take it” describe the book’s own relation to its readers, at one remove.

Decide which the text best supports. Name one sentence from §342 and one from §§339–341.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does §339 say that life is poor in beautiful moments?

Because seeing the ultimate beauties needs knowledge, good-will, and the right standpoint all at once, and these rarely coincide. What unveils itself does so only once.

How does §340 read Socrates’ last words, and what does the German change?

As a thank-offering for being cured of life, so that Socrates was a pessimist who took revenge on life. Common adds “long”; the German says only that life is a sickness.

Does §341 assert that everything recurs?

No. It asks what you would do if a demon said so, and how the thought might transform or crush you. It gives the thought no name.

Where else does §342’s scene appear?

Nearly word for word at the opening of the Prologue of Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883), where the Lake of Urmi becomes the lake of Zarathustra’s home.

Writing prompt: before Book V

Write 250–300 words answering:

What had the 1882 book claimed by the time it ended?

  • Name three claims the book stands behind, from at least two of Books I–IV. Cite each by book and section.
  • Mark each claim P, H, or V, and note any hedge.
  • Name one question the book leaves open, and say whether §§339–342 revise any of your three claims.

A successful response states the claims in the book’s terms, distinguishes them from this guide’s interpretations, and does not draw on Book V, the songs, or the 1887 preface.
