One God Behind the Masks

Optional full-text route · Station 9 of 19

Before you read

Reading 4 read Oedipus and Prometheus as heroes whose wisdom and creative power bring catastrophe. §10 now makes a much bolder claim about those same heroes: they are not, finally, individuals at all. Behind each of them, Nietzsche says, stands one god.

§11 then turns to the end of the story. Greek tragedy, he says, did not grow old and fade like the other arts. It "died by suicide," and the one who carried out the death was Euripides. The charge Nietzsche brings is specific: Euripides brought the spectator onto the stage.

The main question: If every tragic hero is a mask of the suffering Dionysus, what exactly does Euripides destroy when he brings everyday people onto the stage?

Read both sections once. In §10, mark where Nietzsche moves from a tradition about early tragedy to a claim about the nature of suffering. In §11, mark each time he tells you what a later audience felt, and ask what evidence he gives.

What to notice

  1. The masks. If Prometheus and Oedipus are "masks" of one god, what becomes of the individual characters Reading 4 examined?
  2. How myths die. In §10, what, according to Nietzsche, kills a myth, and what brought myth back to life for a time?
  3. Two spectators. Near the end of §11, Nietzsche names one of two spectators Euripides respected and withholds the other. Who is named, and why hold the second name back?

Close reading

One god, many masks

§10 opens with what Nietzsche calls "an indisputable tradition": the earliest tragedy had only the sufferings of Dionysus as its theme, and Dionysus as its only hero. He then goes further. Until Euripides, he says, all the famous figures of the Greek stage, "Prometheus, Œdipus, etc.," are "but masks of this original hero, Dionysus." The god behind the masks explains the "ideality" of these figures. Nietzsche adds a remark he attributes to someone he cannot name: individuals as individuals are comic. §10, the masks

This connects to Reading 3. There, the chorus beheld Dionysus onstage in an Apollonian vision. Here Nietzsche says the hero appears with epic clearness because Apollo, the dream-interpreter, reads the chorus its Dionysian state through this symbolic figure. The clear individual is the form. The god is what the form carries.

Notice what kind of claim opens the section. A tradition about the earliest tragedy is a historical claim about origins. That the later heroes of Aeschylus and Sophocles are masks of Dionysus is Nietzsche's interpretation. Both are stated with the same confidence.

Zagreus and the "mystery doctrine"

The hero, Nietzsche says, is really "the suffering Dionysus of the mysteries": the god who as a boy was "dismembered by the Titans" and worshipped as Zagreus. From the myth he draws a metaphysical conclusion. We must regard individuation "as the source and primal cause of all suffering, as something objectionable in itself." In the same passage, the Olympian gods spring from the god's smile and humanity from his tears, and the initiates hope for a third Dionysus, a rebirth that would end individuation. §10, Zagreus

Nietzsche then names what he has assembled: "the mystery doctrine of tragedy." It has three parts:

  • the oneness of all that exists;
  • individuation as the primal cause of evil;
  • art as the joyous hope that the spell of individuation may be broken.

§10, the mystery doctrine

Use your E and R marks from Reading 1. The myth of a dismembered god is a story you can follow. That individuation is the cause of all suffering is an R claim about the world. Here it comes, not from an argument, but from a reading of a myth. The theme page on the Apollonian and Dionysian notes that these are the pages of the book that lean most heavily on borrowed metaphysics.

How a myth dies

Tragedy, Nietzsche says, took over the Homeric myths and gave them a new life. In Aeschylus, the Titan Prometheus comes back into the light, and even Zeus fears his end. What made this possible was "the Heracleian power of music," which can give a myth new and deeper significance. §10, music and myth

Then comes a general claim about religion. A myth dies, Nietzsche writes, when "an orthodox dogmatism" treats its stories as a completed sum of historical events and anxiously defends their credibility. "For this is the manner in which religions are wont to die out": when the feeling for myth is replaced by a claim to historical foundations. In tragedy, myth rises once more "like a wounded hero," with a last gleam in its eyes. §10, the death of myth

The section ends by addressing Euripides directly, as "impious Euripides." He tried to seize the dying myth and killed it. What he made in its place, Nietzsche says, was "counterfeit, masked" myth, music, and passion. The word masked turns against Euripides: where the older masks carried a god, his carry nothing behind them. §10, the address to Euripides

A suicide, not an old age

§11 begins with a contrast. The older Greek arts, Nietzsche says, died calmly in old age, their successors already standing by. Tragedy "died by suicide, in consequence of an irreconcilable conflict," and its death left a void he compares to the cry that "great Pan is dead." The New Attic Comedy that followed bore tragedy's features, but those it showed in its death-struggle. §11, the death of tragedy

The spectator on the stage

What did Euripides share with the comic poets who admired him? Nietzsche's short answer is that "the spectator was brought upon the stage by Euripides." Through him, "the commonplace individual forced his way from the spectators' benches to the stage." Odysseus, the typical Hellene of the older art, sinks into the figure of the Græculus, the cunning household slave. For evidence, Nietzsche draws on Aristophanes' comedy Frogs, where Euripides boasts of teaching the people to observe, debate, and draw conclusions. "Civic mediocrity," Nietzsche adds, was where Euripides placed all his political hopes. §11, the spectator onstage

Notice the evidence. Much of what Nietzsche says about Euripides' intentions comes from a comedy that mocks him. A comic portrait can be revealing. It is still a comic portrait, and Nietzsche uses it as testimony.

Greek cheerfulness as "the cheerfulness of the slave"

Nietzsche's contempt is plainest here. In the age that followed, he says, "the fifth class, that of the slaves," attains power, "at least in sentiment." If one can still speak of "Greek cheerfulness," it is "the cheerfulness of the slave who has nothing of consequence to answer for, nothing great to strive for." He says the early Christians despised it as a "womanish flight from earnestness and terror." Against it he sets "a Sixth Century" of tragedy, mysteries, Pythagoras, and Heraclitus. §11, Greek cheerfulness

Keep the claims apart. One is a claim about a change in dramatic art: the everyday person, and everyday speech, take over the stage. That can be examined against the plays. Another is an evaluation, carried by class contempt ("the slave") and a gendered insult ("womanish"). The evaluation does not follow from the observation. Nietzsche presents them together.

Two spectators

Nietzsche then complicates his own formula. He writes that "'public' is merely a word." Why should an artist bow to "a power whose strength is merely in numbers"? Euripides, he argues, did not in fact respect the crowd. His formula, that Euripides put the spectator onstage, "was but a provisional one." §11, the public

The real explanation is that Euripides respected only two spectators. One was "Euripides himself, Euripides as thinker, not as poet." Nietzsche compares his critical gift to Lessing's. Sitting in the theatre, this thinker found in the older tragedies something he could not understand: every clear figure had "a comet's tail" of the uncertain and inexplicable. The other spectator, the one "who did not comprehend, and therefore did not esteem, tragedy," Nietzsche leaves unnamed. §11, the two spectators

A difficulty

What would count as evidence for Nietzsche's story of how tragedy died?

§§10–11 combine several kinds of claim, each stated with great confidence:

  • Historical: early tragedy had Dionysus as its only hero.
  • Interpretive: later heroes are his masks.
  • Metaphysical: individuation is the cause of all suffering.
  • About artistic change: the spectator came onto the stage.
  • About motive: Euripides wrote for two spectators.

Some of these could be tested against the surviving plays. Some rest on a comedy's portrait of a rival. One comes from reading a myth as a doctrine about the world.

Choose two claims from the list. For each, write down what evidence would support it, and whether §§10–11 supply that evidence or only assert it. Then ask a harder question. Does Nietzsche's contempt for the "public" and for "the slave" help his argument, or would the account of artistic change be stronger without it?

The theme page on the Apollonian and Dionysian calls §§11–12 the most concretely historical stretch of the book and the most polemical. Test whether that description fits.

Retrospect checkpoint: an 1886 comparison

If you are following the chronological alternate, skip this until after the 1886 preface lesson.

In self-criticism §1, Nietzsche asks whether the "Hellenic cheerfulness" of later Greece might be a "glowing sunset," and whether Socratism might be a sign of decline. §11 of the book already treats later Greek cheerfulness with contempt, but in a different vocabulary: class, seriousness, and art, not the 1886 language of health and weariness. §6 of the preface regrets the book's borrowed Schopenhauerian formulas, and §10's claim that individuation is the cause of all suffering is a strong example. Add both to your ledger, and note whether 1886 describes 1872 or redescribes it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does Nietzsche say the famous heroes are "masks"?

Because, on his account, the only original hero of tragedy was Dionysus, and later heroes are forms in which the suffering god appears. Apollonian clarity gives each a distinct shape; behind the shape is the god who suffers individuation. That is why the figures have their "ideality." Nietzsche asserts this; the section does not demonstrate it.

What kills a myth, according to §10?

Treating it as history. When an orthodox dogmatism systematizes a religion's myths as historical facts and defends their credibility, the feeling for myth dies. Nietzsche says tragedy revived myth for a time through the power of music, before Euripides "killed" it.

Why does Nietzsche call his formula about the spectator "provisional"?

Because Euripides did not in fact defer to the crowd. Nietzsche argues that he wrote for two spectators he respected: himself as a critical thinker, and a second spectator who did not understand tragedy. The formula described an effect. The deeper explanation, which §12 completes, concerns a new demand for understanding.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

What, according to Nietzsche, is lost when the everyday individual takes over the tragic stage, and which part of his account would you want better evidence for?

  • Use one detail from §10 (the masks, Zagreus, or the death of myth) and one from §11 (the spectator, the Græculus, or the two spectators).
  • Separate Nietzsche's claim about artistic change from his evaluation of the audience.
  • Finish by naming the claim that most needs evidence, and saying what kind.

A successful response explains the connection between the masked god and the "counterfeit" masks Nietzsche attributes to Euripides. It states the contempt in §11 accurately, without adopting it, and keeps the historical and evaluative claims apart.
