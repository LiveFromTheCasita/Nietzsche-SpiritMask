Can We Stop Resenting the Past?

Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Essential Reading Path · Reading 6 of 10

Read: Part II, “Redemption” in full, stopping before “Manly Prudence”; then “The Stillest Hour” in full, ending at the close of Part II.
Allow: About 70–90 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann in The Portable Nietzsche. Free alternative: Redemption · The Stillest Hour (Thomas Common, Thus Spake Zarathustra).

Chapter names follow Common’s edition; see the edition guide for alternate titles. Its Roman numerals I–LXXX number chapters across the book, while the assignment above specifies parts and internal sections. If highlighting fails, locate the stated chapter and section. This guide uses overman where Common writes superman. The introduction and appended notes by other writers are distinct from Nietzsche’s text; the explanations below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Imagine recognizing that you spent years pursuing a project you now consider mistaken. You can change your next decision. You can repair some consequences. You cannot make those years not have happened. What might it mean to stop treating that unalterable fact as an enemy?

This ordinary example opens a question that Zarathustra gives a much larger scope. He confronts the past as a field of fragments, accidents, and unrealized possibilities. His proposed redemption would transform our relation to what has been. How that transformation could occur becomes the point at which his speech breaks down.

Reading 5 examined self-surpassing and the doubts a poet raises about his own images. Here the teacher's authority is challenged by a listener and by a demanding voice within a dreamlike experience. His own readiness becomes part of the philosophical problem.

Read “Redemption” through the hunchback's final question. Then read “The Stillest Hour” through Zarathustra's departure. Neither ending lets a confident declaration stand as the whole story.

The main question: Can creative willing change our relation to an irreversible past, and has Zarathustra learned how to do what he asks?

Three things to notice

  1. The people on the bridge. Who asks for healing, and how does Zarathustra answer? Follow the movement from actual disabled petitioners to his image of human incompleteness without losing the original encounter.
  2. The question that interrupts the teaching. Distinguish the will's inability to alter past events from the different relation to them that Zarathustra desires. Where does his explanation stop?
  3. The teacher who cannot yet speak. In “The Stillest Hour,” track Zarathustra's changing explanations for withholding his word. What does the whispering voice challenge, and what remains unnamed?

A way to read the passages

A request for healing receives an unsettling answer

“Redemption” begins on a bridge, where disabled people and beggars surround Zarathustra. Their spokesman argues that healing them would establish his teaching's credibility. The request tests what his promise of transformation could mean for people with immediate needs. “Redemption,” the encounter on the bridge

Zarathustra responds with sayings he attributes to the people. Removing a person's hump would remove their spirit; restoring sight could reveal unbearable things; restored mobility could release destructive desires. He invokes popular wisdom while refusing the expected demonstration of healing.

These are harsh associations between bodies, character, and supposed benefit. The scene does not supply a sound account of disabled people's lives or establish that treatment would harm them. The irony of citing popular wisdom complicates who endorses the sayings, but the petitioners still receive this answer from Zarathustra.

He then turns toward people with one enormously developed capacity and little else. A huge ear, supported by an almost negligible person, becomes his grotesque portrait of a celebrated genius. What society praises as greatness may express a distorted concentration of ability.

The contrast expands his concern from bodily impairment to a hierarchy of human development. It also borrows the language of disability to represent spiritual deficiency. Reading it as an image of narrow specialization should not erase either that hierarchy or the people whose request has been displaced.

The creator also belongs among the fragments

Zarathustra turns to his disciples in dejection. Present and past humanity appear to him as scattered limbs and fearful accidents. He can endure this spectacle because he anticipates a future in which fragments could form a unity.

His description of himself unsettles a simple division between defective people and a complete teacher. He is a creator and bridge to the future, yet also incomplete on that bridge. He offers questions about his identity: physician or healed person, liberator or subjugator, promise or fulfillment?

These alternatives matter after Reading 5. A creator of images must ask what the images are doing and whether he has achieved what he announces. Zarathustra's future gives his present a direction without making him the finished embodiment of it.

His task is to compose what seems fragmentary into a whole. Think of giving a previously scattered course of study a new direction. Earlier interests can acquire a place they did not originally possess. This illustrates creative reinterpretation, but Zarathustra's ambition reaches much further than arranging a useful personal narrative.

The will encounters something it cannot command

The chapter now identifies a limit within willing. We can undertake projects and affect what comes next. We cannot will an accomplished event out of existence. The fact that something happened remains beyond our power to change.

Zarathustra personifies this difficulty as the will's imprisonment. The liberating capacity to form purposes becomes a hostile spectator when it looks backward. It can imagine another past, but cannot produce it.

Return to the abandoned project. You might respond by learning from it, or by becoming angry at every reminder that the years cannot be recovered. In the second case, an inability to change what happened could begin shaping how you treat things and people now.

This gives a small example of the connection Zarathustra proposes. It does not establish his much larger claim that the spirit of revenge arises from the will's hostility to time and its irreversible past. A dramatic explanation of one pattern still needs scrutiny when expanded into an account of human thought.

Revenge borrows the language of justice

Zarathustra describes the imprisoned will taking revenge on whatever can suffer. It cannot reach backward to remove its frustration, so it inflicts suffering elsewhere. Revenge then calls itself punishment and claims a good conscience.

The argument concerns a possible transformation of motive: hostility becomes morally authorized when suffering is interpreted as deserved penalty. The speech escalates from particular punishment to the claim that life itself is punishment and everything that perishes deserves to perish.

Notice the speakers within the speech. Zarathustra attributes these declarations to a personified madness. Its argument about eternally recurring deed and guilt is part of the position he is attacking. It should not be extracted as his completed teaching of recurrence merely because the language anticipates a later problem.

He also rejects deliverance through the extinction of willing. His proposed alternative is creative will. He wants willing transformed, rather than life condemned for containing frustration or freedom purchased by ceasing to want anything.

A serious objection remains. Responding to an injury can aim at protection, acknowledgment, or repair. Zarathustra's diagnosis would need to distinguish these purposes from retaliatory hostility. Calling every demand for accountability revenge would avoid that work.

Redemption asks for more than accepting a fact

Zarathustra imagines the creative will transforming every past event into something it can will. Common's formulations move from “Thus would I have it” toward present and future willing. The temporal movement asks how what is already fixed could enter an affirmative purpose.

This cannot straightforwardly mean causing a different event to have occurred. The will's inability to do that is the difficulty the chapter has established. Nor is the proposal simply forgiving someone: it concerns our relation to what has happened, including chance, loss, and our own history.

One possible reading is that creation gives past material a new place within a life. The failed project might become part of a later undertaking without being described falsely as a success. This makes a limited kind of transformation intelligible.

But Zarathustra asks for something beyond reconciliation. Finding a use for an experience, accepting its irreversibility, and willing that experience are different achievements. A reader should not quietly replace the strongest demand with the easiest one.

The chapter itself keeps that gap open. Has the will ever spoken this way? Has it freed itself from revenge? Who has taught it to will backward? The questions become increasingly urgent precisely where an explanation is needed.

The listener notices what the teacher cannot resolve

Zarathustra suddenly stops. The narrator describes alarm and terror in his gaze toward the disciples. He then laughs and turns to a remark about how difficult it is to remain silent among people. The philosophical question has become a visible difficulty for its speaker.

The hunchback has been listening. He asks why Zarathustra speaks differently to the petitioners and the disciples. After receiving another bodily joke, he sharpens the question: why does Zarathustra speak differently to his disciples than to himself?

This speaker is no mere illustration of somebody else's doctrine. He perceives a discrepancy in the teacher's speech and receives the chapter's last word. The scene gives critical intelligence to a person whom Zarathustra's earlier rhetoric could encourage readers to overlook.

Different audiences may require different explanations. Yet the final question reaches beyond adaptation. Is Zarathustra withholding something, evading an unresolved problem, or giving his disciples an assurance he cannot give himself? The narrative does not select one complete explanation.

The stillest hour questions his refusal

In the second chapter, Zarathustra tells his friends why he must leave. He reports a dreamlike encounter with his personified stillest hour, which he calls his terrible mistress. A voiceless utterance insists that he knows something he has not spoken. “The Stillest Hour”

Keep the narrative levels distinct. We hear Zarathustra recounting an experience and quoting the voice within it. The voice is not simply the narrator announcing an independently established verdict. It challenges him, and its demands remain available for the reader's judgment.

His first answer is defiant refusal. Under questioning, refusal becomes inability: the task exceeds his power. He then doubts his worthiness, laments the failure of his teaching to reach people, recalls mockery, and admits shame.

The sequence examines how an objection can change when pressed. Is he protecting himself through modesty? Is he accurately recognizing a limit? Does the voice expose an evasion, or demand something for which he needs more preparation? His trembling and tears make the conflict something he suffers, rather than an abstract exercise.

A whisper challenges the image of commanding

Zarathustra says he lacks a lion's voice for command. The whisper answers that quiet words and almost inaudible thoughts can transform the world. He may be confusing the capacity to speak consequentially with an impressive performance of authority.

The voice also tells him to become a child and overcome shame. This recalls the earlier images of transformation, but the present demand is personal: the teacher must undergo something he can already describe to others.

Its image of ripe fruit and an unready bearer distinguishes having something to give from being able to give it. That does not prove that the unsaid thought is already correct. It dramatizes a gap between the teaching Zarathustra feels approaching and his capacity to voice and live it.

The content of the demanded word remains unspecified here. Later chapters bring recurrence into the foreground, but we should not insert a complete formula into this scene and declare its difficulty solved. What the chapter explicitly gives us is knowledge claimed, speech withheld, and preparation still required.

Zarathustra tells his friends that he has hidden nothing, then calls himself deeply reserved and admits that he has more to give. The tension echoes the hunchback's question. The narrator finally describes his uncontrollable weeping and solitary departure. Part II ends with an unfinished teacher leaving an unfinished explanation.

A difficulty to keep in view

What could justify asking someone to will a past that includes serious injury?

The ordinary example of a failed project helps distinguish changing a fact from changing its place in a life. It cannot establish that every loss or injury should become something a person wants. The stronger demand becomes especially difficult when one person's affirmative story absorbs another person's suffering.

Suppose someone calls an injustice necessary because it contributed to their later achievement. That interpretation may give their life coherence while denying the perspective of those injured. Creative meaning and adequate recognition of harm can diverge.

The opening encounter keeps this concern concrete. Zarathustra moves from requests for healing toward his future-oriented account of human wholeness. The petitioners' needs do not disappear because his problem seems larger. The hunchback's final intervention resists their reduction to material for his speech.

There is a difficulty about authority too. The stillest hour's command may expose avoidance, but an intense inner demand does not certify its truth. The earlier doubts about poets must apply to Zarathustra's account of his own calling.

These chapters ask how willing might escape revenge. They neither demonstrate that the escape has occurred nor provide a general rule for telling others how to receive their past. Keep the promised transformation and the unresolved justification beside one another.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does the opening encounter matter beyond its imagery?

Actual petitioners within the story ask for healing and receive a harsh answer. Zarathustra uses bodily difference to describe spiritual incompleteness, but their needs remain. Their spokesman later questions the teacher's inconsistent speech and has the chapter's last word.

What makes the will a prisoner in “Redemption”?

It cannot alter the fact that something has happened. Zarathustra describes hostility toward this limit as a source of revenge. That account is stronger than the observation that people sometimes regret events, and its broad reach needs examination.

Does redemption mean changing the facts or simply forgiving?

Neither captures the demand. Zarathustra seeks a creative willing of what has been, beyond mere reconciliation. Giving a past event new significance may illuminate part of this task, but the chapter breaks off before explaining how its strongest demand can be achieved.

Who voices the claim about eternally recurring deed and guilt?

It belongs to the madness Zarathustra quotes while criticizing revenge and the interpretation of life as punishment. Attributing that passage directly to his finished teaching would ignore the speech within the speech.

What remains unnamed in “The Stillest Hour”?

The precise content of the word Zarathustra is commanded to speak. The scene makes his refusal and unreadiness explicit, but does not formulate recurrence as a completed doctrine. His departure confirms that his own preparation remains unfinished.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 200–300 words about the gap between Zarathustra's promise and his readiness. Use an invented, ordinary disappointment if an example helps:

  1. Distinguish changing a past event, changing its significance, and willing it. Explain why these are different.
  2. Identify the point where “Redemption” stops explaining and begins asking questions it does not answer.
  3. Use the hunchback's last question to examine the teacher's authority.
  4. Connect one of Zarathustra's explanations for silence with the voice's response in “The Stillest Hour.”
  5. End with one thing the chapters would need to establish before their promised redemption became convincing.

A strong response keeps speakers distinct and preserves the unsolved problem. You do not need to turn your example into a declaration that every event was desirable.
