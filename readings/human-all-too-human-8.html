Whose Independence Counts?

Human, All Too Human — Essential Reading Path · Reading 8 of 10

Read: I §416, then I §475 (1878), followed by WS §293 (1880). Read each in full, stopping before I §417, I §476, and WS §294 respectively.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternatives: I §416 · I §475 (Helen Zimmern); WS §293 (Paul V. Cohn).

I means Volume I; AOM means Assorted Opinions and Maxims (called Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions in the free edition); WS means The Wanderer and His Shadow. Numbers restart in each collection. The numbered 1886 prefaces are separate texts. If highlighting fails, use the collection and numbered heading.

Before you read

Imagine an association devoted to independent discussion. Its organizers announce that everyone should learn to think for themselves. Then they decide that certain groups cannot be trusted to participate. Their stated reason is the protection of independent judgment.

We now have two questions. What conditions help people judge independently? Who gets to decide that someone lacks the capacity, and what follows from that decision? An attractive account of freedom can conceal a restrictive account of who may exercise it.

Reading 7 asked how psychological understanding might encourage tact and forbearance in friendship. This session moves from relations between individuals to judgments about groups and political membership. The demand for careful understanding becomes a test we must apply to Nietzsche himself.

Read the passages in order: I §416, I §475, then WS §293. They belong to the original 1878 volume and the 1880 Wanderer collection. Their differences matter. A rejection of nationalist persecution appears alongside racial stereotypes; a proposal for independence expressly restricts political participation.

The main question: What happens to an ideal of independence when its author makes unsupported judgments about who is capable of it?

Three things to notice

  1. The step from description to exclusion. In I §416, identify the alleged traits, the activities Nietzsche thinks they threaten, and the qualification at the end. Does that qualification supply evidence for the claims?
  2. The different arguments within I §475. Follow the attack on manufactured nationalism, the defense of Jews against scapegoating, and the racial and civilizational rankings. Keep all three in view.
  3. The means attached to the aim. In WS §293, list the three kinds of independence, then every restriction proposed to secure them. Read the final comparison with existing governments too.

A way to read the passages

A question that already contains its verdict

I §416 opens by asking whether women can be just. The question rests on a generalization: their supposed tendency to love and to take sides is treated as an obstacle to impartial judgment. Nietzsche moves from an account of emotional attachment to doubts about fitness for politics and parts of science, especially history. I §416, the argument about emancipation

Track that movement carefully. Caring about people, favoring a person without sufficient reason, and being unable to investigate fairly are different things. Nietzsche bundles them into a portrait of women, then uses the portrait to justify suspicion of their participation in public and intellectual life.

The aphorism also attributes a concealed contempt for science to even the women it ranks most highly. Such a claim is especially difficult to test: an apparent counterexample can be redescribed as someone whose dismissive attitude remains hidden. Psychological suspicion begins to protect the generalization against correction.

The passage provides no comparative evidence showing that these dispositions characterize women as a group or distinguish them from men. Its rhetorical assurance should not be mistaken for the result of an inquiry. We can identify both the unsupported description and the exclusion it is used to support.

The final qualification does not repair the argument

At the end, Nietzsche allows that the situation might change. That matters: the aphorism does not simply declare an unalterable female essence. Yet he immediately reasserts the present judgment. The possibility of future change leaves the current barrier intact.

Recall I §2 from the opening reading, where Nietzsche criticizes philosophers for treating the people immediately around them as humanity itself. Here we can ask what histories of education, opportunity, and social expectation his description leaves unexamined. Those questions follow his historical method; they do not require accepting his account of women.

Imagine a club that bars newcomers from its discussions, then cites their unfamiliarity with its methods as a reason to keep them out. The arrangement could help produce the condition invoked to defend it. This example identifies a possibility to investigate. It does not prove a complete history of women's intellectual opportunities or explain every claim in the aphorism.

The important task is to make the inference visible. Even evidence of unequal experience would require an argument about how to respond. It would not by itself show that excluding a group from participation is justified.

National feeling has organizers and beneficiaries

I §475 widens the scene. Nietzsche begins with commerce, travel, communication, and cultural exchange. He expects these processes to weaken European national divisions and produce an increasingly mixed population. Nationalist hostility appears as a counterforce to that movement. I §475, the account of European nationality

His explanation asks who benefits from presenting national isolation as the people's interest. He points to ruling dynasties and particular commercial and social groups. What feels like a spontaneous collective loyalty may be organized through manipulation, falsehood, and force.

This is a recognizable application of the book's method: investigate how a prestigious feeling acquires authority and whose purposes it serves. We can understand the proposed mechanism without accepting his prediction that exchange must eventually dissolve nationalities. The aphorism offers that expectation confidently; it does not establish the entire historical trajectory.

His invitation to become good Europeans therefore has a definite direction. He wants readers to assist the mixing of European peoples and resist artificially intensified national hostility. He is advocating a cultural and political project, with judgments about the kind of population it should produce.

The defense of Jews and the prejudices within it

The next part connects nationalist competition to the persecution of Jews. Nietzsche condemns making Jews responsible for public and private troubles. He identifies envy and hostility toward accumulated capacities and achievement, and he refuses the demand that Jews alone should have no objectionable qualities.

He also acknowledges collective responsibility for Jewish suffering and praises Jewish contributions to religion, philosophy, scholarship, and intellectual independence. Christ and Spinoza figure in the account, followed by scholars and physicians whom he credits with preserving enlightenment. These are substantive parts of his opposition to antisemitic scapegoating.

But the same passage treats peoples through inherited group qualities and evaluates their usefulness as ingredients in a stronger European population. It includes a hostile stereotype of a Jewish stock-market figure. The defense does not free itself from racial generalization or antisemitic imagery.

Its final historical picture adds a hierarchy between Europe and Asia. Nietzsche presents Jewish intellectual activity as helping return Europe to a Greek inheritance, while describing Christianity as having drawn it toward the East. This opposition makes his European ideal depend on a demeaning picture of Asia.

Read these features together. Praise and prejudice can coexist within one argument. Calling the passage simply tolerant would suppress the terms of belonging it imposes; calling every sentence an expression of one undifferentiated hostility would erase its explicit attack on persecution.

Acceptance on whose terms?

Ask what grounds the welcome Nietzsche extends. At one point he appeals to the unfairness of demanding exceptional faultlessness from Jews. Elsewhere he emphasizes contributions to European strength and culture. These are different reasons: equal treatment in judgment and usefulness to a collective project.

The difference matters. If a group's welcome depends on the achievements attributed to it, what happens to individuals who do not fit that favorable portrait? Replacing a hostile stereotype with an admiring stereotype can leave the habit of judging persons through assigned group characteristics untouched.

Likewise, opposing separate nationalisms does not automatically create an unrestricted ideal of human equality. Nietzsche's proposed horizon is a particular European future. We should examine its expansive movement and its boundaries without silently converting it into our preferred account of inclusion.

Pause here: Write one sentence explaining what I §475 opposes and another explaining what assumptions its opposition retains. Make both sentences specific enough to locate in the passage.

Three dimensions of independence

WS §293 begins with an account of democracy's goal: securing independence for as many people as possible in opinion, way of life, and occupation. Independence reaches across judgment, everyday conduct, and the means of living. The opening is broader than a claim about casting a vote. WS §293, the proposed goal and means

These dimensions can illuminate one another. Someone may be formally free to express an opinion while fearing that doing so will cost them their livelihood. Someone with resources may still surrender judgment to an organization. Such examples help explain why political arrangements might matter to intellectual freedom.

But Nietzsche's chosen means are severe. He says that the propertyless and the very rich must be denied the vote. He also calls for work toward eliminating these two classes and for preventing measures that aim at party organization. The passage names the poor, the rich, and parties as enemies of independence.

These restrictions are integral to the proposal. Quoting its opening while leaving them out would transform the argument. The goal of independence for many is explicitly joined to denying political voice to entire economic groups and obstructing organized political association.

The limits of the proposed means

A possible reconstruction is that Nietzsche fears dependence among those without resources and domination by those possessing too much, while parties bind judgment to collective loyalties. The aphorism itself does not develop those mechanisms. We should mark that reconstruction as an interpretation, then ask whether the proposed restrictions would address the problems.

Excluding someone from a vote does not explain how their material insecurity will end. Restricting collective organization could also remove a means by which dependent people seek greater independence. And who decides what counts as too little or too much property? The passage supplies no criteria or institutional safeguards.

Nor does its call to remove the two classes specify a workable economic program. We should not fill the gap with policies the text never names. The distance between the announced aim and the proposed means is part of what readers must assess.

The ending distinguishes this imagined future democracy from governments currently bearing that name. Nietzsche's vehicle image suggests that changing who pulls the state along may leave its route and machinery untouched. His closing question asks whether the danger has really diminished. It does not demonstrate that his own restrictions would make the journey safer.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can we use Nietzsche's demand for independent inquiry to criticize the exclusions he endorses?

Yes, provided we identify the reasons for the criticism. I §416 makes inadequately supported claims about an entire sex. I §475 combines resistance to persecution with racial stereotypes and a ranked picture of cultures. WS §293 restricts participation without demonstrating that its restrictions secure the independence it values.

These objections do more than announce disagreement. They ask about evidence, explanatory gaps, and the relation between a proposed end and its means. Those are practices the book has repeatedly encouraged.

However, a method of questioning does not itself guarantee a commitment to equal political rights. Nietzsche can oppose dependence while endorsing hierarchy and exclusion. It would be misleading to claim that the book secretly contains a fully inclusive politics once its inconsistencies are removed.

We can instead distinguish the author's commitments from the uses we make of his questions. If we defend equal participation, we should give our reasons for it. If we preserve an insight about the social conditions of independence, we should state where our conclusions depart from his.

Keep the opening association in mind. Freedom becomes a more exacting ideal when those praised as its guardians must explain who has been excluded, by what evidence, and with what opportunity to challenge the judgment.

Check your understanding

Answer first, then open each suggested response.

What does the ending of I §416 qualify, and what does it leave intact?

It allows that women's dispositions might change, so the claim is not simply one of permanent incapacity. It leaves intact the unsupported present generalization and the suspicion of women's participation in politics and science. Possible change supplies no evidence for that judgment.

Why does I §475 investigate the beneficiaries of nationalism?

Nietzsche thinks ruling dynasties and particular social and commercial groups promote national separation as though it served everyone. Identifying those interests challenges nationalism's claim to express a spontaneous, shared necessity.

How can I §475 oppose antisemitism while retaining prejudice?

It condemns Jewish scapegoating, recognizes suffering, and praises intellectual contributions. It also assigns inherited qualities to groups, includes a derogatory Jewish stereotype, and ranks Europe against Asia. The opposition and the prejudices belong to the same passage.

Which parts of WS §293 prevent an uncomplicated reading as universal political equality?

Nietzsche would withhold voting rights from the propertyless and the very rich and prevent measures promoting party organization. These restrictions accompany the threefold aim of independence in opinion, way of life, and occupation.

What remains open after identifying a tension in Nietzsche's account?

We still need to assess the arguments and defend our alternative. Showing that a restriction lacks support does not make Nietzsche an advocate of equal participation, and adopting his questions does not require adopting his political conclusions.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 180–250 words about the gap between an ideal and its conditions of membership. Begin with one precise claim from this reading:

  1. State the independence or fairness the passage values. Identify who receives it, who is questioned or excluded, and the stated reason.
  2. Separate the description of a group from the conclusion drawn about its treatment. What evidence or further argument would be needed to connect them?
  3. Use a second assigned aphorism to sharpen the problem. You might compare I §416's presumed incapacity with WS §293's presumed dependence, or I §475's opposition to persecution with its conditions of acceptance.
  4. Give one reason for your own judgment. Explain which question you retain from Nietzsche and where your answer departs from his.

A successful response preserves the passage's actual restrictions and names its reasoning gap. Avoid making either a favorable sentence or a prejudiced sentence stand for the whole text.
