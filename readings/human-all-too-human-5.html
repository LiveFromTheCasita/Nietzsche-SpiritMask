What Makes a Genius?

Human, All Too Human — Essential Reading Path · Reading 5 of 10

Read: I §§155, 162, and 222 (1878), then WS §140 (1880). Read each in full, stopping before I §156, I §163, I §223, and WS §141 respectively.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternatives: I §155 · I §162 · I §222 (Helen Zimmern); WS §140 (Paul V. Cohn).

I means Volume I; AOM means Assorted Opinions and Maxims (called Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions in the free edition); WS means The Wanderer and His Shadow. Numbers restart in each collection. The numbered 1886 prefaces are separate texts. If highlighting fails, use the collection and numbered heading.

Before you read

You hear a musician perform a difficult piece with apparent ease. Nothing makes you count the abandoned attempts, repeated exercises, or decisions about phrasing. Calling the player a genius can express admiration while making the work behind the performance disappear.

Nietzsche asks us to look in two directions: toward the artist’s activity and toward the spectator’s investment in calling that activity extraordinary. The explanation we accept may protect the artist’s prestige and the audience’s self-esteem at once. That possibility does not make the music less accomplished.

Reading 4 questioned the inference from losing a consoling belief to devaluing life. This reading makes the problem concrete for art. If we abandon supernatural inspiration and the artist’s special access to ultimate reality, what remains worth admiring?

Read the three Volume I aphorisms from 1878, then WS §140 from The Wanderer and His Shadow, published in 1880. They investigate making, admiration, art’s continuing effects, and the relation between invention and constraint.

The main question: Can explaining artistic achievement make our admiration more exact, while preserving what art has taught us to value?

Three things to notice

  1. Production and judgment. In I §155, imagination produces material of different quality. Which activities turn this supply into an accomplished work?
  2. The spectator’s advantage. In I §162, calling someone a genius can protect the admirer’s vanity. Notice why a finished work encourages this interpretation.
  3. What training makes possible. I §222 asks what capacities art leaves us; WS §140 asks how artists learn to work within constraints and invent new ones. Look for achievements that a history of their formation need not diminish.

A way to read the passages

I §155: the work concealed by inspiration

The aphorism starts with an interest: artists benefit when people believe that a work arrived through sudden inspiration. An apparently miraculous beginning sets its maker apart. Nietzsche includes poets and philosophers alongside other artists, so an arresting philosophical thought belongs within the inquiry too. I §155, inspiration and selection

His alternative is a division of activity. Imagination continually supplies possibilities: good, mediocre, and bad. Practiced judgment rejects, selects, combines, and reshapes them. The important distinction lies between having material and making something accomplished from it.

Think of writing a paragraph. One opening is vague, another impressive but misleading, and a third becomes useful after revision. The final paragraph conceals those decisions. This ordinary example illustrates Nietzsche’s account of selection without equating the achievement with composing a masterpiece.

Nietzsche points to Beethoven’s notebooks as evidence of beautiful melodies developing through different attempts. His point concerns the discrepancy between the finished result and its history. A melody that sounds inevitable can have emerged from repeated choices among alternatives.

He also ranks improvisation below carefully selected artistic thought, associating some improvisation with less exact discrimination and the reuse of remembered material. That judgment is stronger than the claim that preparation matters. It places laborious selection above an art of immediate performance, a hierarchy we should register rather than silently generalize into a neutral account of creativity.

The closing insistence on work includes rejection and rearrangement as well as invention. A sudden idea may occur within the process. What Nietzsche resists is allowing the experience of suddenness to replace an explanation of the achievement.

I §162: why an audience might want a miracle

The next aphorism shifts from what benefits artists to what benefits admirers. We think well of ourselves yet recognize that we could not readily produce a Raphael painting or a Shakespeare scene. The discrepancy could threaten our self-estimation. Declaring the creator miraculously different removes the comparison. I §162, admiration and vanity

On this account, extravagant praise can provide an exemption. A person touched by divine power cannot reasonably become our rival. Their achievement leaves our ordinary picture of ourselves undisturbed because it belongs to a different order.

Imagine admiring someone’s fluency in a language while calling it a gift they simply possess. If you then learn about years of study, your admiration may acquire an uncomfortable comparison with your own abandoned attempts. Nietzsche invites us to ask whether calling the ability inexplicable sometimes protects us from that comparison. The example is illustrative; it does not imply that every admirer secretly envies.

His alternative joins the artist to inventors, astronomers, historians, and strategists. These people concentrate activity in particular directions, draw material from what they encounter, observe themselves and others, and persist in using and developing their means. Artistic achievement remains extraordinarily complex. Its complexity no longer establishes a supernatural exception.

The image of laying stones and learning to build makes development central. A completed structure depends on acquired operations and accumulated material. This explanation does not say that everyone has identical capacities or would reach identical results with equal hours of effort. Those propositions would require arguments the aphorism does not give.

Why completion hides becoming

Nietzsche then asks why the title of genius attaches especially to certain kinds of activity. We readily contemplate an accomplished representation without seeing it emerge. Its present completeness fills our attention, and the process recedes.

Compare seeing a finished drawing with watching someone repeatedly erase and reposition a hand. The drawing’s effect may encourage the thought that its maker simply saw what to do. Watching corrections brings judgment and uncertainty into view. Nietzsche thinks this visible development often lowers spectators’ estimation, even though it can reveal precisely what the artist achieved.

The account connects to I §155. The artist’s interest in inspiration and the spectator’s interest in exemption can reinforce each other. Nobody has to invent a deliberate conspiracy: a finished work and a flattering explanation already make the concealed process easy to overlook.

Nietzsche’s criticism also targets the corresponding undervaluation of scientific work. If we call one complicated activity miraculous and regard another coolly because its procedures are more visible, our standards reflect selective fascination. Understanding development should change what we praise, rather than automatically reducing the praise available.

I §222: the authority art loses

The third aphorism asks a larger question. Art once enjoyed additional value through metaphysical beliefs. Nietzsche names the belief in an unchanging character through which the world’s enduring essence appears. Under that assumption, an artistic portrayal could capture something permanent about reality. I §222, what remains of art

His opposing account stresses historical change, including change within individuals. A work’s portrayal has a situated and temporary validity. It need not disclose a fixed human essence to tell us something worth attending to about people.

A second metaphysical picture treats the visible world as appearance and assigns art a privileged relation to underlying forms or models. Nietzsche reconstructs how this could elevate art above ordinary nature, then rejects the assumptions. We should not mistake his explanation of art’s former authority for his endorsement of it.

The question that follows is therefore substantial: after these supposed grounds of exceptional value fail, what position can art retain? His answer turns from knowledge of an eternal reality to the formation of human feeling and attention.

What an education in art can leave behind

Nietzsche says art has taught people to approach life in its many forms with interest and pleasure, carrying feeling as far as affirming life whatever form it takes. It cultivates the capacity to regard human life as part of nature and contemplate its development. He claims this education has grown into a powerful need for knowledge.

Imagine reading a novel that makes a difficult character’s conflicting motives interesting. Later, that attention may enter how you listen to people. This illustrates a capacity outlasting the occasion that developed it. The passage advances a much broader historical claim about art’s education of feeling.

Nietzsche compares this possibility with religion. Giving up religious belief need not mean losing the elevation and intensification of feeling acquired through religious life. Similarly, even if art disappeared, the range of enjoyments it helped develop could continue to demand satisfaction. Origin and continued exercise are separable.

The claim should not become a command to abandon art. Nietzsche imagines its renunciation to ask what would remain. Nor does he offer an uncomplicated defense of art’s permanent indispensability: his closing claim places the scientific person as a further development of the artistic person. Inquiry inherits a cultivated interest in existence.

That ending changes how we connect these readings. Art may have been credited with privileged knowledge it did not possess while also helping form a capacity that genuine inquiry needs. Explaining away the privilege does not explain away the education. Whether scientific inquiry can fully satisfy that inherited capacity remains an open difficulty.

WS §140: invention inside a discipline

The later aphorism returns to how art is made. Nietzsche describes Greek artists working within inherited conventions and creating additional constraints that others find attractive enough to imitate. Invention can consist in introducing a new rule for what the artist must accomplish. WS §140, artistic constraint

The image of dancing in chains joins restriction to movement. Artistic freedom here involves an acquired ability to do something graceful under demanding conditions. Nietzsche’s Homer already works with inherited formulas and narrative conventions, while creating conventions for successors. Innovation has a past and makes a future discipline possible.

For a small illustration, try describing a room in six sentences without using a color word. The restriction might make you attend to texture, distance, or sound. If you find an expressive solution, its achievement depends partly on what the rule made difficult. The exercise cannot establish Nietzsche’s broad picture of Greek art, but it makes the proposed relation between limitation and invention recognizable.

The ending includes both apparent ease and admired difficulty. Artists conceal effort through accomplished movement while allowing constraint and its overcoming to be perceived. That is a useful tension to retain: recognizing the form’s demands is different from seeing every failed attempt.

Read this beside I §§155 and 162. A performance may hide its labor without having a miraculous origin. Once we understand some of its constraints, we may admire the particular achievement more precisely. Explanation can return us to the work with better attention.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does Nietzsche’s correction of the genius myth become a new simplification?

Practice and selection are indispensable to his account, but the aphorisms do not establish that every difference in achievement is a difference in effort. Access to teaching, time, materials, and collaborators also changes what people can develop. So do differences in capacity and circumstance. A reader who turns these passages into the assurance that anyone can become Shakespeare through sufficient work has supplied a doctrine they do not demonstrate.

The suspicion of vanity also needs limits. People can recognize exceptional work without using it to protect their own self-esteem. Nietzsche gives us a motive to investigate; its plausibility in some cases does not prove that it governs every act of admiration.

I §155’s low ranking of improvisation invites a specific objection. An improvised performance can involve extensively trained judgment acting during the performance itself. If judgment and preparation are what matter, speed alone may not justify the hierarchy. That question tests his account from within its own emphasis on acquired capacities.

Finally, I §222’s inheritance story leaves open whether inquiry can satisfy everything art has taught people to want. Learning to investigate motives does not obviously replace listening to a melody or experiencing a dramatic form. Nietzsche’s claim about continuing capacities is powerful even if we question his confidence about their destination. Keep both the proposed inheritance and the possible loss in view.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What produces an accomplished work in I §155?

Imagination produces possibilities of varying quality; practiced judgment selects, rejects, combines, and transforms them. Nietzsche emphasizes the work that a finished result conceals. A sudden idea may occur, but its suddenness does not explain the full achievement.

How can admiration protect vanity in I §162?

Calling an artist miraculously different removes them from ordinary comparison. The admirer can praise them without feeling challenged by their achievement. Nietzsche treats this as one reason the genius cult appeals to its audience, alongside the advantage artists gain from it.

Why does seeing a work’s development matter?

A finished work directs attention to present perfection and can conceal the choices behind it. Visible development makes acquired procedures easier to recognize. Nietzsche questions the tendency to undervalue this process while treating its concealed outcome as evidence of a miracle.

What remains after I §222 rejects art’s metaphysical authority?

The capacities art has helped cultivate can remain: interest in life, a richer range of enjoyment, and attention to human development. Nietzsche sees the desire for knowledge as an inheritor of that education. He imagines art’s disappearance without establishing that inquiry could replace every artistic experience.

How can a new constraint count as invention in WS §140?

An artist can devise a formal demand and make fulfilling it attractive enough for others to imitate. The achievement involves inherited discipline, additional difficulty, and an accomplished response. Freedom appears as movement within demanding conditions, rather than the absence of all conditions.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one made thing you admire: a song, drawing, speech, meal, or piece of writing. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Describe the achievement precisely. Replace a general label such as “genius” with something the work succeeds in doing.
  2. Identify likely acts of selection or revision. Distinguish what you know about its making from what you are inferring.
  3. Name a constraint its maker worked within. Explain how it might have contributed to the result, using WS §140.
  4. Ask whether calling the maker exceptional changes what you expect of yourself. A negative answer is allowed, but give a reason.
  5. Using I §222, describe one capacity the work might cultivate that could matter beyond this encounter with it.

A strong response makes admiration more specific without pretending that a plausible account of the process explains every feature of the achievement.
