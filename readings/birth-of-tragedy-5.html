Why Tragedy Dies

Essential Reading Path · Reading 5 of 8

Read: §12 of The Birth of Tragedy
Allow: About 45–60 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Open the reading: §12

Read §12 in full, stopping at the heading 13. In the free edition, the link opens at page 94, just above the heading 12. The translation is William A. Haussmann’s; follow the same section number in your own edition.

The opening refers to two spectators introduced in §11: Euripides, imagined watching earlier tragedy critically, and a second spectator whom Nietzsche now identifies as Socrates. You can read §11 as an optional bridge. Today’s argument concerns the standard of judgment these figures bring into the theater.

Before you read

Imagine a director stopping a rehearsal. Why does the chorus sing here? Why does the hero do that? What happened before this scene? The director asks for clearer motives, fuller explanations, and a story whose pieces fit together.

Those requests sound reasonable. They might make a confusing play better. Nietzsche wants us to consider a harder possibility: the director’s idea of improvement might remove the source of the play’s power.

Our earlier readings followed tragedy’s union of a visible, individual hero with the Dionysian experience expressed through the chorus. Now Nietzsche describes an artistic revolution. Euripides submits inherited tragedy to conscious examination. Behind him stands Socrates, whom Nietzsche makes the representative of a new demand: beauty must answer to understanding.

The main question: What might art lose when every part of it must justify itself through an explanation?

Read §12 before continuing. Mark an example where Nietzsche explains what Euripides changes, and another where he judges the change. Keeping those tasks distinct will help you assess his case.

What to notice

  1. The new opposition. Why does Nietzsche name the Dionysian and the Socratic as opponents here? What prevents us from simply replacing “Socratic” with “Apollonian”?
  2. The prologue. Why might giving away the story help an audience feel a play more intensely? Notice the connection between explanation and emotion.
  3. The inconvenient play. What does Nietzsche do with Euripides’ Bacchae, a play in which Dionysus proves impossible to overcome?

A standard that decides what counts as art

Nietzsche summarizes aesthetic Socratism with the rule “to be beautiful everything must be intelligible.” The word everything does much of the work. Language, characters, dramatic structure, and choral music all face the same examination. An element that cannot make itself understood becomes a candidate for correction. §12, aesthetic Socratism

The issue is the authority given to this test. A spectator can ask what a scene means while remaining open to the force of its rhythm, images, and unresolved tensions. Nietzsche’s Euripides gives conscious understanding the final say over what belongs in the work. The play is rebuilt around that judgment.

Connect this change to Reading 3. There the chorus helped generate the dramatic vision. Here the whole artwork answers to an inspecting intelligence. Ask what happens if the activity that gives birth to the drama cannot explain itself in the terms its inspector requires.

Why an explained play can be intensely emotional

Euripides’ prologue provides Nietzsche’s concrete example. A speaker tells the audience who the characters are and what has happened. Nietzsche imagines a modern playwright objecting that this destroys suspense. His answer is revealing: the intended effect lies in the great scenes of suffering and argument. Knowing the background lets the audience attend fully to them. §12, the prologue; discussion continues on page 99

Imagine watching a family quarrel without knowing who anyone is. You spend your attention solving the relationships. A short explanation might free you to feel the conflict. Nietzsche understands the strength of that theatrical calculation, even while criticizing what it serves.

He also describes a god guaranteeing the opening information and, at the end, settling the characters’ future. The familiar term deus ex machina names this divine intervention. In his account, assurances frame the dramatic present: the audience can trust the story’s background and its eventual settlement. §12, divine guarantees

These devices show why “reason versus emotion” would misread the passage. Explanation can prepare an audience for powerful feeling. The question is which kind of feeling the revised drama produces, and whether it still needs the Dionysian experience Nietzsche has described.

The Apollonian is more than tidy reasoning

Removing Dionysus might seem to leave Apollo in sole command. Nietzsche explicitly tests that possibility. He imagines a drama based on epic poetry, whose images offer pleasure even when the events are terrible. Yet he then argues that Euripides fails to achieve this Apollonian alternative. §12, the proposed Apollonian drama

Recall the dreamer in Reading 1: absorbed in vivid appearances while retaining some distance from them. Nietzsche contrasts that artistic relation with a performer who shares the character’s agitation directly. Euripides combines calculated construction with forceful passion; the calm shaping power of Apollo and the dissolution of individuality through Dionysus are both displaced. §12, thoughts and passions

This is why the new opposition matters. Socratic understanding is a demand placed upon art, whereas the Apollonian is one of the powers through which art creates experience. Clear form and an explanatory argument can cooperate, but they perform different work.

A useful test: could you explain a scene’s causes perfectly yet miss its artistic effect? Conversely, could an image have a precise, compelling form while leaving its meaning unsettled? Your answers help identify the distinction Nietzsche needs.

The god returns inside the alleged destroyer’s play

Early in the section, Nietzsche faces an awkward example. Euripides’ Bacchae depicts Dionysus overwhelming his opposition. Nietzsche reads it as the aging poet’s recognition that the Dionysian cannot simply be abolished. He nevertheless declares that the reversal arrives too late: the artistic tendency Euripides helped establish has already won. §12, Nietzsche’s reading of the Bacchae

Treat this as an interpretation of the play and its author. The claim that Euripides recants requires support; the presence of a powerful Dionysus does not by itself tell us everything the dramatist believed. Nietzsche’s reference to suicide belongs to his dramatic account of an artist overturning his own tendency and its accompanying image of someone falling from a tower. It is not a report that Euripides killed himself.

When Nietzsche calls Euripides a mask for Socrates, he makes a cultural force into a character. The image is vivid: a different voice speaks through the theater. Assess the explanatory work of the image. Which features of the plays does it illuminate, and which does it encourage us to overlook?

A difficulty

Has Nietzsche explained tragedy’s death, or defined a changed kind of tragedy out of existence?

His argument combines a judgment about artistic value with a claim about historical change. He identifies genuine questions for comparison: how a prologue works, how characters argue, and what the chorus contributes. But a changed technique becomes evidence of destruction only if his account of tragedy’s essential power is convincing.

Use the Bacchae to press the difficulty. Its place in §12 could support his story of a belated reversal. It could also make you question whether his category of “Euripidean tendency” accommodates the work he is discussing. What evidence would let us decide between those readings?

You do not need to settle Greek theater history today. Write two separate judgments: one about the philosophical cost of requiring intelligibility, another about the evidence Nietzsche offers for assigning that cost to Euripides. A persuasive answer to the first question does not automatically settle the second.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does the prologue matter to an argument about tragedy’s death?

It makes Nietzsche’s account of aesthetic Socratism concrete. The audience receives enough background to follow the later scenes without uncertainty about their premises. Nietzsche takes this arrangement as evidence that conscious intelligibility now governs the drama. The example does not establish his conclusion by itself: we still have to ask why this way of organizing attention undermines tragic experience.

Why are Euripides’ intense passions not automatically Dionysian?

For Nietzsche, the Dionysian concerns the loosening of individuality and the experience expressed through music and the chorus. A character’s vivid fear or anger can leave the spectator attending to that particular individual. Euripides’ combination of reasoning and emotional excitement therefore differs from the union of artistic drives developed earlier in the book.

What question does the Bacchae create for Nietzsche’s explanation?

A play by the supposed opponent of Dionysus represents that god’s overwhelming power. Nietzsche interprets it as a late reversal that cannot undo an established cultural change. A reader can ask whether the play supports that account or exposes an overly simple picture of Euripides. Identifying the difficulty is more useful than assuming the play proves either side.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

Does making every part of a play intelligible necessarily make it less tragic?

Begin by explaining what Nietzsche means by the demand for intelligibility. Use the prologue as your main example: describe the problem it solves for the spectator and the kind of experience it encourages. Then make the strongest case you can for Nietzsche’s objection. Explain what he believes the drama loses, drawing on the earlier account of the chorus or the distinction between Apollonian images and Socratic explanation.

End with one reason to question his conclusion. You might challenge the necessity of the loss, or ask whether the Bacchae complicates his account of Euripides. Keep the objection specific enough that evidence from a play could help answer it.

A successful response distinguishes an artistic technique from Nietzsche’s judgment of it, explains why strong emotion alone does not settle the question, and gives a reason for its own conclusion. You may agree, disagree, or remain undecided.
