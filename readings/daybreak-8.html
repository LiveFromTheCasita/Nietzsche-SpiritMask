Must We Admire Power?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 8 of 10

Read: Book IV, §262, then Book V, §548. Read each in full, stopping before §263 and §549 respectively.
Allow: About 45–60 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §262 · §548 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Think of someone whose confidence fills a room. People listen when they speak. They can secure agreement, direct other people's efforts, and make disagreement uncomfortable. Their effectiveness may be obvious before anyone asks whether their judgment deserves trust.

Now imagine the same person abandoning a favorite proposal because another is better supported. That moment might attract less attention than a successful speech. Yet it could reveal something the speech concealed: the ability to make a powerful capacity answer to a reason.

Reading 7 examined how work can consume the conditions of independence, and how Nietzsche's proposed departures can retain exclusions of their own. Here we investigate another obstacle: our attraction to power, both as something to possess and as something to admire.

Read §262 in Book IV, then §548 in Book V. These passages from Daybreak concern the desire for power and its evaluation. Begin with what the 1881 text says, without treating every reference to power as a statement of Nietzsche's later philosophy.

The main question: What would make power worthy of admiration, beyond its ability to impress us or prevail?

Three things to notice

  1. A desire that survives satisfaction. In §262, what can people possess and still find insufficient? Notice the reverse case too: what happens when other goods are taken away?
  2. The difference between strength and its direction. In §548, identify the proposed standard for assessing power. What must the powerful capacity become an instrument of?
  3. The achievement spectators miss. Follow the movement from visible works to the maker's work on themselves. Why might this achievement leave little public evidence?

A way to read the passages

The desire that comfort does not answer

Section 262 begins with a strong claim about human motivation. Nietzsche places the love of power beneath persistent discontent. Providing food, shelter, health, and enjoyment does not, in his picture, necessarily satisfy the person receiving them. The demand for power remains. §262, the unsatisfied demand

His demon is a personification of that restless demand. It waits despite the satisfaction of other wants. The image makes power-seeking appear insistent and difficult to dismiss, rather than a luxury people pursue only after their ordinary needs have been met.

Imagine a participant whose practical needs are accommodated in a shared project. They still resent having no influence over its decisions. That resentment might concern control, recognition, or a justified objection to the arrangement. We would need to investigate. The example shows why comfort and a sense of agency are distinguishable; it does not prove that a single desire explains every complaint.

Nietzsche makes a much broader claim than this example establishes. His account of humanity as a whole provides no comparative evidence separating the love of power from the other causes of dissatisfaction. The force of the image should prompt that investigation.

What people may surrender for power

The second movement reverses the first. Nietzsche imagines other goods removed while the demand for power is satisfied. People might then be nearly happy. The reversal presents power as capable of compensating for losses that otherwise seem decisive.

Think of someone accepting inconvenience and reduced comfort to have the final say in an undertaking. The trade-off could reveal how highly they value control. It could also have other explanations: responsibility for others, an absorbing interest, or a belief that their decision is necessary. The claim about power gains support only if those possibilities are examined.

The word nearly matters. The passage does not offer mastery as a guaranteed cure for unhappiness. Its final reference to human beings and demons preserves an uneasy tone. Satisfying the demand need not abolish the restless character of the creature making it.

Nietzsche closes with Luther's hymn, whose speaker imagines losing life, possessions, honor, and family while a kingdom remains. The remaining kingdom makes those devastating losses appear secondary. Nietzsche seizes on it as the sign that a relation to power survives the apparent surrender of everything else.

This is the aphorism's concrete provocation: religious renunciation may preserve the very demand it seems to overcome. The claim concerns the ordering of attachments, rather than simply accusing the believer of secretly wanting ordinary comforts. Yet Nietzsche's interpretation of the hymn still needs assessment. The presence of royal language does not by itself establish every motive of those who sing it.

Explaining an attraction is not endorsing it

The aphorism asks us to recognize the possible strength of a desire. It does not follow that gratifying that desire is always justified or that everyone ought to seek domination. A powerful motive and a good reason for action remain different things.

This distinction recalls Reading 1. Section 103 separated the reality of moral motivation from the truth of the judgments that guide it. Here we can likewise distinguish an explanation of why someone wants power from an assessment of what they want to do with it.

We should also resist turning the aphorism into an all-purpose diagnosis. If someone's denial of wanting power automatically counts as proof of a concealed desire for it, the account becomes difficult to test. An inquiry needs possibilities that could count against its favored explanation.

Carry these distinctions into §548. Its question concerns the standards of admiration. Even if power fascinates us, what makes the fascination deserved?

The ease of manufacturing a genius

Section 548 opens by questioning the historical worship of exceptional intellect. Nietzsche thinks it takes disturbingly little intellect to feel superior to much that has been revered as genius. That judgment makes the history of reverence itself look intellectually poor. The height of someone's reputation may tell us something about the audience's standard. §548, the assessment of greatness

This is an attack on an inference: because a person has been acclaimed as a genius, their powers must merit the acclaim. Reputation does not establish the quality of judgment exercised by either the admired person or the admirers.

Nietzsche's own historical generalization is severe and insufficiently demonstrated here. We can question it while taking seriously the more precise problem it exposes. What achievement are we admiring, and what comparison or criterion supports our admiration?

The criticism then turns toward the worship of power. Nietzsche describes kneeling before it as an inherited habit of subservience. The language challenges the spectator as much as the powerful person. We may surrender our judgment at exactly the moment we think ourselves most perceptive about greatness.

Power must answer to something

Nietzsche proposes that the degree of reason within a power should determine its claim to reverence. He asks how far the power has been overcome by something higher and made to serve as an instrument. The decisive question becomes what governs the capacity.

Consider an eloquent speaker. They can win an audience with a misleading simplification. They can also discard that advantage, acknowledge an objection, and reshape the argument. Their ability to persuade remains real in both cases; its direction and governance differ.

The second performance makes a capacity answerable to reasons rather than using success as its own justification. That example helps illuminate Nietzsche's proposed standard. It does not settle what every higher purpose should be, or establish a complete ethics of influence.

The title's victory over power is therefore not exhausted by defeating another powerful person. One can win such a contest while continuing to worship the victor's strength. Nietzsche redirects attention to the mastery through which a capacity becomes usable rather than simply commanding its possessor.

He retains a language of greatness and hierarchy. The passage does not replace ranking with a claim that every achievement is equal. It disputes the usual evidence for rankings and asks readers to look for a less spectacular form of superiority.

The maker becomes part of the work

The final movement identifies that achievement. A genius can expend power upon a public work, but also upon their own formation. Nietzsche names self-control, the refinement of imagination, and the ordering and selection of inspirations and tasks.

Each example concerns discrimination. An inspiration's intensity does not make it worth following. Having many possible projects does not decide which deserve sustained attention. A creator's power may become more capable through refusing an attractive possibility.

Imagine a writer removing the passage most likely to win applause because it distorts the argument. The missing passage cannot display the effort of removing it. Likewise, a task wisely declined leaves no finished object announcing the decision. This is one reason the work Nietzsche values could remain publicly obscure.

His distant-star image emphasizes that invisibility. Songs and reputations gather around what becomes visible, while self-formation may go unwitnessed. The closing claim that the hierarchy of great people remains undetermined challenges established rankings rather than handing us a finished replacement.

Pause here: Name one impressive capacity and one occasion on which its possessor would have reason to restrain it. What would distinguish thoughtful restraint from fear or simple inability?

A difficulty to keep in view

How could we distinguish admirable self-command from another successful exercise of power?

Control alone cannot settle the question. A person might organize their imagination and suppress distractions in pursuit of a cruel project. Efficiency and discipline do not automatically make that project worthy.

Section 548 supplies a direction through its emphasis on reason and on making power serve something higher. It does not explain every standard by which purposes should be assessed. We must still ask what the discipline serves, what reasons support that purpose, and who bears its costs.

There is also a problem of evidence. If the finest achievement is largely invisible, we should not treat a lack of visible evidence as proof that it occurred. A famous person's imagined inner struggle is no more automatically established than their reputation for genius.

We can instead seek specific indications: a decision revised, an argument improved, a tempting advantage refused. Even then, motives may remain mixed, and restraint in one setting does not establish admirable character in every setting.

Remember also §109 from Reading 5, where intellect serves a contest among drives. That account sits uneasily beside §548's demand that power answer to reason. We should not quietly install an independent rational ruler inside the self. How reasoned self-command could develop within such a contest remains a question; the two passages do not resolve it into a finished theory.

Check your understanding

Answer before opening each suggested response.

What does the reversal in §262 add to its opening?

The opening says that other goods can leave the desire for power unsatisfied. The reversal says that satisfying this desire might partly compensate for losing those goods. Luther's imagined losses leave the kingdom intact. Together these cases portray its strength, without providing evidence that it explains every human motivation.

Does the strength of a desire establish that we ought to satisfy it?

No. Explaining why a desire moves someone and evaluating the desired action are different tasks. Section 262's psychological claim does not by itself justify domination or provide a rule for conduct.

Why does §548 question the audience's standard of greatness?

Nietzsche thinks exceptional reputations can arise against a poor intellectual standard and become customary objects of worship. The existence of admiration is therefore something to investigate, rather than evidence that the admired capacity has been properly evaluated.

What distinguishes victory over power from victory over a rival?

Defeating a rival can leave the worship of strength intact. Section 548 asks whether a powerful capacity has become answerable to reason and a higher direction, so that its possessor can employ, order, and restrain it.

Why might self-formation receive little recognition?

Selection, restraint, and the revision of tasks may leave fewer visible traces than finished works. Nietzsche thinks this can distort rankings of greatness. But invisibility alone does not prove that admirable self-command has occurred.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 180–250 words about a capacity you admire: eloquence, organizational ability, artistic inventiveness, or another definite example.

  1. Describe what the capacity enables its possessor to do. Separate observable effects from your assumptions about their motives.
  2. Use §262 to ask what satisfaction exercising it might provide. Identify an alternative explanation that would need consideration.
  3. Use §548 to describe a situation in which restraining or redirecting that capacity would reveal better judgment.
  4. State the reason for your evaluation. What purpose should the capacity serve, and why?
  5. Identify the evidence you would need before attributing admirable self-command to this person.

A successful response distinguishes effectiveness, attraction, and justified admiration. It gives reasons for the proposed restraint rather than treating every refusal to exercise power as a victory.
