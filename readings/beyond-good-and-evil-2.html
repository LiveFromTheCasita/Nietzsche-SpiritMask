Before you read

We visit §36 early because it extends this session’s argument about willing. Read the selections from Part I first, then move to Part II for that experiment.

The first session asked what a philosopher’s commitments reveal about the philosopher. Now Nietzsche questions the apparent owner of those commitments. Keep three claims separate in your notes: what introspection establishes, how an action gets accomplished, and what the whole world might consist of. Moving between them requires arguments.

The main question: If thinking and willing involve several interacting processes, what justifies treating the self as their single, independent cause?

Three things to notice

  1. A word can conceal a problem In §§16–19, mark where ordinary grammar makes an activity seem simpler than Nietzsche thinks it is.
  2. Command depends on obedience In §19, distinguish the experience of giving a command from the processes that successfully carry it out.
  3. Track the conditions In §36, list what Nietzsche asks us to suppose and what would have to succeed before the final conclusion followed.

A way to read the passages

The self as an organization: §12

Nietzsche attacks the picture of a soul that is indivisible, permanent, and independent of bodily change. His comparison with atomism concerns this demand for an ultimate, simple unit. Rejecting that demand does not, he says, require abandoning every use of the word soul. He proposes alternatives in which the self is multiple and organized: drives and affects stand in relations to one another.

This changes the question from ‘Which inner object is the real me?’ to ‘How are these tendencies organized into this person?’ It opens a program of investigation; it does not establish a complete psychology. A collection of competing desires still needs an account of memory, continuity, and coordination before it explains a person.

What introspection assumes: §§16–17

When someone identifies an experience as thinking, §16 asks how that identification was made. It involves distinctions between thinking, feeling, and willing, together with assumptions about activity and agency. Nietzsche’s target is the claim that self-observation provides knowledge without interpretation. Even this apparently intimate knowledge brings concepts with it.

Section 17 adds that thoughts often arrive without being summoned. That complicates the picture of an ego directing every mental event. Replacing the ego with an unspecified thinker does not settle the matter: grammar still invites us to place a producer behind the production. Nietzsche has challenged an inference from a sentence’s structure to reality’s structure. He has not demonstrated that thoughts occur without any conditions or that persons do not exist.

Why willing feels simple: §19

Nietzsche separates several elements in willing: sensations of moving from one condition toward another, bodily sensations, a directing thought, and the affect of commanding. The apparent unity of willing conceals this complexity. A successful action also involves obedience, resistance, and execution. Because the same person commands and obeys, the commanding side can take credit for the whole result.

Consider a musician beginning a difficult phrase—our example. The intention to begin depends on practiced movements, attention, hearing, and habits that may cooperate or fail. The intention feels effective partly because those capacities already exist. Nietzsche’s political comparison makes the attribution visible: a ruler claims the success of an organized community. This illuminates agency, although calling bodily processes commanders and subordinates remains a model needing further explanation.

Responsibility without self-creation: §21

The strongest version of free will would make a person ultimately responsible for being the source of an action, independently of inheritance, circumstances, and prior formation. Nietzsche objects that this requires being one’s own cause: causa sui. You would have to create the self that does the creating. Yet he also rejects an opposing picture of an unfree will crushed by causality, treating it as another projection of human concepts into reality.

His proposed contrast is between strong and weak wills. Read beside §19, this suggests differences in the capacity to organize and execute, rather than independence from every condition; that connection is our interpretation. Nietzsche also suspects emotional interests behind both positions: wanting complete credit or wanting release from blame. Identifying such motives can explain an attraction to a theory, but it cannot by itself refute the theory.

Three uses of power: §§13, 22–23

Section 13 proposes that living activity primarily expresses strength, with preservation often following as a result. Nietzsche presents will to power as an alternative to treating survival as the basic aim. Section 23 applies that proposal to psychology: drives conventionally condemned as bad may contribute to capacities we admire. Moral discomfort should not decide in advance which explanations an investigator may consider.

Section 22 moves to the interpretation of nature. Nietzsche suspects that language about natural laws borrows an ideal of equality before law. He imagines a competing account in which forces express their power without obeying a legislator. He explicitly accepts that his competing account is an interpretation. Notice the three tasks: explaining life, studying drives, and interpreting nature. Success at one would not automatically establish the others.

How far the hypothesis goes: §36

Section 36 asks whether the world of drives could provide a model for understanding mechanical processes. Nietzsche proposes trying one kind of causality before multiplying kinds. If willing is causally effective, if drive activity can be explained through will to power, and if organic functions can be understood on that basis, a universal interpretation might become available. The conclusion has metaphysical scope, but the passage does not prove its conditions.

This creates a demanding connection with §§19 and 21. The will used in the experiment cannot simply be the transparent, indivisible faculty those sections criticized. Nor does the desire for one explanation establish that one explanation works. Keep §36 as an ambitious hypothesis whose explanatory reach must be earned. Turning its last sentence into an unconditional slogan removes the work the preceding sentences require.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does explaining the self through power repeat the projection Nietzsche criticized in the Stoics?

It might. Nietzsche’s account gains no immunity because he calls it an interpretation. His language of command could project his preference for hierarchy into psychology, then project that psychology into nature. Section 22 acknowledges the interpretive status; §36 exposes several assumptions. Neither move supplies the missing evidence.

The productive question is comparative: what does this account explain better than its rivals, and what would count against it? An account that redescribes every possible result as power risks becoming impossible to challenge. Retain the specific insights about willing while testing whether the larger explanation adds anything.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

What does §12 retain after rejecting an indivisible soul?

The possibility of understanding a self as a changing organization of multiple drives and affects.

Why does §17 question the grammar of thinking?

A grammatical subject does not establish that a simple ego independently causes thought.

How does successful willing generate a misleading feeling?

The commanding aspect claims the success of the cooperating processes and experiences the outcome as its own sufficient achievement.

Which freedom does §21 reject?

Ultimate self-causation. It also questions the opposing metaphysical picture of an unfree will.

Has §36 established that all reality is will to power?

No. It proposes that conclusion conditionally and leaves its major explanatory assumptions to be demonstrated.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 250–350 words evaluating the claim: ‘Because I experience myself choosing, I know that my will is the sole cause of my action.’

  1. Use one ordinary action to identify the intention, bodily capacities, habits, and possible resistance involved. Label this as your example.
  2. Explain how §§16–19 would question the claim without erasing the difference between a deliberate action and an accident.
  3. Use §21 to separate effective agency from being ultimately self-created. End with one question Nietzsche’s alternative still needs to answer.

Check that your objection targets a particular inference. ‘The self is an illusion’ is too broad unless you specify which conception of self you have challenged.
