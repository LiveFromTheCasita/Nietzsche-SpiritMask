The 1886 Preface: A Later Author Reopens the Book

Optional full-text route · Station 1 of 19 (printed order) · 18th stop in the chronological alternate

Read: Attempt at a Self-Criticism §§1–7, the preface Nietzsche added to the book in 1886. Stop before the dedication to Richard Wagner.
Allow: About 55–85 minutes, including the preface, this guide, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Haussmann): §1; §2; §3; §4; §5; §6; §7.

The self-criticism has its own section numbers, separate from §§1–25 of the book. Kaufmann heads it “Attempt at a Self-Criticism”; Haussmann heads it “An Attempt at Self-Criticism” and prints its first section as I. Quotations on this page follow Haussmann’s free translation; your edition’s wording will differ.

Before you read

Where you are in the route.

  • If you are following the printed order, you are starting where the 1886 edition starts: with the author looking back at a book you have not yet read. Editions differ in how they arrange this front matter. Take what he says as claims to test, not as instructions for reading.
  • If you are following the chronological alternate, you have just finished §25. You can now test each claim against the text.

Imagine being handed a novel with a preface in which the author says what the book was "really" about. You would learn something about the book. You would also learn something about the author years later, and about what he now wants the book to have meant. Both are worth knowing, and they are not the same thing.

Nietzsche published The Birth of Tragedy in 1872. For the new edition of 1886 he added a new subtitle, Hellenism and Pessimism, and this preface. In it he calls the book "a total stranger" to him "after sixteen years." Publication in 1872 and the new edition in 1886 are fourteen years apart. One possibility is that Nietzsche counts from the book's composition during the war he describes in §1. He defends the book's questions, attacks its style, and renames its purpose. By the time he wrote the preface, he had also published books the 1872 reader could not have known, and §5 names one of them in passing.

The main question: What does the author of 1886 claim his first book was really about, and which of those claims could the 1872 text itself confirm or resist?

Read all seven sections once, then make a two-column ledger:

  • in the first column, what Nietzsche says the book was about;
  • in the second, what he says was wrong with it.

This page gives close reading to §§2–4, which the eight-session introduction does not assign. For §§1 and 5–7 you will build ledger entries here; Reading 8 gives those sections their fuller close reading at the end of the route.

What to notice

  1. The circumstances. §1 begins with a war, a battle, the walls of Metz, and an illness brought home from the field. Why does Nietzsche want you to know when and in spite of what the book was written?
  2. The problem he now names. §2 says what the young author "laid hands on." Is this a description of the book's own task, or a new name for it?
  3. The question marks. Count the questions in §4 against its assertions. What kind of preface mostly asks?

Close reading

A question asked under fire

§1 dates the book's first thoughts to "the Franco-German war of 1870-71." Nietzsche was writing on the Greeks while the war went on; he was later under the walls of Metz, and then recovering from an illness brought home from the field. From these circumstances he draws a claim about the book: whatever lies at its bottom must be a deeply personal question of the first rank. Self-criticism §1, the circumstances

The question he then restates is whether pessimism must be a sign of decline, or whether there is "a pessimism of strength": an attraction to what is terrible that comes from health and abundance. Near the end of the section he turns the suspicion around. Might the Socratic confidence of the theoretical person, and even science itself, be a sign of weariness, a way of evading pessimism?

Ledger entries: the book was about the value of existence; the Greeks' need for tragedy may express strength; Socratism may express decline. These are questions in §1, and the book will have to show whether it asked them in this form.

The problem of science

In §2 Nietzsche names what the young author took hold of: "the problem of science itself—science conceived for the first time as problematic, as questionable." He adds that this problem cannot be seen from within science. The book had to stand elsewhere, on the ground of art, and so became "a book perhaps for artists," with "an artists' metaphysics in the background." Self-criticism §2, the problem of science

Science here translates a German word covering organized knowledge in general, not only the laboratory; Reading 6 discusses this range. The claim is ambitious. It says the book's deepest subject was not Greek tragedy but the value of knowledge, and it presents the Greek material as the vantage point from which that value first became questionable.

The section ends with a double formula for the task the book first attempted: "to view science through the optics of the artist, and art moreover through the optics of life." Notice the two steps. The first, looking at knowledge from the standpoint of art, describes something the 1872 book plainly does in its account of Socrates. The second, looking at art from the standpoint of life, asks for a standard above art. You will have to decide whether the young book supplies that second step, or whether 1886 adds it. Self-criticism §2, the closing formula

Ledger entry: the book's real subject was the problem of science. Where to test it: §§13–15 and §18.

A verdict on the writing

§3 is Nietzsche's harshest judgment. He calls the book "badly written, heavy, painful, image-angling and image-entangling." The list includes a gendered insult: the book is "sugared at times even to femininism." It also contains a charge that goes beyond style. The book is "very convinced and therefore rising above the necessity of demonstration," written as "a book for initiates," as music for those who already share the experience. Self-criticism §3, the verdict

That last charge concerns how the book persuades. It addresses readers who already share an experience of music instead of arguing for claims that others could test. If it is true, it is an objection to the argument, not just to the prose.

He then offers an alternative: "It should have sung, this 'new soul'—and not spoken!" He regrets not having written as a poet, or at least as a philologist. And he closes with a problem the book raised and did not solve: as long as we cannot answer "what is Dionysian?", the Greeks remain unknown to us. Self-criticism §3, "should have sung"

Ask whether these alternatives fit together. A book that sings would persuade even less by demonstration, while a philologist's book would demonstrate more. Nietzsche wants both at once, which tells you how unsettled the verdict is.

Ledger entries:

  • The book was about the question "what is Dionysian?"
  • It was written in the wrong voice.
  • It trusted conviction over proof.

A bundle of questions

§4 opens by admitting that the book answered "what is Dionysian?" in the voice of a disciple, and that Nietzsche would now speak more guardedly. What follows is almost entirely questions. Self-criticism §4, the opening

  • Did the Greeks' relation to pain stay constant?
  • Did their longing for beauty grow out of privation?
  • If so, where did the older longing for the terrible, and for tragic myth, come from? Perhaps from strength and "over-fullness."
  • Could the Dionysian madness out of which tragedy and comedy grew be, not a sign of degeneration, but one of the "neuroses of health"?
  • What does the satyr, half god and half goat, say about the experience behind the chorus?

Then the direction of the questions reverses. Perhaps the Greeks became more optimistic, more logical, more "cheerful" and "scientific" as they weakened. And perhaps modern optimism, practical and theoretical utilitarianism, and democracy, which he calls its contemporary, are symptoms of "physiological weariness" rather than of health. The section ends by adding the "weightiest question": "Viewed through the optics of life, what is the meaning of—morality?" Self-criticism §4, optimism, democracy, and morality

Two things deserve your attention. First, the vocabulary: symptoms, health, weariness, the physiological. This is the language of the author of 1886, and it recasts the book's questions as diagnoses. Second, the political claim. Nietzsche presents democracy as a possible symptom of decline. That is his 1886 claim, stated here as a question but plainly pointed. The 1872 book makes political claims of its own, especially in §18 and §23, and they are not the same claims. Do not let one stand in for the other.

Ledger entries:

  • Tragedy may have grown from strength.
  • Optimism, logic, and democracy may be symptoms of decline.
  • The book's deepest question was morality.

What the later sections claim

Read §§5–7 now for their claims; Reading 8 gives them their fuller close reading. In your ledger:

  • §5 says the dedication already set art, not morality, as the properly metaphysical activity of man. It recalls that the book repeatedly makes the existence of the world justified only as an aesthetic phenomenon. It reads the book's silence about Christianity as a sign of hostility, and it names the book's counter-valuation "Dionysian." Its phrase for the book's pessimism, "Beyond Good and Evil," is also the title Nietzsche gave a book published that same year. Self-criticism §5
  • §6 regrets expressing new valuations in Kantian and Schopenhauerian formulas. It says his greater regret is having mixed the Greek problem with hopes for modern German music and the "spirit of Teutonism." Self-criticism §6
  • §7 asks whether the book is itself Romantic. It quotes a passage from the book about a new generation that would desire "metaphysical comfort," and answers that one should first learn "the art of earthly comfort" and "learn to laugh." The section ends with a quotation from Zarathustra. Self-criticism §7

Ledger entries:

  • The book was anti-moral and anti-Christian from the start.
  • It borrowed the wrong philosophical language.
  • It attached its insight to false hopes.
  • It needed a comfort it should have refused.

The passage §7 quotes comes from §18 of the book. On this route you meet it in its original setting at station 14.

A difficulty

How much should a preface be allowed to decide about the book it introduces?

Nietzsche knows the book better than anyone. He can tell you what he intended and how his thinking changed. He is also an interested witness. By 1886 he wants the first book to appear as the beginning of the thinker he has become. His account is evidence, and it is also an interpretation made for a purpose.

The risk depends on where you stand in the route:

  • Printed order: you will be tempted to read the 1872 book through 1886 vocabulary, such as strength, decline, health, morality, and Christianity. Here is a simple rule. When you meet a passage the ledger mentions, first write down what the 1872 text says in its own terms. Consult the ledger only afterwards.
  • Chronological alternate: the opposite risk. You have your own reading of the book, and you may dismiss every later claim that disagrees with it. Ask instead which of Nietzsche's claims the text actually supports, and where it resists them.

Choose one ledger entry that you expect to be partly right. Say which part you expect the book to confirm, which part you expect it to resist, and why.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does Nietzsche say the problem of science could not be seen "on the groundwork of science"?

To question the value of knowledge, you need a standpoint outside the pursuit of knowledge. §2 says the book found it in art. The closing formula then adds a second standard, life, by which art itself is to be judged. Whether the 1872 book already has that second standard is a question to test, not something the preface settles.

Is §3 only a complaint about style?

No. Some of its charges concern the prose. Others concern the book's way of persuading: its conviction without demonstration, its address to initiates. That is an objection to how the argument works. Nietzsche's alternatives, singing or philology, pull in different directions.

Does §4 claim that Greek tragedy grew out of strength?

It asks whether it did, in a long sequence of questions ("perhaps"), using the 1886 language of health, symptoms, and physiology. The section proposes hypotheses. It also makes a pointed suggestion about democracy that should be recognized as a later political judgment, not read back into the 1872 book.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words.

  • Printed order: Which claim in your ledger do you most expect the 1872 book to confirm, and which do you most expect it to resist? Name the section of the book where you will test each (use the station list), and state what you would need to find there.
  • Chronological alternate: Choose one claim from your ledger and judge it against one passage of the 1872 book. Quote or paraphrase the passage precisely. Then decide whether the later claim describes it, extends it, or rewrites it.

A successful response separates what the preface says from what it proves, names a specific place in the book, and keeps the verdict open to evidence. Keep the ledger; later lessons refer back to it.
