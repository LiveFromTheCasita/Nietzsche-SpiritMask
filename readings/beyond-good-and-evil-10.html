What Is Noble, and Who Pays for It?

Beyond Good and Evil — Introductory Reading Path · Reading 10 of 10

Read: Part IX, §§257–260, 265, 287, 289, 295–296; the closing poem in full. Read every listed section in full; unlisted sections are outside this lesson’s assignment.
Allow: About 110–145 minutes for the assigned text, commentary, rereading, questions, and writing. You can divide this lesson across two sittings.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative: Part IX · Closing poem (prose translated by Helen Zimmern; closing poem by L. A. Magnus).

Before you read

You can divide the session after §265. Keep the poem for the end: its difficult encounter between old friends belongs to the book’s conclusion.

Bring your first-lesson notes and later revisions. Mark one claim you understand better, one you accept with qualifications, and one you find hardest to defend.

Distinguish historical descriptions, claims about life, and recommendations for conduct. Nietzsche moves between them.

The main question: Can Nietzsche's ideal of a self-revering creator be defended without accepting his account of the people and social relations that must sustain it?

Three things to notice

  1. Outer rank and inner distance Section 257 connects class hierarchy to the enlargement of distance within a soul. Identify the claimed connection before deciding whether it is convincing.
  2. Equality has a boundary Sections 259–260 and 265 allow restraint, duties, and reciprocal respect among equals while treating those outside the circle differently. Ask who draws that boundary.
  3. The conclusion changes its form The final pages move from self-reverence to masks, a dialogue with Dionysus, the limits of written thought, and a poem about lost and renewed friendship. Their changes of voice matter.

A way to read the passages

The cost enters the foreground

Section 257 gives social hierarchy a foundational role in human elevation. Nietzsche connects an aristocratic caste’s experience of commanding subordinates with the capacity for demanding distinctions within a soul. The inward development matters, but it grows from conquest, domination, and slavery. Reading the section solely as advice to master oneself removes its asserted social condition.

Section 258 makes the cost explicit. A healthy aristocracy regards the sacrifice and reduction of many lives as justified by the higher existence it enables. Society supports a select group. Nietzsche describes accepting this dependence as part of aristocratic health. Ask what excellence means here and why achieving it should authorize these sacrifices.

A claim about life does not finish the argument

Section 259 identifies life with appropriation, growth, imposition of form, and exploitation. Reciprocal restraint can operate among comparable members of one body, but Nietzsche denies that freedom from exploitation can govern living relations altogether. The earlier language of will to power now carries an overt social burden.

Distinguish the claim that living processes involve taking and transforming from the conclusion that particular arrangements of human domination are necessary or justified. Granting the first does not determine how resources or opportunities should be distributed. Nietzsche can reply that opposition to exploitation also expresses a valuation. That exposes the disagreement; it does not demonstrate why his hierarchy deserves acceptance.

Two ways of making moral distinctions

Section 260 presents master and slave moralities as recurring types of valuation. The noble type begins by affirming its own qualities and distinguishes what it admires from what it regards as low or contemptible. The dependent type evaluates from conditions of vulnerability and suffering: kindness and patience become valuable, while threatening power becomes evil. The distinction concerns the formation and function of judgments, not simply two lists of actions.

Nietzsche immediately allows combinations and conflict between the types, even within one person. This matters when the terms tempt us to classify everyone neatly. Generosity can arise from abundance; truthfulness can express pride; care can respond intelligently to vulnerability. Identifying a motive does not complete an evaluation.

Nor should the noble type be modernized into an uncomplicated rebel. Section 260 gives it reverence for ancestors, tradition, and established rank, as well as self-command and value creation. Its duties are principally to equals. The account is richer, and harder to endorse, than a contrast between independent people and conformists.

Respect among peers, reverence for oneself

In §265 noble egoism includes careful reciprocity with those recognized as equally privileged. The noble person honors himself in his peers, limits himself in their company, and expects an exchange of recognition. Egoism here does not mean constant discourtesy or isolated appetite. Yet the opening assumption remains that other people may properly be subordinate and sacrificed. Internal fairness coexists with external domination.

Section 287 places nobility in fundamental self-reverence rather than celebrated deeds or works. The noble soul does not wait for applause to authorize its worth. This helps explain the ideal’s attraction: creation requires independence from prevailing approval. It also creates a problem. How can a reader distinguish grounded self-reverence from inflated entitlement when visible achievement does not settle the question?

A mask does not make criticism impossible

Section 289 describes a recluse's suspicion that books conceal their authors and that every apparent foundation might have another depth beneath it. The point is stronger than saying that a philosopher keeps a final doctrine secret. The passage also doubts whether there are final opinions waiting intact behind the published ones. Philosophical expression can be provisional, selective, and shaped by the distance between writer and reader.

This complicates a tidy summary without erasing uncomfortable assertions. If every objection could be answered by imagining a hidden, more acceptable meaning, the text would become immune to judgment. Interpretations must attend to voice and concealment while asking what the actual sentences assert. Masks invite another reading; they do not make all readings equally persuasive.

The philosopher becomes a disciple

In §295 the narrator becomes a disciple of Dionysus. The god tempts people beyond their settled condition, making them more receptive and uncertain, awakening possibilities they cannot yet name. Nietzsche’s speaker is therefore not the sovereign master of a completed teaching. He too has encountered an influence that exceeds him.

The dialogue remains disturbing. Dionysus imagines making humanity stronger, more profound, more beautiful, and more evil. The narrator reacts with alarm and retains a human distance from the god. That distance prevents a smooth conversion of the scene into a practical moral command. It also leaves the course's problem intact: a transformation may enlarge capacities while challenging, or damaging, what we already value.

Section 296 turns toward the book itself. Once fixed in words, living thoughts can lose their movement and look like permanent truths. The worry is not that nothing can be understood, but that preserved formulations may replace the thinking that produced them. Returning critically to an argument can keep its questions active.

Why the poem belongs to the course

The closing poem stages a meeting that fails. Old friends arrive, but the speaker's transformation has made recognition difficult. Self-overcoming appears through estrangement, vulnerability, and the limits of a shared past. The mountain height brings an expanded prospect and a painful distance from others. Read the departure without reducing the old friends to inferior people who simply deserved to be left behind.

The speaker then turns toward new friendship, and Zarathustra arrives in the final movement. The book ends with renewed company and a feast, not with permanent isolation. This does not resolve the social objections raised by §§257–259. It changes the register in which the reader encounters the wish for a different future: argument gives way to an invitation whose costs and possibilities have become more visible.

A difficulty to keep in view

Must we accept the whole aristocratic project to learn from Nietzsche's account of self-creation?

No, but separating elements requires an argument. You may defend demanding practices, resistance to conformity, or independence from applause while rejecting imposed subordination. Explain why those capacities can develop through relations different from the ones Nietzsche presents as necessary. Otherwise the separation risks making his position more comfortable without examining it.

The strongest objection also needs the strongest response. Nietzsche can ask whether your alternative supports exceptional development or quietly makes general comfort its highest standard. You can answer by distinguishing equal human standing from identical achievements, and protection from domination from exemption from difficulty. Then assess whether that answer addresses his concern rather than merely restating your preference.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why cannot §§257–259 be read only as an account of inner self-mastery?

They explicitly discuss ruling castes, slavery, conquest, and the sacrifice of many people. Inner development is connected to these social arrangements rather than substituted for them.

Does §260 divide every person into one of two pure categories?

No. Nietzsche explicitly allows mixtures and conflicts between master and slave valuations, including within one soul. The types help analyze the sources of judgments.

How can noble egoism include equality in §265?

It recognizes reciprocal rights and respect among those accepted as peers. That bounded equality coexists with the expectation that others will remain subordinate.

What tension connects §§287 and 289?

Section 287 locates nobility in fundamental self-reverence; §289 complicates access to a philosopher's settled identity or final views. Confidence and interpretive uncertainty coexist, leaving judgment demanding.

What do §296 and the poem add to the ending?

The final prose section questions what fixed words preserve of living thought. The poem gives transformation a human scene of failed recognition, lost companionship, and the arrival of new friendship.

What should I write in my notes?

Write a final essay of 600–800 words: What would it mean to affirm honesty after reading Beyond Good and Evil? Return to the account of honesty you wrote in the first lesson.

  1. State your original account of the value and give your present thesis. Say what has changed and what you still defend.
  2. Explain its relation to truth: why desire this value, and what follows from discovering mixed or interested motives behind it? Use at least one assigned section from Part I.
  3. Explain its relation to discipline: what habits, constraints, or difficult experiences could cultivate it? Use an earlier lesson and test the account against §§227 or 229–230.
  4. Explain its relation to rank: who gets to define its best expression, whose capacities it serves, and who may bear its costs. Engage §§257–260 or 265 directly.
  5. Develop the strongest objection to your thesis, drawing on one concrete problem from the course: misogyny, national or racial stereotyping, exploitation, or self-certifying authority. Give a response that concedes any point you cannot answer.
  6. End with a revised commitment and one unresolved question. Explain how you can hold that commitment without treating your formulation as the last word, using §296 or the closing poem.

Use section numbers for at least five references across four parts of the book. Your conclusion may defend, revise, or reject Nietzsche's proposal; it should show what your judgment now has to answer for.

Continue the reading

The introductory course ends by returning you to judgment: what can you now affirm, what needs revision, and what remains unresolved? Keep §260 beside the opening essay of On the Genealogy of Morals: ask how its contrast of valuations becomes a more sustained inquiry into origins, conflict, and morality’s value.
