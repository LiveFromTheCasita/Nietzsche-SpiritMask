Can Europe Outgrow Nationalism?

Before you read

Read §256 in full, including its concluding verse about Wagner. If you divide the session, pause after §242 and resume with §§250–251.

No musical listening or outside historical reading is required. Section 240 discusses Wagner's opera Die Meistersinger; §256 ends by attacking the Christian direction Nietzsche hears in Wagner's Parsifal.

Keep three separate headings: criticisms of nationalism, claims about peoples, and hopes for future rulers. One section may belong under all three.

The main question: Does Nietzsche's opposition to nationalism offer an escape from inherited group prejudice, or does it reorganize that prejudice around a European ruling elite?

Three things to notice

  1. The patriotic feeling belongs to the narrator too Section 241 looks back at §240 as an instance of the patriotic attachment a good European can still experience. The critic is implicated in the feeling he diagnoses.
  2. One process, divergent outcomes In §242 the mixing and adaptation that produce leveling also prepare unusually capable commanders. Do not read the passage as a straightforward celebration of democratic progress.
  3. Read the whole of §251 Its opposition to antisemitic agitation, racial stereotypes, assimilation proposals, and concluding ambition for a ruling caste belong to the same passage. Keep them in view together.

A way to read the passages

Why a book about values turns to nations

Part VIII can look like a digression into national preferences. Its deeper connection is the formation of taste. Earlier parts asked how drives and inherited disciplines speak through an individual's judgments. Here the question expands: how do a people's habits shape what its members call depth, greatness, progress, or culture? National pride can make a local preference appear to be an authoritative measure.

Nietzsche writes from a European horizon and ranks cultures freely within it. His opposition to narrow patriotism should not be confused with a commitment to equal standing for every person or culture. The task is to discover what he thinks might become possible beyond national attachment, and who is included in that future. That requires reading the attractive proposal and its exclusions together.

Admiration without innocence

Section 240 hears German qualities in Wagner's music: richness, accumulated history, force, awkwardness, and an unresolved relation to the present. The description mixes admiration with criticism. Nietzsche does not simply place himself above the pleasure of belonging. In §241 he identifies the preceding response as one of the patriotic episodes to which even good Europeans remain susceptible.

That admission recalls the previous lesson's concern with self-scrutiny. Overcoming an allegiance need not mean never feeling its attraction. It may mean becoming able to interrupt the interpretation it encourages. The dialogue between old patriots in §241 then distinguishes the capacity to build a powerful state from genuine greatness. Political success cannot by itself answer whether a nation has deepened its culture or sacrificed it to collective ambition.

Europe's new flexibility has two faces

In §242 Nietzsche interprets Europe's changing conditions as loosening the link between people and an inherited environment. Increasing adaptability can make individuals less tied to one locality or established way of life. He describes this as a physiological process as well as a cultural one. Treat that language as part of his proposed explanation, not as a scientific result the course asks you to accept.

The same process has opposed possibilities. Nietzsche expects a broad leveling toward useful, adaptable workers, but also exceptional individuals whose wider training increases their power. Democracy may therefore prepare commanders whom its admirers did not intend to produce. At the end, the language of tyranny includes a spiritual sense, but it is not restricted to it. The passage cannot be reduced to advice about flexible thinking or personal leadership.

Here the earlier discussion of philosophers as legislators meets a social prediction. What institutions, habits, and human material might produce future command? Nietzsche's interest in that question explains why European integration appeals to him without making him a democratic egalitarian. A shared political or cultural space can still be imagined as sharply hierarchical.

Gratitude and stereotype occupy the same pages

Section 250 credits the Jews with a demanding moral inheritance that has given European culture grandeur, tension, and difficult questions. Nietzsche regards that inheritance as both valuable and dangerous. This combination matters: criticism of the origins or consequences of a morality need not erase indebtedness to it. His brief expression of gratitude interrupts a simple story in which European culture is self-generated.

Section 251 opens by treating antisemitic agitation as one symptom of German nationalist folly. Later it rejects the claim that Jews are plotting European domination and suggests removing antisemitic agitators rather than persecuting Jews. These statements are real features of the section. An account that describes the passage as straightforward endorsement of the antisemitic movement would misrepresent it.

The opposition does not remove the passage's prejudices. Nietzsche generalizes about Jews as a homogeneous hereditary group, repeats stereotypes about money and intellect, and imagines their contribution through selective incorporation into a future ruling order. He also speaks in the language of racial strength and purity. Favorable stereotypes remain stereotypes: praise of a group's supposed collective nature can still deny the variety and autonomy of its members.

Follow the argument past its most quotable line

The difficult movement of §251 includes a presentation of German fears about Jewish immigration and the imagery of a weak national body struggling to digest a stronger group. Nietzsche diagnoses nationalist insecurity while also giving some of its exclusionary language a hearing. Read how the speaker shifts, qualifies, and implicates himself. A single sentence of praise or condemnation cannot safely stand for the entire argument.

Its endpoint clarifies the stakes: the formation of a new European ruling caste. The projected encounter between Jewish capacities and Prussian habits of command is evaluated for what it might produce at the top. This is not a proposal grounded in everyone's equal rights. It is an aristocratic project that can oppose national hatred while retaining selective admission, inherited group assumptions, and inequalities of power.

The distinction gives us a precise critical question. Does criticizing hostility to an outsider necessarily change the habit of treating people as material for someone else's future? Nietzsche makes the first criticism, but the final ambition of §251 keeps the second problem alive.

Culture already crosses the borders

Section 256 argues that major nineteenth-century figures anticipated a European synthesis through their work. Wagner is a particularly useful test because nationalist admirers claim him so readily. Nietzsche emphasizes the French connections and cross-cultural impulses in his art, then refuses to treat the artist's own account of himself as decisive. Cultural achievement may exceed the national story subsequently told about it.

This is not uncomplicated praise. The same artists are described as excessive, theatrical, driven, and vulnerable to Christian retreat. Wagner's late religious direction draws the final attack. The achievement can point beyond a national culture without completing Nietzsche's desired transformation of values.

Notice also that §256 continues to make broad comparisons between German and French or Latin dispositions. Its effort to unsettle national ownership does not consistently escape national typology. The productive insight is that cultural creation depends on crossings its public mythology can conceal. The unresolved danger is that the interpreter continues to rank whole peoples through the very generalizations that made the mythology persuasive.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can we recognize Nietzsche's opposition to antisemitism without presenting him as free of anti-Jewish prejudice?

Yes, by separating distinct claims and then showing how they coexist. Opposition to organized antisemitic agitation, rejection of a conspiracy claim, and support for Jewish participation in Europe are not identical to rejection of racial stereotypes or support for universal equality. Section 251 combines the former positions with assumptions that obstruct the latter.

This distinction is not a compromise between two labels. It is a more exact account of the text. Explain what Nietzsche opposes, what he repeats, and what he wants to build. The same discipline applies to his opposition to nationalism: crossing national boundaries does not by itself settle who should rule or whose interests should count.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why should §§240 and 241 be read together?

Section 241 identifies the preceding musical response as an example of patriotic feeling that can still attract a good European. It places the narrator within the phenomenon he criticizes.

What two outcomes does §242 associate with increasing adaptability?

A broad leveling toward flexible, useful workers and exceptional concentrations of capacity that may produce powerful commanders. Nietzsche presents the second as an unintended possibility within democratization.

What does §250 credit to the Jews?

A moral inheritance of exceptional scope, demandingness, and grandeur that shapes European culture and its problems. Nietzsche treats its value as mixed rather than simply beneficial or harmful.

Why is §251 insufficient evidence for calling Nietzsche an egalitarian?

Although it opposes antisemitic agitation, it retains racial and cultural stereotypes and ends with the selective formation of a European ruling caste. Its proposed future remains hierarchical.

What does Wagner demonstrate in §256?

An artist claimed as nationally representative can depend on cross-cultural sources and express a wider European development. Nietzsche also criticizes the artist's limitations and late religious direction.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 350–500 words defending or challenging this claim: Nietzsche escapes national narrowness more successfully than he escapes collective prejudice.

  1. Give the strongest textual case for his challenge to nationalism, using §§241 or 256.
  2. Explain the social future envisaged in §242 or at the end of §251. Identify who gains freedom or power and who becomes useful to them.
  3. Use two distinct features of §251: one that opposes antisemitic agitation and one that retains a stereotype or exclusionary assumption. Explain why neither erases the other.
  4. Conclude with a reasoned judgment about whether crossing national boundaries is enough to change the way human beings are ranked.

Attribute the racial and national generalizations to Nietzsche. Your task is to evaluate their role in his argument, not to repeat them as established descriptions of peoples.
