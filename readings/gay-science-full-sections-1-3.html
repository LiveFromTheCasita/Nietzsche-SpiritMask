The Comedy of Existence and Its Serious Teachers

Optional full-text route · Station 2 of 35 (proposed)

Review draft. This lesson belongs to a proposed full-text route that has not been published. The stations before and after it are not yet written. For the complete published introduction, use the ten-session guided reading path.

Read: Book I, §§1–3, in full, from the original 1882 edition. Begin at §1 and stop before §4.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. This is an estimate, not a measured time. §1 is the longest aphorism in Book I; if you need two sittings, stop after §1.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): §1 · §2 · §3.

Quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation, including his section titles; Kaufmann’s wording and titles differ, and none of his wording is quoted here. The prose sections are numbered 1–383 across the five books. The verse prelude (poems 1–63) and the later preface (§§1–4) have their own numbering, so “§1” here always means the first prose aphorism.

Before you read

In the 1882 book, sixty-three short poems of jest and mockery come before the prose. The first prose section then takes on the most serious people in history: the founders of moralities and religions, who tell us why we exist. Nietzsche does not simply call them liars. He asks what they have done for the human species, and why they never want us to laugh.

§1 is long, and its argument turns several times. §§2–3 are shorter and personal. Each of the three ends with a judgment turned back on someone who judges: the teachers of purpose, the speaker himself, and “noble natures.” Read them as a sequence, not as three separate observations.

The main question: If every teacher of the purpose of life serves the species while believing he serves something higher, what is left of the book’s own “joyful wisdom”—is it exempt from the diagnosis, or one more case of it?

Read all three sections once before continuing.

A habit for the whole route

The book changes voice constantly. On this route, ask three things of each aphorism before you decide what it claims.

  1. Who speaks, and to whom? An “I,” a “we,” a “thou,” a dialogue, a scene, or an impersonal maxim.
  2. What kind of claim is it? Mark the margin:
    • P for a psychological observation you can test against conduct;
    • H for a historical or developmental claim that needs outside evidence;
    • V for a valuation or ranking;
    • ? where Nietzsche himself hedges (“perhaps”).
  3. What would test it? Name one kind of evidence or one counter-case.

A single sentence can carry more than one mark. The marks do not grade Nietzsche; they help you see which kind of support each claim would need.

What to notice

  1. The “economy” of §1. Hatred and ambition are said to preserve the species. What does Nietzsche offer as evidence, and where does he write “perhaps”?
  2. The “fors.” “Life should be loved, for …!” What fills the gap, and why does §1 leave it empty?
  3. Three endings. Compare the last sentences of §§1, 2, and 3. Who is being judged in each?

Close reading

One problem for everyone

Common titles §1 “The Teachers of the Object of Existence”; this guide calls them the teachers of purpose. The section opens with a claim about all human beings: whatever else they do, they serve “the conservation of the human species.” They do this not out of love for the species, but because nothing in them is older or stronger than that instinct; it is “the essence of our race and herd.” In Common’s English, “race” here means the human race as a whole. The section is about the species, not about peoples. §1, the opening claim

The next step unsettles ordinary moral sorting. On a “general calculation,” we lose confidence in dividing people into useful and harmful, good and evil. “Even the most hurtful man is still perhaps … the most useful of all,” because he preserves impulses without which humanity might have decayed. Hatred, delight in mischief, rapacity, and ambition belong to “the marvellous economy of the conservation of the race”—which Nietzsche at once calls “a costly, lavish, and on the whole very foolish economy.” §1, the foolish economy

Use the marks. The claim that such impulses have helped preserve the species is an H, and it carries a ?: Nietzsche writes “perhaps” twice in this stretch. His evidence is only that the species has in fact survived, “as is demonstrated to us.” Survival shows that these impulses did not destroy the species. It does not show that each of them helped preserve it. Keep that gap in view; the whole section leans on it.

A future for laughter

The address then turns to “thou,” the individual reader. Whatever you do, you probably serve the species somehow, so you will find both admirers and mockers. But no one yet has been able to mock you as truth would require: to laugh at oneself “out of the veriest truth” needs more sense of truth, and more genius, than even the best have had. “There is perhaps still a future even for laughter!” §1, the future of laughter

The condition he names is startling. When the maxim “The race is all, the individual is nothing” has been incorporated into humanity, laughter may unite with wisdom, and “perhaps then there will be only ‘joyful wisdom.’” This is the first appearance of the book’s title phrase in the prose. It arrives as a conditional and a “perhaps.” It is tied to a standpoint from which the individual counts for nothing, and which Nietzsche calls “ultimate emancipation and irresponsibility.” §1, the maxim and “joyful wisdom”

Do not assume that Nietzsche simply endorses the maxim. It is in quotation marks, and it describes the view from which the individual’s seriousness becomes laughable. Put a V and a ? beside it, and carry the question forward: §3 values exceptional individuals, and much of this book will ask how a single life can be given shape.

The period of tragedy

“Meanwhile,” the comedy of existence has not yet become conscious of itself. It is still “the period of tragedy, the period of morals and religions.” Nietzsche stages history as a theatre. The founders of moralities and religions, and the instigators of moral struggle and religious war, have been its heroes. Everything else has served as scenery, confidants, or valets. The parenthesis is pointed: “The poets, for example, have always been the valets of some morality or other.” §1, the stage of moral heroes

These tragedians also work for the species, although they believe they work for God. They further life by furthering “the belief in life”: each cries out, “It is worth while to live.” The same species-preserving impulse appears in them as “reason and passion of spirit,” surrounded by “a brilliant train of motives.” Those motives try to make us forget that it is “just impulse, instinct, folly and baselessness.” §1, impulse in the costume of reason

Now look at the “fors”: “Life should be loved, for …! Man should benefit himself and his neighbour, for …!” Nietzsche leaves the reasons blank. The moral teacher supplies them, and so makes what happens “of itself and without design” appear to happen by design. To do this, he “devises a second and different existence” and lifts ordinary existence off its hinges. He wants no one to laugh at existence, at ourselves, or at him. For him, the individual is “something first and last and immense.” §1, the second existence

Two marks are needed here. That moral teachings serve the species while claiming a higher purpose is an H of great scope, and the section offers no historical cases. That their reasons are “folly and baselessness” is a V. Nietzsche also adds a harsh generalization: every system of ethics so far would have ruined humanity had it prevailed. Ask what evidence could support a claim that large.

The visionary animal

Yet the teachers achieved something real. Each time “the hero” appeared, people were shaken by the thought “Yes, it is worth while to live! yes, I am worthy to live!” Life became interesting to itself again. In the long run, laughter has won: “the short tragedy always passed over once more into the eternal comedy of existence,” and the “waves of innumerable laughters”—Nietzsche credits the phrase to Aeschylus—beat over even the greatest tragedies. §1, tragedy and comedy

Then comes the section’s most important turn. The teachers have changed human nature. Humanity now needs them to keep appearing: “Man has gradually become a visionary animal,” and must “from time to time believe that he knows why he exists.” The species cannot flourish “without periodically confiding in life!” Even the most clear-sighted friend of humanity will add that the tragic, not only laughter, belongs among the conditions of preservation. §1, the visionary animal

This complicates the opening. Laughter does not simply defeat the teachers of purpose; it alternates with them, in what Nietzsche calls “this new law of ebb and flow.” The section ends in a new voice: “Consequently! Consequently! Consequently! Do you understand me, oh my brothers? … We also shall have our time!” §1, the closing address

Who are “we”? At least three readings are possible:

  • the laughers, whose time of joyful wisdom will come;
  • new teachers of purpose, whose tragedy will come;
  • both, one after the other, under the same law.

The address to “my brothers” sounds like a preacher’s. Keep all three readings open. The book will not settle them quickly.

The weights behind the scales

§2 was the first assignment of Reading 1, which gives it a full close reading. On this route, read it as the next step after §1. The teachers of purpose supplied reasons, their “fors.” The speaker of §2 finds that most people have never asked for reasons at all. They keep using their scales, “calling this good and that bad,” and no one blushes “when you remark that these weights are not the full amount.” §2, the scales

Put §2 beside §1 and a question appears that Reading 1 does not ask. Is the speaker’s “longing for certainty,” his “innermost desire and profoundest need,” exempt from §1’s diagnosis? Or is it one more impulse that has gathered “a brilliant train of motives” around itself? The section closes by conceding a personal failing: “This is my special kind of unrighteousness.” The speaker expects everyone to share his passion simply because they are human. §2, the closing admission

Keep the image of the weights. Book III ends with a catechism in which one answer is that “the weights of all things must be determined anew” (§269, at station 19).

Noble unreason

§3 turns the same pattern into a typology. “Ignoble natures” cannot credit a magnanimous act without suspecting a hidden advantage. When they are convinced there is none, they treat the noble person as a fool. The ignoble nature “keeps its advantage steadily in view”; that is its wisdom. Compared with it, “the higher nature is more irrational”: it yields to its impulses, and in its best moments its reason lapses. §3, advantage and unreason

Notice what is reversed. Ordinary praise calls noble conduct rational and selfish conduct base. §3 calls the noble conduct less rational: the parent animal that protects its young at the risk of death “becomes stupider than at other times.” Among the passions the ignoble cannot understand, Nietzsche names one directly: how a person “out of love of knowledge can stake his health and honour on the game.” The passion of §2 is now classed as a noble unreason. §3, the passion for knowledge

The section does not stop at praise. The higher nature has “a singular standard of value,” yet it mostly believes that its tastes are “the generally valid values and non-values.” It takes its own passion for “the concealed passion of every one,” so it cannot judge average people fairly. It marvels at the world’s madness, and the world’s failure to recognize “the one thing needful for it.” The last sentence names the fault: “This is the eternal unrighteousness of noble natures.” §3, the closing judgment

State both sides plainly. §3 keeps its ranking. It speaks of “higher” and “ignoble” natures and of “average men,” and it describes the ignoble as governed by calculation. It does not withdraw the hierarchy. What it adds is a charge against the higher: they mistake their own standard for a universal one. Compare the endings of §§2 and 3. “My special kind of unrighteousness” becomes the “eternal unrighteousness” of a type. The phrase “one thing needful” will return as the title of §290 in Book IV.

A difficulty

Can the diagnosis include its diagnostician?

§1 claims that every teacher of purpose serves the species while believing otherwise, and that his reasons are impulse in costume. §§2–3 then describe the speaker’s own passion for questioning, and the noble passion for knowledge, as ruling impulses that mistake themselves for universal standards. If the diagnosis applies to them, it seems to apply to the book. Is the demand for intellectual conscience a truth about how belief should work, or one more species-serving passion that must periodically believe it “knows why” it exists?

There are several ways to respond. Nietzsche might accept the consequence and treat his book as another wave in the ebb and flow. He might hold that an impulse’s origin does not settle whether its demands are right. Or the self-accusations that end §§2 and 3 might themselves be the book’s answer: a thinker who laughs at his own passion without abandoning it. Book V will state the second option explicitly (§345, station 26). For now, decide which reading the text of §§1–3 best supports, and name the sentence that supports it.

A second difficulty concerns evidence. §1’s central claim is functional: impulses exist because they have preserved the species. That kind of explanation needs more than the fact of survival. Before accepting it, ask what would count against it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What evidence does §1 give that “evil” impulses belong to the economy of preserving the species?

Very little beyond the fact that the species has survived (“as is demonstrated to us”), together with Nietzsche’s own “perhaps.” Survival shows that these impulses did not destroy the species; it does not show that each contributed to preserving it. The claim is a large developmental hypothesis, and it would need further evidence.

Why do the teachers of purpose not want us to laugh?

Their work depends on the belief that “there is something which really may not be laughed at”: a purpose behind life and an individual who is “first and last and immense.” Laughter at existence, or at oneself, would expose that purpose as the species-impulse in the costume of reason.

Does §1 say that the teachers of purpose were simply harmful?

No. They made life “interesting to ourselves once more,” and they changed human nature. Humanity now needs periodic belief that it knows why it exists. The section ends with alternation—tragedy and laughter under a “law of ebb and flow”—not with a victory of laughter.

What do the last sentences of §2 and §3 have in common?

Both charge a passionate judge with injustice. §2’s speaker admits his “special kind of unrighteousness”: he expects everyone to share his passion for questioning. §3 names the “eternal unrighteousness of noble natures”: they take their singular standard for a universal one. In each case the judge becomes the one judged.

Does §3 withdraw its ranking of noble and ignoble natures?

No. It keeps the ranking and adds a criticism of the higher nature’s fairness. Reading it as a simple defence of ordinary people, or as uncritical praise of the noble, misses one of its two halves.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Is the speaker of §§1–3 one more of the teachers of purpose that §1 describes?

  • Begin with §1: state in your own words what the teachers of purpose do and what they serve.
  • Then use one sentence from §2 or §3 to show the speaker’s own passion or standard.
  • Decide whether §1’s diagnosis applies to it, and say what the self-accusations at the end of §2 or §3 change.

Finish with the sentence: The claim in §1 I would most want evidence for is …

A successful response separates a psychological observation from a historical claim and from a valuation. It treats “joyful wisdom” as a conditional possibility rather than a slogan. It does not decide the question by quoting a single line without its context.
