Can We Learn to Love?

The Gay Science — Essential Reading Path · Reading 6 of 10

Read: Book IV, §334, then §338, from the original 1882 edition. Read both aphorisms in full, stopping before §335 and §339 respectively.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): §334 · §338.

Before you read

Think of music you now love but once found difficult to follow. Later, you recognized a phrase, anticipated its return, and heard another part answer it. The music acquired a shape for you.

Section 334 begins with this experience and extends it to everything we learn to love, including ourselves. Section 338 asks a more uncomfortable question about our attention: can the desire to help someone leave us unable to understand their suffering?

Reading 5 examined practices through which we shape our character and our experience of ordinary things. Here we ask how a relation to something unfamiliar develops, and how concern for another person can become too quick or too general.

The main question: What kind of attention allows us to love and help without reducing another life to our first impression of it?

Three things to notice

  1. The sequence in §334. Distinguish learning to hear, tolerating strangeness, becoming accustomed, and finally desiring the thing's return.
  2. The two questions in §338. Nietzsche asks what pity does to the sufferer and what it does to the person who feels it. Follow where he changes from one question to the other.
  3. The closing account of friendship. What does Nietzsche still want to give his friends? Pay particular attention to the shift toward sharing joy.

A way to read the passages

Learning to hear is already an achievement

Section 334 begins before love. Nietzsche says we must first learn to hear a musical theme distinctly, recognizing it as something with its own shape. Sound can reach our ears without this recognition having occurred.

Imagine a melody carried quietly beneath a stronger rhythm. Once someone points it out, you can follow its changes and notice its return. Your experience has acquired a distinction that was previously unavailable. Attention has made a different encounter possible.

This first step matters because it changes the meaning of an initial dislike. Perhaps the piece has disappointed a developed judgment. Perhaps you have not yet learned to hear what it is doing. Nietzsche asks us to leave room for the second possibility.

Goodwill gives the unfamiliar time

The next stage requires effort, patience, and goodwill toward what feels strange. We remain with the thing before it has become rewarding. The openness precedes the pleasure.

Then familiarity changes the experience. We begin to expect the theme and would miss it if it disappeared. Eventually we actively desire its return. Nietzsche describes a movement from endurance through recognition into attachment.

The account gives activity and receptivity a place together. We learn distinctions and sustain attention, allowing the unfamiliar thing time to present features we did not initially recognize.

Return to the music example. Playing a recording repeatedly while attending to something else might leave the melody as unfamiliar as before. The stages Nietzsche describes involve an increasingly specific relation to what we hear. Mere exposure does not capture everything in his account.

The strong claim about everything we love

Nietzsche extends this musical account to all the things we have learned to love. He also includes self-love. We may need patience with our own unfamiliar or awkward features before they become bearable and, eventually, lovable.

This reconnects with §290's question of satisfaction with oneself. The emphasis has shifted, however: §334 follows the hospitality of attention more closely than the command of an organizing taste.

The universal claim needs scrutiny. A musical example can illuminate the development of attachment without proving that every love begins this way. Sudden attraction, longstanding affection, and love that survives disappointment might each require a more complicated account.

Nor should the passage become a demand to love whatever we encounter. Nietzsche describes the unfamiliar rewarding patience in very confident terms. We can ask whether this always happens and whether some encounters give us reasons to withdraw. Patience creates an opportunity for discovery; it does not determine in advance what we must discover.

Pause here: Describe one change in the listener between the beginning and the end of §334. Then read §338 in full, noticing its return to the first question midway through.

Pity can misread the person it wants to help

Section 338 begins with two questions: is being chiefly governed by pity good for you, and is it good for the sufferer? Nietzsche initially concentrates on the second. He argues that another person's most intimate suffering can remain difficult to understand even when we live close to them.

Here the problem with pity is a loss of particularity. The observer recognizes a sufferer, wants the suffering stopped, and may pass over what the difficulty means within that person's life. The wish to help can outrun the work of understanding.

Consider someone leaving a stable position because the work has become incompatible with a serious commitment. A friend might see only distress and respond by arranging a return to the same job. The friend could relieve an immediate difficulty while overlooking the decision that made the difficulty worth confronting.

The example illustrates Nietzsche's concern about an intervention that assumes it already knows the person's good. It does not establish that returning is wrong or that the departure must succeed. Those judgments require attention to the particular case.

The stronger argument about suffering

Nietzsche goes beyond asking helpers to listen. He describes misfortune as part of a person's development: it may alter needs, close old wounds, break with the past, or reorganize a life. He then speaks of suffering and its opposites as personally necessary.

This is a much stronger claim than saying that some difficulties can have valuable consequences. The passage connects the possibility of substantial happiness with exposure to misfortune, and challenges a way of valuing life that aims chiefly at secure comfort.

We should keep its force visible without accepting more than its argument supports. An experience can produce growth without having been necessary for it. Other suffering can destroy capacities or leave damage that no later achievement justifies. Section 338 does not supply evidence that all suffering belongs to a beneficial development.

A reader can therefore take the challenge to comfort seriously while contesting the claim of necessity. What would we lose if avoiding every difficulty became the governing aim of a life? Which difficulties serve a chosen commitment, and which simply prevent someone from living it?

The difference also matters to helping. Another person's possible growth gives us no automatic authority to decide that they should continue suffering. Nietzsche's objection to the helper who assumes control of someone's fate gives us reason to question that judgment as well.

Helping can become an escape from one's own task

Nietzsche now returns to the first question: what does pity do to the person who feels it? He describes how easily a call for assistance can draw someone away from their own path.

The argument concerns motivation as well as time. A demanding personal task may offer little recognition or gratitude. Helping someone can bring a clearer role and a publicly approved purpose. We might welcome the interruption partly because our own work has become difficult to bear.

Imagine a person who has promised to finish a piece of writing and feels exposed whenever they return to it. They volunteer to handle every minor organizational problem around them. The assistance may be useful, while its usefulness also protects them from examining why the writing is continually postponed.

Nietzsche admits how readily visible distress could draw him away. His extreme examples include a friend's demand for a shared death and the appeal of joining a struggle for freedom.

Publicly honored sacrifice might even hide a wish to abandon one's own task or life. His account raises a suspicion about motives without establishing that every sacrifice has this meaning.

The cost of the withdrawal Nietzsche proposes

His response includes living in concealment, placing distance between oneself and the urgencies of one's age, and allowing its public conflicts to become less commanding. He is proposing a substantial withdrawal of attention, not merely a more convenient schedule.

It has a purpose within the argument: protecting a difficult personal course from demands that claim unquestionable moral priority. Yet this protection has possible costs. Other people's needs can be real obligations, and public events can shape the conditions that make private work possible.

The passage leaves us with a judgment to make about Nietzsche's response. When does distance protect a valuable task? When does it become a privileged way of avoiding responsibilities? A serious reading can feel the pressure of his question without treating his chosen distance as a universal prescription.

Friendship ends in shared joy

The closing paragraphs still contain a will to help. Nietzsche turns toward friends whose distress he understands through shared sorrow and hope. He wants to increase their courage, endurance, simplicity, and joy.

This offers a different image of assistance: something that strengthens the person's capacity to go on living and acting. The ending's emphasis on shared joy also challenges a relationship organized entirely around distress. Friendship can receive another person's delight and possibility, as well as their need.

The restriction to friends is severe. Read it alongside the opening claim that even an intimate companion's deepest suffering remains hidden. How could understanding become complete enough to meet the standard Nietzsche now sets? The passage offers shared experience as a resource, but it does not resolve this tension.

Read beside §334, the ending suggests the value of learning to attend to a particular person over time. That is a connection between our two readings. It does not remove §338's stronger claims or turn it into an uncomplicated celebration of kindness.

A difficulty to keep in view

What should we do when helping requires a decision before understanding is complete?

The concern is practical. If we wait for complete knowledge of another person's suffering, we may never help. If we assume that distress tells us everything necessary, we risk imposing a response that disregards the person.

One useful distinction is between noticing an immediate need and claiming authority over an entire life. We might provide requested assistance while asking what the person hopes to preserve or change. We can treat our understanding as partial and remain willing to revise the help.

This is a way of working with the problem Nietzsche exposes; it is not the explicit rule he gives. His own closing restriction is narrower, and his valuation of suffering remains open to challenge.

Return also to the helper's motivation. Discovering that assistance brings us relief or gratitude does not prove that it is worthless. The question is what those rewards encourage us to overlook: the other person's wishes, the limits of our knowledge, or the commitments from which we are escaping.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does §334 begin with learning to distinguish a musical theme?

We may need to recognize a thing's shape before we can develop a specific relation to it. An initial lack of pleasure might reflect an undeveloped capacity to attend, although that possibility does not invalidate every first judgment.

What role does patience play before love appears?

It allows us to remain with strangeness before the encounter has become rewarding. Familiarity and expectation can then develop into attachment. Nietzsche extends this account very broadly, and that extension remains something to examine.

How might pity diminish a sufferer's individuality?

It can treat suffering as an immediately recognizable condition and overlook its place within the person's commitments and history. The helper may decide what counts as improvement before understanding what the person is trying to do.

Why does Nietzsche examine the attraction of helping?

A morally praised activity can provide an escape from difficult personal work. He includes himself among those susceptible to that attraction. The diagnosis raises a question about motives without proving that every act of assistance is evasive.

What does the ending of §338 contribute?

Nietzsche still wants to help friends become more courageous, enduring, simple, and joyful. Shared joy gives friendship a purpose beyond responding to pain. His restriction of help to friends whose distress he understands also creates a difficulty the reader should retain.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose a low-stakes example of someone facing a difficult commitment: learning a skill, changing a project, or deciding whether to persist in unfamiliar work. It may be invented. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Describe the commitment and the difficulty without assuming that you know everything the person feels.
  2. Using §334, identify something patient attention might reveal that a first impression misses.
  3. Using §338, describe one well-intentioned response that could overlook the person's purpose.
  4. Propose a form of help that takes their wishes seriously and could increase courage or joy. Explain how you would discover whether it helps.
  5. State one limit to Nietzsche's account that your example brings into view, such as incomplete understanding or a difficulty that serves no valuable purpose.

A strong response uses both aphorisms while leaving the other person's judgment a real place. It does not assume that suffering must continue for growth to occur.
