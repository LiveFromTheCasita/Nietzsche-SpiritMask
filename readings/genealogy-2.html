Who First Called Themselves Good?

Before you read

Imagine a powerful group calling its own members good. The word distinguishes people of their standing from everyone below them. Now imagine people praising someone who helps them and gradually making helpfulness the standard of goodness.

These are different starting points. One begins with the people doing the naming and their sense of superiority. The other begins with the recipients of beneficial actions. Before comparing the explanations, ask what evidence could tell us which process occurred.

The examples are ours. They introduce the conflict that opens the First Essay. Nietzsche disputes an account in which goodness begins with the usefulness of unselfish actions, then develops his own account of aristocratic self-naming.

Reading 1 asked us to examine the value of our values. The first historical investigation now makes that question concrete. Explaining how a judgment developed and deciding whether it deserves authority remain different tasks.

The main question: Who first had the power to call something good, and how would the answer change our understanding of that judgment?

Read Essay I, §§1–7 in sequence. The closing conflict develops within aristocratic valuation before becoming the opposition that dominates the rest of the essay.

Three things to notice

  1. The interpreter’s own motives. In I §1, why does Nietzsche investigate the psychologists themselves while still praising the courage to seek unpleasant truths?
  2. The competing origins. In I §§2–5, distinguish praise from grateful recipients, noble self-affirmation, and the linguistic evidence offered for the latter.
  3. A division within nobility. In I §§6–7, how do priestly and warrior valuations diverge? Keep Nietzsche’s historical proposal separate from his sweeping claims about peoples and their motives.

A way to read the passages

I §1: the investigators become objects of investigation

Nietzsche begins with the English psychologists who have attempted histories of morality. He finds their attraction to unflattering explanations itself interesting. Why do they seek governing causes in habit, forgetting, passivity, or mechanisms that offend human pride? Essay I, §1

He proposes several possible motives: disappointment, hostility, a taste for paradox, or combinations of these. The questions turn psychological suspicion toward the people practicing it. A person exposing the motives of others does not thereby become transparent to themselves.

But follow the turn at the end. Nietzsche refuses the dismissive image of tedious frogs and wishes instead to find courageous investigators capable of sacrificing comforting conclusions to unpleasant truths. He does not simply reject a theory because it wounds human self-esteem.

This opening sets a demanding standard for what follows. Nietzsche’s own preference for an explanation must answer to evidence too. Enjoying an aristocratic origin no more establishes it than enjoying an embarrassing origin establishes the psychologists’ account.

I §2: useful to whom, and named by whom?

The rival genealogy proceeds through several steps. Recipients first praise unselfish actions because those actions benefit them. People later forget that basis of praise. Habit preserves the judgment, until goodness appears inherent in the action. Essay I, §2

Notice what this story could explain: an action’s practical benefit becomes a moral property apparently independent of any benefit. Its ingredients include usefulness, repetition, forgetting, and mistaken interpretation.

Nietzsche attacks its starting point. He claims that the people calling themselves good were the powerful and high-ranking, who experienced their own lives and actions as superior. They did not wait for beneficiaries to confer moral standing upon them.

He calls the sustaining experience the pathos of distance: the felt separation between a dominant group and people it regards as beneath it. This is a social relation of rank and power, not simply an individual possessing healthy confidence.

Imagine a ruling family using a favorable name for its own kind and a dismissive name for the people it governs. The naming would help assert its distinction. That illustrates Nietzsche’s proposal without proving that the history of goodness universally followed it.

His parenthesis about naming extends the point: attaching authoritative names to things can be a way of taking possession. The power to define a hierarchy includes power over the language through which people recognize it.

The claim challenges the supposed original identity of goodness and unselfishness. Nietzsche locates the dominance of the selfish–unselfish opposition later, as aristocratic values decline. He is not merely offering a different explanation for why people always admired altruism; he disputes that they always did.

I §3: a problem inside the story of forgetting

Nietzsche next attacks the rival account on its own terms. If useful conduct continued to benefit people every day, why would its usefulness disappear from consciousness? Repeated experience should reinforce the association, he argues, rather than erase it. Essay I, §3

This is a psychological objection, distinct from his claim that the history began elsewhere. Even without accepting noble self-naming, a reader can ask whether the proposed sequence from usefulness to forgetting makes sense.

Herbert Spencer supplies a comparison. As Nietzsche presents him, Spencer derives goodness from accumulated, unforgotten experience of usefulness. Nietzsche considers that explanation coherent while still rejecting it as true. A consistent possible history and an accurate history are not the same achievement.

His objection also has a limit. People might continue benefiting from a practice while forgetting how it first acquired authority. Present usefulness and knowledge of an origin are distinguishable. Nietzsche would need to show that the rival theory depends on the particular forgetting he declares impossible.

Keep that distinction when evaluating his victory over the rival. Exposing a weakness in one explanation does not independently prove the alternative.

I §§4–5: words become historical evidence

Nietzsche identifies etymology as the clue that directed his inquiry. He claims that across languages the same development occurs: terms for social nobility become terms for goodness, while terms for common or low standing become terms of disapproval. Essay I, §4

His German example connects schlecht, bad, with schlicht, plain or simple. He proposes an earlier social contrast before the later condemnatory meaning. The general movement is from a group’s position to a judgment of character.

Section 5 expands the inquiry. Names for the powerful, wealthy, or commanding identify obvious grounds of distinction. Names for truthfulness can likewise become a noble group’s self-description, then survive as descriptions of personal character after the original social order declines. Essay I, §5

Theognis supplies Nietzsche’s Greek example of aristocratic truthfulness contrasted with common dishonesty. The argument asks how a favorable trait becomes attached to a class and then detached from it, carrying an altered meaning into later judgments.

That is a substantive kind of evidence to seek. It is not self-validating. Each derivation requires linguistic support, and a word’s development does not by itself establish the origin of every practice associated with goodness.

Nietzsche’s claims differ in confidence. He introduces conjectural connections among Latin bonus, warfare, and an older word form; he speculates about German goodness and the Goths; he leaves supporting grounds for the latter outside the work. A careful reader should preserve those differences instead of presenting every proposed derivation as settled knowledge.

Racial speculation enters the historical argument

Section 5 also moves from language into racial history. Nietzsche associates Latin badness with dark-haired earlier inhabitants and contrasts them with blond conquerors. He invokes Gaelic naming and claims about Celtic ancestry, complexion, and skull shape.

He then connects the supposed return of a subordinated population with democracy, anarchism, and socialism. Political movements become possible signs of a conquering race’s physiological decline. This is more than a neutral description of changing class names.

The passage joins speculative etymology to racial hierarchy and Nietzsche’s hostility toward contemporary egalitarian politics. Its confidence does not establish either the ancestry claims or the connection between ancestry and political judgment. We must not repeat these portraits as reliable descriptions of populations.

Section 4’s attack on democratic prejudice and the historian Buckle shows that Nietzsche’s own evaluative commitments were already present. He believes egalitarian assumptions conceal aristocratic origins. We can ask whether his preference for hierarchy also encourages him to find one pattern wherever he looks.

This does not make historical inquiry into moral language pointless. It makes the demand for evidence especially important where a suggested history conveniently supports the investigator’s preferred ranking.

I §6: priests belong within the aristocratic beginning

The priestly caste initially enters as a form of aristocracy. Its distinctions can also move from social standing into personal value. Nietzsche describes clean and unclean as beginning with concrete bodily practices before acquiring wider moral force. Essay I, §6

His examples include washing, dietary restrictions, sexual separation by class, and avoidance of blood. He warns against assuming that these terms began with the elaborate symbolic significance later readers may assign them.

The important structural point is that a priestly hierarchy does not initially stand outside noble valuation. Its distinctive practices supply another way a high-status group understands its superiority. The later opposition must develop from a differentiation within this field.

Nietzsche then describes priestly life through inactivity, intense inward attention, and volatile feelings. He calls its condition diseased and its remedies more damaging than the original sickness. Fasting, sexual abstinence, isolation, and hostility to the senses figure among those remedies.

These are sweeping polemical diagnoses, not reliable medical findings about priests or religious practice. His compressed equation of mystical union, God, and Buddhist nothingness also requires scrutiny rather than automatic adoption.

Yet the conclusion refuses a merely dismissive picture. Priestly culture makes human beings more inwardly complex and interesting; it deepens the soul and intensifies evil. Nietzsche credits the very development he finds dangerous with creating distinctive human depth. Its significance is not exhausted by calling it weakness.

I §7: rivalry turns two aristocracies against each other

Section 7 explains the division through rival forms of power. Warrior nobility values vigorous activity: combat, hunting, adventure, dance, and physical abundance. Priestly valuation can diverge from these aims, especially when priests and warriors compete and the former cannot prevail in direct conflict. Essay I, §7

Nietzsche makes weakness the source of an exceptionally inventive hatred. Revenge changes its means. Instead of defeating the opponent under the opponent’s standards, it changes which qualities count as good at all.

The aristocratic equation associates goodness with nobility, beauty, happiness, and divine favor. The reversal makes poverty, suffering, and powerlessness grounds of exclusive goodness, while the mighty become evil and damned.

This is a change in the organizing opposition, not merely promotion of different people within the old ranking. Traits that previously signified inferiority now authorize a claim to moral superiority.

Nietzsche attributes the decisive historical initiative to Jews, whom he depicts collectively as a priestly people animated by carefully cultivated revenge. His account makes this inversion the beginning of the slave revolt in morality and presents its eventual dominance as a victory whose origins have become hard to see.

The account contains explicit anti-Jewish generalization. Its admiration for the ingenuity and historical power of the reversal does not cancel the portrayal of an entire people through poisonous hatred and revenge. The chapter supplies no basis for treating those motives as facts about Jewish people.

We can examine the proposed mechanism of changing a ranking while refusing that collective diagnosis. We must also ask what evidence would connect the mechanism to the immense history Nietzsche claims for it. A compelling explanation of how a reversal could work is not that history’s demonstration.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does an aristocratic origin make a value better, and a reactive origin make it worse?

Nietzsche’s prose can make the historical contrast feel like its own evaluation. Noble creation appears active and abundant; priestly inversion appears poisoned by weakness. But the qualities of an explanation and the worth of the resulting practice require examination.

A ruling group’s confident self-description could conceal exploitation. People suffering under it might identify injuries that the rulers have no reason to notice. An objection’s origin in powerlessness would not by itself show that its account of those injuries was false.

Conversely, a value can grow from real suffering while encouraging vindictiveness or suppressing capacities worth developing. Calling its origin sympathetic does not finish evaluating it either.

Use three questions to keep the argument precise. What historical change does Nietzsche claim occurred? What psychological process does he propose to explain it? Why should the resulting values be retained, revised, or rejected?

The questions interact without collapsing into one another. The First Essay has begun to provide competing histories and explanations. The Preface’s demand to assess the value of values remains unfinished, and Nietzsche’s own preference for aristocratic life belongs among the judgments to test.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Where do the rival histories locate the first judgment of goodness?

The psychologists’ account begins with recipients praising useful, unselfish acts. Nietzsche begins with dominant groups affirming and naming themselves and their conduct. The disagreement concerns who creates the judgment and what initially grounds it.

Why does Nietzsche find Spencer more coherent without accepting his account?

Spencer’s explanation retains the connection between goodness and repeatedly experienced usefulness. Nietzsche thinks this avoids the rival account’s unexplained forgetting. Coherence makes an explanation possible, not necessarily historically true.

What could etymology establish, and what remains unproved?

A supported derivation could trace a moral term through earlier social meanings. Nietzsche must still establish each derivation and the scope of the generalization. His racial and political extrapolations require evidence beyond linguistic resemblance.

Why should priests not be placed outside aristocratic valuation from the beginning?

Section 6 presents priestly aristocracy as another high-status group whose distinguishing practices acquire moral meaning. Section 7 explains its divergence from warrior values. Nietzsche also credits priestly development with deepening human inwardness despite condemning its dangers.

What is reversed in the slave revolt, and what needs critical attribution?

The qualities formerly associated with low standing become signs of goodness, while the powerful become evil. Nietzsche attributes this process to a collective Jewish revenge. That anti-Jewish historical diagnosis is his claim, not an established fact about a people.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to a value you considered while reading the Preface. Write 200–300 words:

  1. State what you currently think makes the value worth retaining.
  2. Describe two possible histories of its authority, using the difference between self-naming and praise from beneficiaries.
  3. Name evidence that could distinguish those histories. Separate what you know from your conjectures.
  4. Explain how a change in power might alter its place within a ranking.
  5. Say which part of your original evaluation the proposed history would change, and which part would require further reasons.

A successful response does not invent ancestry or hidden motives for a whole population. It makes a specific hypothesis answerable to evidence.
