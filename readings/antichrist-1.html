What Makes a Value Worth Defending?

The Antichrist — Complete Reading Course · Reading 1 of 12

Read: Nietzsche’s Preface in full; §§1–7. Read every assigned section in full.
Allow: About 85–115 minutes for the text, commentary, rereading, questions, and writing. You may divide this lesson across two sittings.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann in The Portable Nietzsche. Free alternative: Open the reading in H. L. Mencken’s translation.

Begin with Nietzsche’s Preface, then read §§1–7. Mencken’s preceding Introduction is his own essay and is not assigned. Stop before §8.

Mencken’s notes are editorial material, separate from Nietzsche’s numbered text. Words such as pity, sympathy, and compassion vary across translations. Follow section numbers and the argument’s context. The explanations below are original commentary; see the edition guidance.

Before you read

Begin with a conviction you would usually defend without much hesitation: compassion is good; people deserve equal consideration; honesty should take precedence over comfort. Nietzsche asks what such convictions cultivate, what they prevent, and why they have acquired authority. You do not have to abandon a conviction to investigate it. You do have to let the investigation reach its assumptions.

This opening also makes demands that deserve resistance. It recruits an exceptional readership, ranks human lives, and calls for the destruction of people it devalues. These are part of the argument’s substance. Reading closely means understanding the standards Nietzsche proposes while asking whether those standards justify what he wants them to justify. Keep your opening conviction in your notes: the final lesson returns to it.

The main question: What would make a value worth defending if its moral reputation could no longer count as its justification?

Three things to notice

  1. The reader Nietzsche invents. The Preface describes unusual honesty, independence, and endurance, but also superiority and contempt. Notice how the invitation to courage can make disagreement feel like a confession of weakness.
  2. Definitions that already take sides. In §2, power and weakness organize good, bad, and happiness. Ask whether Nietzsche is reporting familiar meanings, proposing replacements, or supplying reasons to accept them.
  3. Two different objections to pity. §7 says pity spreads suffering and that it preserves lives Nietzsche thinks should perish. The first is a claim about effects; the second also depends on a judgment about whose continued existence is desirable.

A way to read the passages

The Preface makes the audience part of the dispute

The Preface imagines readers capable of hearing what their contemporaries cannot hear. Its reference to Zarathustra links this book to a work concerned with teaching, misunderstanding, and the difficulty of finding companions. Nietzsche expects a reader who can endure an unwelcome conclusion without first asking whether it will be personally profitable. This is a demanding conception of intellectual honesty: inquiry should not have to flatter its investigator.

Yet the same invitation offers a flattering identity. The reader might belong among the rare, the independent, the people strong enough to face forbidden questions. In §1 the mythical Hyperboreans become an image of distance from modern habits. They are not an actual geographical community to which the reader can travel. Nietzsche uses them to dramatize a different climate of judgment, where direction and accumulated strength replace weary compromise.

The invitation therefore needs a double reading. What habits would actually make you better able to examine an argument? Which descriptions merely reward you for identifying with the speaker? Refusing an argument because it is offensive and accepting it because it feels courageous can both prevent examination. Keep the distinction between courage in inquiry and the truth of the conclusion courage makes it possible to hear.

§2 proposes a scale of value

The rapid definitions in §2 give power an organizing role. Happiness concerns increasing power and overcoming resistance. The contrast with mere contentment makes difficulty potentially valuable: a demanding piece of music or a difficult inquiry may develop capacities that uninterrupted comfort would leave unused. That example helps explain one attraction of the proposal, but it does not exhaust its meaning.

Nietzsche immediately turns from an account of developed capacities to a demand that weak and unsuccessful people perish and that others help bring this about. The formulation is destructive and dehumanizing. It cannot be adequately explained as advice to discard your own limiting beliefs. Nor does the passage specify a complete institutional program whose details a reader could simply report. What is explicit is a demand directed against people, connected to his rejection of practical pity.

Now ask what the opening definitions accomplish. A definition can tell us how a writer intends to use a word. It does not by itself prove that increased power is the right standard, that all forms of power are valuable, or that someone’s dependence licenses their destruction. Nietzsche makes the stakes visible early. A close reading should preserve the distance between his standard and the reasons that would be needed to defend it.

The object of cultivation is a human type

§3 asks what kind of human being should be willed and cultivated. Its question concerns the conditions under which a valued kind of life can emerge, rather than a simple prediction of a new biological species replacing humanity. Nietzsche thinks exceptional possibilities have occurred, but often as accidents and objects of fear. He charges Christianity with cultivating the opposite type.

§4 then separates historical change from improvement. Later does not necessarily mean better. This is a useful distinction even if we reject his comparison of modern Europe with the Renaissance. Technological development, increased wealth, and moral progress are different claims; none automatically establishes the others. But Nietzsche also needs evidence for the historical rankings he substitutes for the progress story. Naming a preferred epoch does not supply that evidence.

The language of cultivation reaches beyond solitary self-improvement. It raises questions about institutions, education, collective selection, and whose possibilities a culture supports. When §5 presents Christianity as hostile to higher human development, ask what counts as higher, whose testimony establishes the supposed hostility, and whether a society could cultivate excellence while also protecting people who need assistance. Nietzsche’s formulation does not settle that last question. In §5, Pascal supplies Nietzsche’s example: he attributes to Christianity the damage Pascal associated with original sin. This is Nietzsche’s reversal of an explanation, not an established account of Pascal’s life.

Decadence is an explanation that contains a judgment

In §6 Nietzsche distinguishes his account of corruption from a conventional moral accusation. He calls a life decadent when it chooses what damages its own sustaining and developing capacities. The accusation is thus meant to expose a contradiction within a form of life: what it reveres allegedly works against it. An ideal can look elevated while producing impoverishment.

That makes the inquiry more interesting than a simple exchange of insults. A demanding moral practice might strengthen judgment and responsibility, or it might teach someone to distrust every impulse and seek relief only outside earthly existence. To assess Nietzsche’s account, we would need to examine the practice, its effects, and possible alternative explanations.

The physiological vocabulary does not turn his evaluation into a medical finding. He moves among individual people, cultural formations, species, and ideals. The relevant meaning of health or growth can change with that movement. Ask which capacity is being damaged and how he knows. Calling the judgment nonmoral also does not make it value-free: power, growth, and a preferred kind of human flourishing still organize it.

The attack on pity requires more than one answer

The first line of argument in §7 concerns the multiplication of suffering. Witnessing another’s distress can make the witness suffer too. That observation invites further questions. Does the response also produce effective help? Does shared concern reduce isolation? Is distress necessarily a net loss of capacity? Nietzsche’s description of emotional contagion does not answer all of these questions, even if it identifies a real possibility.

His further objection is more radical. Pity allegedly interferes with selection by preserving those he judges unfit for continued life. Here a claim about how nature operates is joined to a claim about what people should permit or promote. Even an accurate account of a natural process would not, without another argument, establish an obligation to reproduce that process in social conduct. His language of natural selection should not be treated as a demonstrated scientific basis for the prescription.

Finally, Nietzsche connects pity with Schopenhauer and the denial of life. The issue becomes whether suffering makes existence itself seem something from which deliverance is needed. This is one reason translation matters: pity, sympathy, and compassion can suggest different relations to the sufferer. Specify the relation under discussion. A superior person’s condescending pity, attentive practical care, and an ideal that treats all existence as a mistake should not be assumed identical merely because they share a word. The section ends by casting the philosopher as a physician who must attack accumulated pity without mercy. Ask what this image of treatment authorizes—and what the image itself cannot demonstrate about the people or practices it judges.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can we keep the inquiry into values without accepting Nietzsche’s ranking of people?

We can distinguish an investigative question from the answer Nietzsche gives it. Asking what a practice cultivates does not logically force us to accept his exclusionary standard. But this distinction should not become a way of describing his own position as more inclusive than it is. §§2–7 tie the investigation to preferred human types and to the condemnation of people judged weak.

A useful response therefore has two stages: reconstruct his account, then state where your argument departs from it. You might accept that care can sometimes become controlling or incapacitating while maintaining that vulnerability never removes a person’s claim to consideration. That is a position to defend with reasons. It is neither established nor refuted by describing it as compassionate.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

What does the Preface ask of its reader, and what temptation accompanies that demand?

It asks for intellectual integrity, independence, and endurance of difficult truths. The accompanying temptation is to treat membership among the supposedly rare and strong as evidence that one has understood or that Nietzsche must be right. An attractive reader identity cannot substitute for assessment of an argument.

Does §2 prove that power should determine value?

It proposes power as the standard and formulates judgments through it. A proposed definition does not establish that the standard deserves acceptance or that destructive prescriptions follow legitimately from it. Those are further argumentative burdens.

Why is the distinction between change and improvement important in §§3–4?

It prevents a later historical condition from counting automatically as a better one. Nietzsche wants deliberate cultivation of valued human possibilities. His own preferred types and historical rankings nevertheless require justification; rejecting inevitable progress does not prove his alternative.

What does decadence mean in §6?

It names a pattern in which a life favors what Nietzsche takes to damage its instincts and capacities for growth and power. The description aims to expose self-undermining valuation. It remains evaluative and requires evidence; it is not a neutral clinical diagnosis.

Why are the two objections to pity in §7 not interchangeable?

The claim that pity spreads distress concerns its effects and invites comparison with the help it may enable. The claim that it preserves people who should perish depends on a further, dehumanizing judgment about whose life should continue. Answering one claim does not answer the other.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 250–350 words about one conviction concerning care or moral responsibility that you want to examine throughout this course. For example: compassion is always good, or everyone deserves equal consideration.

  1. State the conviction precisely. Define its central term and give a concrete, nonmedical example of what following it would involve.
  2. Explain your strongest reason for holding it. Separate an observation about what happens from your judgment about what ought to matter.
  3. Use one passage from §§2–7 to formulate Nietzsche’s strongest relevant challenge. Give its section number and explain it in your own words.
  4. State one objection to his challenge and one condition under which you would revise your own conviction. Address any difference between evaluating a practice and devaluing a person.

Keep this page. Reading 12 will ask you to return to it after considering Jesus, belief, interpretation, and Nietzsche’s defense of rank. The aim is a better supported judgment, not a predetermined conversion.
