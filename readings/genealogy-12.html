Before you read

Return to the value you chose in Reading 1 and the sentence you wrote about what a history of that value might change. Keep those notes beside you. The final assignment asks why the search for a truthful explanation should itself command our allegiance. A course that has repeatedly requested evidence now has to examine the value guiding that request.

The preceding lesson ended with science as a possible opponent of the ascetic ideal. Read Third Essay §§24–28 for Nietzsche’s answer, following three different questions: whether a claim is true, why someone wants to discover it, and whether truth deserves priority over every competing consideration. Those questions can influence one another without becoming interchangeable. Explaining a researcher’s motive does not settle the truth of their result.

Finish the essay before writing your final response. Its last paragraph changes the scale of the discussion: the ascetic ideal has preserved a willingness to live by giving suffering a meaning, even while directing that will against life. Ask what Nietzsche thinks this interpretation accomplished, what it cost, and what remains unfinished when its authority becomes questionable.

Three things to notice

  1. An opponent can inherit the commitment it thinks it has escaped. The unbeliever may reject religious doctrine while retaining the unconditional authority of truth. Nietzsche asks how far that rejection has gone.
  2. Truthfulness can turn against its own foundations. The discipline that challenges a religious explanation may eventually challenge the value that required this discipline. The inquiry includes Nietzsche and the readers following him.
  3. A damaging interpretation can still perform an indispensable service. Nietzsche credits the ascetic ideal with making suffering meaningful and preserving the will. His criticism has to account for that achievement as well as its costs.

A way to read the passage

§24: how free are the free spirits?

Third Essay §24 addresses unbelievers who regard their independence from religious authority as established. Nietzsche turns their demand for truth into the object of inquiry. They may have abandoned an otherworldly explanation while retaining the conviction that truth possesses an unconditional value. On his account, that conviction continues the ascetic ideal in an intellectually demanding form.

Unconditional matters. An inquiry can have specific reasons: finding out whether a bridge is safe, understanding an illness, correcting a historical injustice. Nietzsche’s target here is a commitment that places truth beyond any further question about its value. Ask what changes when the demand becomes absolute. Are the ends, costs, and conditions of inquiry still open to consideration? These are questions about the authority of a pursuit; their answers do not make an unsafe bridge safe or a false historical claim accurate.

His criticism of intense conviction works in the same territory. A person’s willingness to sacrifice themselves for a belief does not prove it true. Nietzsche pushes further, treating the need for certainty as a reason for suspicion. Keep the sound distinction from becoming an automatic counterproof: fervor alone establishes neither truth nor falsity. His psychological diagnosis needs support of its own.

The section includes a story about the Assassins and a provocative secret motto. Treat the story as Nietzsche’s illustration of how radical unbelief might unsettle moral permission, rather than as a demonstrated history or his instruction to treat every action as permissible. The argument he develops concerns the remaining faith of modern knowers, who still revere truth even after losing faith in God.

He also challenges the ideal of merely submitting to facts while refusing interpretation. Research requires questions, distinctions, and judgments about importance. Nietzsche argues that science cannot authorize the overriding value of its own pursuit simply by producing more scientific findings. Recalling The Gay Science §344, he asks what sustains the belief that truth should be sought at any price. The next task is a criticism of the value of truth itself. That announcement opens a problem; it has not yet supplied its solution.

§25: why scientific progress may leave the ideal intact

§25 intensifies the claim. Nietzsche claims that science never creates its own governing values and always needs a value-creating ideal. He presents science and the ascetic ideal as necessarily allied through their shared, unquestioned valuation of truth, even when scientific discoveries undermine religious beliefs. A new account of how the world works does not necessarily change what a culture treats as the highest purpose of knowing. Nietzsche questions the governing commitment beneath the disagreement over particular claims.

Art appears as a more fundamental possible opponent because it can give appearance, invention, and illusion a legitimate place. Remember the beginning of the Third Essay: Wagner’s art could also serve ascetic values. The contrast here therefore cannot mean that every artwork liberates its audience or that every scientific inquiry impoverishes life. It concerns whether appearance must always be condemned before the tribunal of unconditional truth. An affirmative relationship to artistic invention offers a different possibility, whose specific forms still require judgment.

Nietzsche’s account of scientific modernity also contains sweeping claims about exhaustion, lowered vitality, and human self-belittlement. His list connects democracy, arbitration in place of war, and equal rights for women with what he calls decline. These political and gendered judgments are part of his evaluation. The physiological vocabulary does not turn them into established medical findings, and their presence prevents us from treating his criterion of health as politically innocent. Ask what he values, what he dismisses, and what evidence could warrant those dismissals.

The discussion of Copernicus develops a different but related possibility. Displacing humanity from the center of the cosmos may diminish human self-importance without producing an affirmative alternative. Nietzsche reads modern accounts of human smallness as capable of continuing an older movement of self-abasement. This is his interpretation of a cultural meaning attached to discovery; a discovery itself does not dictate that response. The same astronomical knowledge could be interpreted in other ways.

Likewise, his criticism of the limits placed on knowledge asks whether the inaccessible can become a new object of reverence. Acknowledging a limit need not automatically amount to worshipping what lies beyond it. Distinguish the position he attacks from the wider range of reasons someone might have for intellectual modesty. Across the section, the recurring question is whether a supposed emancipation has changed the ideal by which its own achievement is measured.

§26: the historian, the spectator, and moral performance

§26 turns to historical scholarship. Nietzsche attacks an ideal of recording events as though the historian could become a neutral mirror without judgment, direction, or commitment. Compare §12: perspectival objectivity required an ability to bring several views under disciplined consideration. It did not require the disappearance of every interest. A history stripped of avowed purposes can still express a valuation, including a resignation about whether anything deserves affirmation.

He is also hostile to the theatrical spectator who turns detachment into an attractive pose. His distinction between a serious ascetic commitment and a counterfeit one matters: he can prefer the rigor of an actual opponent to a fashionable performance of having transcended every ideal. This is consistent with his earlier willingness to grant the ascetic priest intelligence, discipline, and a powerful response to suffering.

The long attack on moral agitation includes an explicit denunciation of organized anti-Semites and their Christian-Aryan posturing. Read this passage alongside the anti-Jewish polemic and racialized history in the First Essay. His opposition to that movement does not erase those features of his own writing. Both belong to the text, and neither can responsibly be used to make the other disappear.

The larger target is a culture that seeks stimulation through indignation, fashionable ideals, and displays of righteousness. A declared moral purpose may help an audience avoid a more difficult encounter with itself. That diagnosis should also be turned toward the writer. Nietzsche’s furious satire can stimulate its readers too. Does the anger clarify the position he attacks, or sometimes make its rejection pleasurable before its argument has been assessed? The question follows the book’s method instead of placing its author outside it.

§27: the conscience of inquiry turns inward

§27 gives the historical argument its decisive turn. Nietzsche presents uncompromising atheism as a late result of the Christian cultivation of truthfulness. The demand for honesty eventually makes inherited religious explanations impossible to maintain. A discipline produced within a tradition can undermine that tradition’s explicit doctrines.

He recalls The Gay Science §357 to describe this development. The moral demand to be truthful outlives the beliefs it helps dissolve. The next question is whether that demand can survive the same scrutiny. What warrants its authority? What in us insists on truth? Nietzsche predicts more than continued examination: Christian morality will destroy itself through its own truthfulness, just as that truthfulness destroyed Christian dogma. He asserts that morality will collapse as the will to truth becomes a problem for itself. This proposed self-dissolution is the outcome he expects; the forecast still needs to be assessed.

This is a useful place to distinguish self-overcoming from a simple victory over someone else. The commitment turns against a foundation or assumption of its own practice. The knower cannot finish by diagnosing only religious people, political opponents, or less sophisticated readers. The Preface’s problem of our being unknown to ourselves returns at the point where our confidence in inquiry seems strongest.

Nietzsche frames the coming conflict in sweeping terms, projecting a prolonged European drama. Read that forecast as a claim about an unfolding problem and his expectations for it. Its rhetorical force does not establish a historical law or guarantee the future it describes. Similarly, his reference to a planned work called The Will to Power announces a project; it is not a reference to a completed, authorized book containing a settled answer.

There is an apparent difficulty in using truthful inquiry to question the value of truth. It becomes a direct contradiction only if every truthful judgment already commits us to truth’s unconditional superiority to every other value. Nietzsche’s question invites us to examine that supposed implication. Specific reasons to prefer an accurate account can remain available while an absolute command to know at any cost comes under scrutiny. How far this response succeeds is a philosophical question the ending leaves readers to pursue.

§28: what the ascetic ideal accomplished

§28 gathers the essay around the meaning of suffering. Nietzsche argues that the deepest problem was suffering without an answer to why it should be endured. The ascetic ideal supplied an interpretation, made the sufferer’s existence intelligible, and helped preserve the will. His criticism therefore credits the ideal with an enormous achievement.

The cost was the direction given to that will. Suffering became connected with guilt; bodily existence, change, and desire could become objects of hostility. The interpretation increased suffering even as it answered a need for meaning. This completes the distinction developed across the priest’s treatments: helping a person continue does not by itself settle the value of the account through which they continue.

Nietzsche’s claim that this ideal supplied the only meaning available hitherto is exceptionally broad. Read it as a thesis demanding historical argument, not as a fact already established about every culture and every sufferer. The essay offers a powerful account of one kind of interpretation while also advancing a sweeping claim about its historical dominance. Those claims require different amounts of evidence.

The ending distinguishes a will directed toward nothingness from the absence of willing. An ideal can keep desire active while turning it against the conditions of life. The point describes the paradoxical achievement of the ascetic ideal; it is not an instruction to embrace self-destruction. Nietzsche has made the problem of an alternative urgent, but he has not ended with a complete replacement morality or a universal method for making suffering worthwhile.

Return to your opening value with that unfinished task in view. Learning its history might change the authority it claims, reveal costs that were hidden, or suggest different reasons for preserving it. What would justify your judgment now? That question remains more demanding than either inheriting the value unquestioningly or rejecting it because its history is uncomfortable.

A difficulty to keep in view

What authorizes the critic’s own standard of life? Nietzsche asks whether values obstruct or enhance human possibilities, but the meaning and distribution of that enhancement need examination. The hierarchy visible in §25 makes the issue concrete: greater power or vitality for whom, at whose expense, and measured how? We have not escaped a questionable value merely by replacing its vocabulary with health, strength, or affirmation.

Apply the same care to the course’s recurring demand for evidence. We have treated distinctions, accurate reading, and reasoned objections as valuable practices. We should be able to explain what they enable: a better understanding of a claim, a fairer assessment of people, or a more responsible choice. Giving such reasons is different from declaring inquiry beyond every question about its purposes. The ending asks us to accept that burden of justification ourselves.

Check your understanding

Try each question before opening its answer. Include the section number that supports your response.

1. Why might an atheist still belong to the ascetic ideal?

In §§24 and 27, Nietzsche argues that rejecting religious doctrine can leave an unconditional faith in truth intact. The commitment to truthfulness may have been cultivated by the very tradition whose beliefs it undermines. Atheism alone therefore does not establish that its governing valuation has been overcome.

2. Does questioning the value of truth mean that every claim is equally true?

No. Whether a claim is accurate differs from why truth should be pursued and whether that pursuit deserves unconditional priority. Nietzsche asks about the authority of the commitment. His challenge does not itself erase distinctions among accurate, mistaken, and unsupported claims.

3. Why can art oppose the ideal more fundamentally than science?

In §25, art can affirm appearance and invention where an unconditional will to truth treats them as subordinate or suspect. This is a possible relation to the ideal, not a guarantee attached to every artwork. The opening discussion of Wagner already showed that an artist can serve ascetic values.

4. What does the ascetic ideal achieve, and what does that achievement cost?

According to §28, it gives suffering meaning and thereby preserves willing. Its interpretation also deepens guilt and directs the will against bodily, changing life. Nietzsche’s criticism recognizes the need it answered while challenging both its explanation and its consequences.

5. Why must the conclusion return to the knower?

The discipline of truthfulness can turn against its own inherited authority in §27. Nietzsche and his readers cannot exempt their desire to know from the inquiry into values. This returns to the Preface’s concern with our limited self-knowledge and leaves the critic’s standards open to examination.

What should I write in my notes?

Write a final response of 350–450 words about the value you chose in Reading 1. Reread Preface §6 and select one relevant passage from each essay. Use essay and section numbers to make your reasoning traceable.

  1. Recover the starting judgment. State what you originally thought made this value good, and which part of that confidence the book has tested.
  2. Explain a history without treating it as a verdict. Use the First and Second Essays to identify a possible formation or transformation of the value. Say what evidence would be needed before accepting that account.
  3. Assess a meaning and its cost. Use the Third Essay to ask what need the value answers and what its interpretation makes possible or difficult. Give a reason for your judgment rather than relying on the value’s age or popularity.
  4. Include yourself in the inquiry. Explain what you hope truthful investigation of this value will enable. Identify a commitment in your own assessment that still needs defense.

Then revise your opening sentence: “A history of this value would change my judgment if it showed…” Your conclusion may retain, qualify, or reject the value. What matters is that the reasons now distinguish its formation, its effects, and the standard by which you assess them.
