The Hero and the Cost of Wisdom

Before you read

A judge follows the evidence carefully. Each answer brings the investigation closer to success—and the judge closer to discovering that he himself is implicated. Nietzsche finds this terrible reversal in Oedipus. The same intelligence that makes the hero admirable carries him toward ruin.

Prometheus presents another difficulty. He brings humanity the fire on which its culture depends, yet obtaining that gift violates divine authority. His achievement and his punishment belong to the same action.

In §8 the chorus gives Dionysian suffering a visible figure; §9 turns to Oedipus and Prometheus to ask what such a clear, individual figure lets us confront. Nietzsche reads their clarity and dignity against the terror within their myths. We will follow his interpretations of Sophocles and Aeschylus, then test the general claims he draws from them.

The main question: What happens to our idea of a good life when wisdom and creative power can themselves bring catastrophe?

You do not need to know both plays beforehand. On your first pass, mark the change from Oedipus to Prometheus. Then notice where Nietzsche moves from discussing their stories to making claims about whole peoples, sexes, and the nature of existence.

What to notice

  1. The bright figure against the dark background. What does Nietzsche’s opening comparison with afterimages suggest about the clear language and visible beauty of a tragic hero?
  2. Wisdom and suffering. How does knowing more change Oedipus’s situation? How does the value of Prometheus’s gift complicate the meaning of his punishment?
  3. Conflicting claims to justice. Near the end, what happens when divine and human worlds each assert their own right? Can their conflict be settled by declaring one side simply evil?

Close reading

The hero as a healing image

Nietzsche opens with the precision of Sophoclean dialogue. We seem able to enter the hero’s inner life immediately. He then reverses an optical comparison: after confronting terrible darkness, the imagination produces bright figures that help it recover. These are the Apollonian appearances of the hero. §9, the bright image and Oedipus

The image does more than soften a story’s subject. Clear speech, recognizable character, and an intelligible dramatic sequence let an audience attend to what would otherwise overwhelm it. Greek cheerfulness, in this interpretation, belongs to the artistic treatment of terror. It gives us no reason to suppose that the lives represented are comfortable or secure.

Oedipus: solving the case destroys the solver

Nietzsche describes the Oedipus drama as an investigation that the judge unravels to his own destruction. The audience can take pleasure in its skillful unfolding while discovering something appalling.

He then turns to Oedipus at Colonus. The suffering, aged Oedipus gains a beneficent significance extending beyond his death. Nietzsche contrasts that final power with his earlier deliberate striving. This is what his opposition between activity and passivity concerns: successful agency may take a form that conscious mastery could not achieve. §9, Oedipus at Colonus and the underlying myth

Behind both poetic treatments Nietzsche hears a harsher mythic thought. The solver of nature’s riddle also violates its most sacred relationships. He reads wisdom as a transgression whose force turns against the knower. His appeal to a supposed Persian belief helps him construct this connection; it is not a demonstration that knowledge actually requires such violations.

Prometheus: creation comes with a claim against it

Prometheus gives Nietzsche an active counterpart to Oedipus. The hero helps humanity acquire what it needs to make a culture. Fire matters because it concentrates the value of that achievement: something indispensable is obtained through a trespass against the gods.

If we call the trespass merely wicked, we miss the value of the human achievement. If we call the achievement merely progress, we miss the conflict and suffering bound up with it. Nietzsche gives the deed dignity while keeping the punishment in view. He hears the artist’s pride in creative power, too: the capacity to make a world can challenge authorities that once seemed untouchable. §9, creative power and the Promethean myth

Notice the change of scale. Aeschylus’s hero becomes an image of the creator, and then of culture’s origin. Nietzsche proposes that humanity’s highest gains carry a cost rooted in the structure of existence. The passage’s force depends on whether that larger interpretation clarifies the story or burdens it with a conclusion the story cannot establish.

A contrast built from unequal standards

Nietzsche compares Prometheus with the biblical Fall using the labels Aryan and Semitic. He assigns dignity to an active transgression and associates the other story with supposedly feminine weaknesses. His racial and gender hierarchy is part of the reasoning on the page: one kind of wrongdoing receives admiration while another is reduced to seduction and weakness. §9, the comparison of myths

We should ask what the comparison has earned. Has he shown that these myths express fixed traits of peoples or sexes? He has supplied a sharply unequal interpretation, then generalized it. His reading offers no adequate basis for those sweeping judgments. Recognizing that weakness lets us test the more specific claim about Prometheus without accepting the hierarchy used to elevate him.

Justice without a simple acquittal

Near the end, Nietzsche imagines separate divine and human worlds, each asserting a right. The individual who tries to cross their boundaries encounters the contradiction between them. Prometheus becomes Dionysian in that expansive striving, while the concern for distinct rights and limits retains an Apollonian dimension. §9, competing worlds and the concluding formula

His closing formula is: “Whatever exists is alike just and unjust, and equally justified in both.” §9, conclusion This is more radical than saying disputes have two sides. In his account, contradiction belongs to existence itself: individuation creates rival claims, while Dionysian striving pushes beyond it. Tragedy gives that conflict an artistic form.

That formulation leaves a difficult question: what can it say to the individual who bears the cost? A perspective that makes the whole intelligible may still fail to justify what happens to a particular person. Keep that gap open as the book turns toward attempts to make suffering rationally comprehensible.

A difficulty

Does giving transgression dignity also excuse the suffering it causes?

Nietzsche asks us to admire wisdom and creation even where their consequences are terrible. That can challenge the comforting expectation that virtue guarantees happiness. Yet admiration may also concentrate our attention on the exceptional hero while leaving other people’s suffering at the edge of the picture.

Return to his presentation of Oedipus: how much attention do the father and mother receive apart from their place in the hero’s destiny? Then consider Prometheus: does the cultural value of fire settle whether every cost attached to acquiring it is justified?

The task is to separate three judgments: understanding why a deed occurs, recognizing something valuable in it, and approving its consequences. Find where Nietzsche brings those judgments close together. Decide whether his tragic account can retain their differences without losing its force.

Check your understanding

Try an answer before opening each suggested response.

Why is Oedipus more than an example of someone punished for being bad?

Nietzsche presents a noble and intelligent figure whose inquiry exposes his own catastrophic situation. His suffering exceeds an ordinary lesson about avoiding wicked choices. The problem concerns the relation between wisdom, fate, and the limits of agency. Nietzsche also finds a later beneficent significance in Oedipus’s suffering at Colonus.

What makes Prometheus’s deed tragic rather than simply heroic?

The gift advances human culture through a violation of divine authority, with suffering attached to it. Its value does not remove the conflict; the conflict does not erase its value. Nietzsche uses this double significance to connect the expansive Dionysian impulse with Apollonian boundaries and justice.

Must we accept the racial and gender comparison to understand Nietzsche’s Prometheus?

No. We can examine the relation between cultural creation, violated authority, and suffering through his discussion of the deed itself. His generalizations about peoples and sexes require their own justification, which this passage does not provide. Separating the claims also lets us ask how the unequal comparison influences his admiration.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering: Does Nietzsche’s reading of either Oedipus or Prometheus explain suffering, justify it, or do both?

Choose one hero. Explain the achievement Nietzsche values and the suffering connected to it. Use two details from §9, then distinguish making a catastrophe intelligible from showing that it is right.

A successful response treats the hero as more than a moral example, identifies the grounds of Nietzsche’s interpretation, and states a reasoned limit or defense of it. End with the strongest question someone who bears the cost could ask.
