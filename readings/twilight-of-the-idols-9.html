What Does Art Intensify?

Before you read

Keep your own edition open. Kaufmann remains the preferred translation; Ludovici calls this chapter “Skirmishes in a War with the Age.” The section numbers let you move between them.

Recall the distinction between a judgment and an explanation of the person making it. Nietzsche frequently moves between those tasks here. Record one place where the movement clarifies the argument and one where it substitutes for an argument.

The main question: When Nietzsche describes art as an expression of increased power, what does this explain about creation, and what does it leave unproved?

Three things to notice

  1. The critic inside the portrait In §§1–6, separate a named writer, Nietzsche’s charge against that writer, and the evidence supplied for the charge. A memorable insult may supply only the middle item.
  2. Different ways of making Track the sequence from artistic selection in §7 to intensified feeling in §§8–9, expressive transformation in §10, and architecture in §11. These are connected claims, not interchangeable definitions.
  3. Which kind of strength? Compare the noisy certainty criticized in §12, Emerson’s cheerfulness in §13, and the survival of Nietzsche’s preferred exceptional people in §14. Notice when the standard changes.

A way to read the passages

A gallery of judgments: §§1–4

The chapter begins with compressed attacks on writers, musicians, and philosophers. You need not recognize every name to identify the method. Nietzsche replaces a cultural reputation with a vivid bodily or theatrical image. Seneca’s virtue becomes a performance; Kant’s philosophical standing is punctured by wordplay. The images make an established judgment briefly strange. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate that the reputation is undeserved. Read this opening as an exhibition of critical taste whose grounds still require examination.

The portraits become more specific in §§2–4. Renan, a historian of religion, is charged with combining intellectual freedom with an inherited Christian valuation of humility. Sainte-Beuve, a literary critic, is accused of using apparent objectivity to avoid judging greatness on his own authority. The devotional Imitation of Christ and Comte’s religious project become further targets. Nietzsche also uses femininity and national identity as terms of disparagement. Those prejudices are part of how the polemic works; translating them into neutral criticism would hide a defect in his reasoning.

Can inherited morality justify itself? §§5–6

The criticism of George Eliot in §5 gives the opening portraits a more substantial philosophical question. Nietzsche claims that people can abandon belief in God while retaining Christian moral judgments as self-evident truths. Their confidence then reveals the depth of an inheritance rather than an independent justification. Notice the difference between the psychological claim that a conviction was inherited and the argumentative claim that it cannot survive without its original theology. Nietzsche makes both, but establishing the first does not establish the second.

An obligation to keep promises, for example, might be learned in religious childhood and later defended through trust, mutual dependence, or the protection of other people’s plans. Whether that new defense succeeds requires argument. Its historical origin cannot settle the matter. The attack on George Sand in §6 returns to aesthetic accusation: Nietzsche treats her generous sentiment as manufactured and her writing as mechanically produced. His misogynistic portrait supplies no adequate evidence about her creative process. The question worth carrying forward is how one distinguishes expressed feeling from staged feeling without pretending to see directly inside another person.

Why the artist is not a collecting machine: §7

Section 7 attacks the deliberate collection of observations as a sufficient model of psychology or art. Nietzsche imagines writers accumulating tiny details until the finished work becomes a heap. His alternative emphasizes a selective process that operates before a completed artistic insight reaches consciousness. An artist does not simply carry the world indoors intact. Attention, memory, compression, and form have already transformed it. His criticism is therefore about the organization of experience, not merely about whether an artist owns a notebook.

Nevertheless, his prohibition on purposeful observation overreaches. A painter can study a hand carefully and still compose a powerful picture; a novelist can collect details without surrendering judgment to them. We should distinguish his strong insight about the insufficiency of raw accumulation from his unsupported claim that deliberate observation betrays weakness. Apply the same distinction to psychological interpretation. The fact that understanding involves selection does not excuse a critic from checking the facts being selected. Otherwise the portraits in the opening sections become immune to correction.

Intensification and idealization: §§8–9

Nietzsche now proposes a bodily prerequisite for artistic activity. Kaufmann’s intoxication and Ludovici’s ecstasy name a heightened condition that can arise from many sources: desire, victory, collective celebration, exertion, or a surge of willing. Substance use is one item in his list, not his definition of artistic creation. The decisive feature is a felt increase of strength and abundance. Nietzsche gives a philosophical psychology of creation here, using physiological language; these pages do not establish a scientific law that every work of art requires the same bodily condition.

Idealization then means intensifying principal features until lesser details recede. Imagine a portrait whose arrangement makes its subject’s guarded posture dominate the room. The artist has not merely removed blemishes or made the subject prettier. Form gives selected features an enlarged force. In §9 this process becomes the projection of the maker’s abundance into things. Nietzsche contrasts it with impoverishing, life-denying interpretation, using Christianity as his target. His dismissal of Raphael as insufficiently Christian, however, risks defining every counterexample out of existence. A theory cannot prove itself by reclassifying all inconvenient artists.

Apollo, Dionysus, and the architectural exception: §§10–11

Readers of The Birth of Tragedy will recognize the Apollonian and Dionysian. Here Nietzsche presents them as different forms of artistic intensification. The Apollonian chiefly heightens vision: the painter, sculptor, or epic poet creates compelling images. The Dionysian involves the whole expressive body, its capacity to enter gestures, emotions, and roles. Music becomes a specialized remnant of this richer bodily expressiveness. Treat the account of that specialization as Nietzsche’s proposed genealogy, not an established history of music.

Section 11 connects actors, dancers, musicians, and lyric poets through their expressive instincts, then introduces a crucial exception. Architecture belongs to neither of the two states as just described. It gives material form to a powerful organizing will. A building can persuade, flatter, command, or seem securely indifferent to its spectators. This is why the passage cannot be reduced to a formula assigning every art to Apollo or Dionysus. Ask how architecture makes power perceptible, then ask whose power is displayed and whose labor or exclusion the account leaves out.

Loud conviction and cheerful independence: §§12–13

The portraits of Carlyle and Emerson test cultural authority through two contrasting styles. Nietzsche reads Carlyle’s emphatic reverence for heroes as evidence of a need for certainty that he cannot satisfy. The loudness is compensatory: a person who actually possesses confidence might tolerate questions more easily. Emerson represents a more cheerful selectivity, taking nourishment from what he encounters without treating heaviness as proof of depth. Nietzsche admires the ability to think gratefully without constantly staging a moral emergency.

This contrast helps specify what artistic abundance was supposed to mean. It need not mean volume, aggression, or exhaustive seriousness. Yet psychological diagnosis again outruns available evidence if rhetorical intensity alone is supposed to prove inward insecurity. A person may speak forcefully because an issue is urgent. Cheerfulness can reflect discernment, but it can also reflect evasion. Instead of treating these portraits as settled biographies, ask what evidence would distinguish Nietzsche’s diagnoses from their alternatives. His praise should undergo the scrutiny his attacks invite.

The trouble with “strong”: §14

The section called Anti-Darwin challenges a picture of life organized chiefly by scarcity and a struggle for survival. Nietzsche stresses abundance and rivalry for power, then argues that survival does not necessarily favor the exceptional types he admires. Numbers, cunning, patience, and cooperation can defeat an isolated superior individual. Notice his unusually broad sense of intellect here: it includes tactical restraint and self-control. Cleverness is not automatically on the side he calls strong.

The passage exposes an important distinction between surviving and realizing a preferred human excellence. It does not refute evolutionary theory. Biological fitness is not a certificate of moral, artistic, or intellectual superiority, and Nietzsche’s ranking of human types cannot serve as its scientific definition. His claims that weakness generally wins and that abundance is life’s normal condition also require evidence he does not supply. The lesson therefore ends with a problem for his vocabulary: if art, confidence, domination, health, and exceptionality all suggest power, what warrants treating them as one standard?

A difficulty to keep in view

If Nietzsche explains judgments through their makers, must we explain his own judgments the same way?

Yes, his method invites that question. If Eliot’s moral certainty may express an inheritance, Nietzsche’s admiration for exceptional creators may express one too. This does not automatically make either judgment false. It makes the relation between explanation and justification unavoidable.

A useful reading separates three tasks: identify the valuation, assess its stated reasons, and investigate possible motives or conditions. Psychological explanation can disclose a hidden assumption or suggest a test. It cannot replace every other kind of evidence. Otherwise whichever portrait is most entertaining wins, and philosophical criticism becomes a competition in suspicion.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

What distinction must we preserve when reading the criticism of Eliot in §5?

The historical inheritance of a moral conviction differs from its present justification. Showing that a conviction came through Christianity does not prove that no independent defense is possible.

What does idealization mean in §8?

It means strongly accentuating principal features so that lesser ones recede. It is an active transformation, not simply removing ugly details or copying accurately.

Why is “every art is either Apollonian or Dionysian” inaccurate here?

Section 11 explicitly places architecture outside those two states and associates it with an intensified organizing will that gives power visible form.

Why compare Carlyle with Emerson?

The contrast tests whether intellectual authority requires noisy certainty and solemnity. Nietzsche prefers Emerson’s cheerful selectivity, though neither portrait establishes its psychological diagnosis independently.

Does §14 show that evolution rewards inferior people?

No. It expresses Nietzsche’s objection to identifying survival with his preferred excellence. His human rankings and broad claims about weakness are not demonstrated biological conclusions.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 350–500 words answering: Can Nietzsche’s account of artistic intensification explain a work without excusing an unsupported judgment about its maker?

  1. Choose one actual artwork you know well and describe one observable decision about emphasis, form, rhythm, or scale. Separate the observation from your inference about the maker.
  2. Use §§7–9 to explain how selection and intensification might illuminate that decision. Use §10 or §11 to specify the kind of artistic activity involved.
  3. Introduce a serious objection using one portrait from §§1–6 or §§12–13: when might the critic mistake a persuasive image of a person for evidence about that person?
  4. Answer the objection and name one claim your example supports and one it cannot establish. Return briefly to §14 if your argument relies on the word strong.

Keep the work itself in view. A successful response explains an artistic feature and marks the limits of the psychological story offered about it.
