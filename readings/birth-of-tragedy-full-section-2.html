Nature's Artists and the Greek Festival

Before you read

Reading 1 introduced Apollo and Dionysus through experiences you could try to imagine: dreaming, and losing yourself in a crowd's song. §2 begins by changing the point of view. Nietzsche now treats the two as powers that burst forth "from nature herself, without the mediation of the human artist." On this view, the human artist is secondary: an "imitator" of what nature is already doing.

Then Nietzsche turns to the Greeks. He asks how far these powers developed in them, and he answers in two very different tones. About Greek dreams he says he can speak only conjecturally. About the difference between Greek and non-Greek Dionysian festivals he says he need not speak conjecturally at all.

The main question: What changes when Apollo and Dionysus become powers of nature working through artists, and on what grounds does Nietzsche separate the Greek Dionysian from the festivals he calls barbarian?

Read §2 once. Mark every place where Nietzsche tells you how confident he is: "conjecturally," "we can prove," "it would seem." Those marks will matter.

What to notice

  1. The artist as imitator. If nature is the first artist, what is left for the human artist to do? Notice the figure who sinks down apart from the revelling chorus.
  2. Two degrees of confidence. Which claim does Nietzsche call conjecture, and which does he say can be proved? Does he supply the proof?
  3. The Apollonian Greek's astonishment. At the end, what does the Greek who watches the Dionysian dithyramb suspect about himself?

Nature as the first artist

Nietzsche calls dream and intoxication "immediate art-states of nature." Neither depends on the individual's talent or culture. Every human artist stands in relation to them as an "imitator": an Apollonian artist in dreams, a Dionysian artist in ecstasy, or, as in Greek tragedy, both at once. He then pictures the double artist as a reveller who sinks down, alone and apart from the dancing chorus. Through Apollonian dream-inspiration, the reveller's oneness with the source of the world appears to him in a "symbolical dream-picture." §2, the art-states of nature

Keep that sleeping reveller in mind. In §5 (station 6), the lyric poet Archilochus becomes exactly this figure.

Use the marks from Reading 1. The phrase "art-states of nature" names an R claim: it says something about what nature is and does, not only about an experience you can recognize. Notice how much of the rest of the section depends on it.

Greek dreams: a conjecture

Nietzsche admits he can speak "only conjecturally, though with a fair degree of certainty," about the dreams of the Greeks. His evidence is their art. From the precision of their eyes and their delight in colour, he infers that even their dreams had the order of their best reliefs. He will call "the dreaming Greeks" Homers, and Homer a dreaming Greek. §2, Greek dreams

This is an inference from what a culture made to how its people experienced the world. It is openly marked as conjecture. Keep that honesty in view, because the next claim is not marked the same way.

Greek and "barbarian" Dionysians

Here, Nietzsche says, he would not have to speak conjecturally. He writes that "from Rome as far as Babylon, we can prove the existence of Dionysian festivals." Their centre, he says, was usually "extravagant sexual licentiousness" and "that detestable mixture of lust and cruelty." For a time the Greeks were protected against these festivals by Apollo, who held out "the Gorgon's head" against them. Nietzsche finds that refusal preserved in Doric art. §2, the non-Greek festivals and Apollo's resistance

The resistance could not last once similar impulses broke out within Greek life itself. Nietzsche describes a reconciliation: the Delphic god took the destructive weapons from his antagonist's hands. Yet "the chasm was not bridged over." What changed was the character of the Dionysian. Among the Greeks, the orgiastic festival became one of "festivals of world-redemption and days of transfiguration." "Not till then," Nietzsche writes, "does the rupture of the principium individuationis become an artistic phenomenon." §2, the reconciliation

The Greek Dionysian keeps a trace of what it has left behind. Pain begets joy; the highest joy breaks into a cry of horror. Nietzsche compares this to the way medicines remind one of deadly poisons. Nature seems to sigh over her dismemberment into individuals.

Notice what the contrast is doing. It lets Nietzsche say that the Greeks did not simply suppress the Dionysian or surrender to it. They turned it into art. That is the claim the rest of the book needs. But the contrast also carries a historical assertion, that festivals across the ancient world had a common "type," and an evaluative label, "barbarian." Neither is supported in this section beyond Nietzsche's say-so.

The music Apollo excluded

Before the Dionysian arrived, Nietzsche says, music seems to have been known to the Greeks only as an Apollonian art: rhythm, "Doric architectonics in tones." Apollo carefully excluded what Nietzsche calls the essence of Dionysian music, and so of music in general: the power of tone, the flowing melody, the world of harmony. In the Dionysian dithyramb, every symbolic power of the body is called on at once: voice, gesture, the dance that moves every limb. §2, Apollonian music and the dithyramb

The claim is that a new experience requires a new kind of symbolism. The whole body becomes expressive because what must be expressed is no longer a clear image but the loss of individual boundaries. Nietzsche adds that only those who have reached the same self-abandonment can understand the dithyrambic reveller.

The Apollonian Greek's suspicion

The section ends with the Apollonian Greek watching the dithyramb in astonishment. His astonishment is mixed with the "shuddering suspicion that all this was in reality not so very foreign to him." His Apollonian consciousness, like a veil, had only hidden this Dionysian world from his view. §2, closing sentences

That suspicion prepares the next station. If Apollonian beauty is a veil over something the Greek already carries within him, what exactly is it veiling? §3 answers with Silenus.

A difficulty

Nietzsche's contrast between Greek and "barbarian" Dionysians does two jobs at once.

The first job is conceptual. The contrast shows what it means for the Dionysian to become artistic: its destructiveness is taken up into a form that can be celebrated and shaped, not simply let loose or suppressed. You can understand, and even find persuasive, this distinction between release and transfiguration without knowing much about ancient festivals.

The second job is historical and evaluative. Nietzsche asserts that festivals "from Rome as far as Babylon" shared a type, calls it barbarian, and says the difference can be proved. That claim needs evidence the section does not give. The label also does rhetorical work: it makes the Greek form look noble by contrast.

Write the two claims in separate sentences. Then ask two questions:

  • Could the first stand if the second turned out to be exaggerated?
  • Where else does the book need the Greeks to be unique, and does it give better evidence there?

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does Nietzsche call every human artist an "imitator"?

Because dream and intoxication are, for him, art-states of nature itself, working without the human artist's mediation. The human artist gives form to powers he did not create: in dreams, in ecstasy, or in both. The claim is metaphysical, so the E/R marks apply: it goes beyond describing an experience.

What makes the Greek Dionysian "artistic," on Nietzsche's account?

Through the reconciliation with Apollo, the rupture of individuation becomes something celebrated and shaped: festivals of redemption and transfiguration, with a new music and a new language of the whole body. The destructive power is not abolished; "the chasm was not bridged over." It is given a form.

Why does the Apollonian Greek feel suspicion as well as astonishment?

Because the Dionysian world is not simply foreign to him. His Apollonian consciousness had veiled it. The suspicion prepares §3, where Olympian beauty is explained as a response to terrors the Greeks already knew.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

What does Nietzsche claim the Greeks did with the Dionysian that others did not, and what would you need to know to believe him?

Explain the difference between release and transfiguration using two details from §2. Then separate the conceptual claim from the historical one, and say what kind of evidence the historical claim would need.

A successful response states Nietzsche's contrast accurately, identifies where he marks his own confidence, and distinguishes an interpretation you can assess from the text from an assertion that needs outside support.
