Why Question the Value of Our Values?

Before you read

When you call someone a good person, what are you praising? Perhaps they keep their promises, put other people first, tell the truth, or refuse to retaliate when injured. Choose one quality whose value seems fairly clear to you.

Now ask two questions. How did people come to admire that quality? What reasons support admiring it now? You might discover an uncomfortable history without deciding to abandon the value. You might also find that an attractive history does less to justify it than you expected.

This distinction opens the Genealogy. Nietzsche investigates the formation of moral judgments in order to ask what those judgments do to human life. His account will challenge familiar priorities. It will also give us reasons to examine the priorities directing his own inquiry.

The preface presents that undertaking through a portrait of the inquirer, a retrospective account of earlier writing, a dispute with other explanations, and instructions for reading. Read all eight sections. The movement from knowing to valuing matters as much as the best-known declaration in §6.

The main question: What would it take to investigate the value of a moral value, including the standards used to judge it?

Three things to notice

  1. The inquirer is implicated. Why does §1 begin with people who pursue knowledge while remaining strangers to themselves?
  2. Origin and value. In §§3–6, distinguish the conditions under which a judgment arose from the effects and possibilities Nietzsche uses to assess it.
  3. Evidence and reading. What does §7 demand from a history of morality, and why does §8 say that understanding requires more than reading a sentence once?

A way to read the preface

Knowledge can become a way of overlooking oneself

§1 addresses knowers who gather knowledge with the purposefulness of bees gathering honey. Their attention goes toward bringing something back to the hive. Meanwhile, the experiences through which they themselves are living fail to receive the same attention.

The image of the clock makes this failure temporal. Someone absorbed elsewhere hears the strokes too late and tries to reconstruct what happened. Nietzsche makes the attempt uncertain: the knowers can even count their own experience incorrectly. Possessing information does not guarantee an adequate account of the person possessing it.

This opening belongs to the book's argument. An investigator of moral values has attachments, habits, and interests too. Becoming skilled at diagnosing other people may leave those commitments obscure. The problem reaches both Nietzsche and the reader who becomes pleased with Nietzsche's explanations.

Imagine recognizing a wish for admiration in someone else's generosity while regarding your own criticism as entirely disinterested. Our example does not establish your hidden motive. It shows why an explanation can be revealing and still become a shelter from self-examination.

The opening also makes a strong claim about our estrangement from ourselves. We need not turn that into the conclusion that every self-description is worthless. The course will ask what particular observations support an interpretation and what might show that it is mistaken.

A history of the inquiry is part of the inquiry

In §2, Nietzsche presents these essays as the growth of problems already expressed in Human, All Too Human. He emphasizes continuity and maturation. His thoughts belong together like the fruit of a tree, expressing an underlying organization of values and commitments.

Read this as Nietzsche's retrospective account. It tells us how he now understands the connection among his writings. It does not relieve us of comparing what those writings actually say. A declared common root need not make every earlier claim identical to a later one.

The tree image also complicates a picture of philosophy as a collection of detached conclusions. What a thinker affirms and rejects may express the shape of a life. That possibility gives us another question to carry through the book: what kind of valuation makes this particular inquiry important to its author?

§3 then recounts a change in the question. The young Nietzsche looked for a supernatural origin of evil. The later question concerns the conditions under which people invented judgments of good and evil, and the worth those judgments possess. The inquiry moves toward histories of peoples, periods, and social groups.

Notice the evaluative alternatives he supplies. Have these judgments advanced or hindered human flourishing? Do they express impoverishment or abundance, confidence or exhaustion? The language does more than locate an origin. It begins to state the standards through which origins and consequences will matter.

Rival explanations need more than a reversal

§4 names Paul Rée as a stimulus to Nietzsche's published hypotheses about morality. He portrays Rée's book as provoking a nearly comprehensive disagreement. His response, however, is described as offering more probable explanations, with the possibility that one error has sometimes replaced another.

The qualification matters. A rival's explanation can be weak without making Nietzsche's alternative correct. The account that places the origin of a practice elsewhere still needs evidence for that placement. Vividness, psychological ingenuity, and opposition to convention do not by themselves meet that need.

Nietzsche also points back to earlier discussions of custom, justice, asceticism, and punishment. These references announce several lines of investigation that the three essays will develop. They should help orient a first reading rather than become a demand to finish every earlier book before beginning this one.

If you have followed the Human, All Too Human or Daybreak courses, keep their distinctions nearby. A sincere moral belief can have a history; a plausible account of a motive is not yet proof; and changing an explanation can leave the question of how to act unsettled.

Why the history serves a question about worth

§5 states that the ultimate concern was more important than competing theories of origin. Nietzsche wanted to investigate morality's value. Explanations of its formation were among the means serving that inquiry.

He focuses on the elevation of pity, self-denial, and sacrifice in Schopenhauer. What had been presented as supremely valuable becomes, for Nietzsche, a possible route toward the denial of life. The issue is the authority granted to these dispositions and what that authority encourages people to become.

Keep the strength of his opposition visible. He describes the expanding morality of pity as a grave danger to European culture, associating it with exhaustion and nihilism. This is not merely a reminder that a well-intended act can have unintended consequences. It is a far-reaching evaluation of an ideal.

The evaluation also has a burden. What would show that these values diminish rather than strengthen possibilities for living? His claim about earlier philosophers' contempt for pity, and his polemical comparison with Buddhism, require scrutiny. A compressed historical assertion cannot substitute for examining different thinkers, practices, and experiences.

Nor should the target be silently expanded to include everything now called kindness or assistance. As the third essay will show, Nietzsche can distinguish the actual usefulness of an activity from the larger ideal under which it is practiced. We will need both levels to understand his criticism.

A value can be a result and a cause

§6 gives the project its broad formulation: the value of moral values must become a question. Answering it requires knowledge of the conditions in which those values grew and changed.

His list makes morality both an expression of conditions and something that acts upon them. It can appear as a symptom, mask, misunderstanding, remedy, stimulant, restraint, or means of dulling pain. A moral judgment may tell us something about the people who need it while also changing what they can do.

Take the value you chose at the beginning. A demand for constant self-denial might have arisen under conditions of scarcity, obedience, religious hope, or mutual dependence. Those are possibilities for inquiry, not findings about its actual origin. The same demand might now sustain valuable cooperation, enforce an unequal arrangement, or do both for different people.

The example separates three tasks: establish the history, understand the practice's effects, and defend the standard by which those effects count as beneficial or harmful. A genealogy can connect those tasks. It cannot complete all three simply by attaching an unflattering motive to a practice.

Nietzsche's own question about the supposedly good person then becomes severe. What if that person's comfort and security come at the expense of future human possibilities? The preface invites us to consider whether familiar goodness may inhibit the power and splendor Nietzsche values.

This is a challenge rather than a demonstrated reversal of every moral ranking. We must ask what those future possibilities consist in, whose lives bear their costs, and why they should outrank other goods. The critic of inherited valuations does not begin without valuations of his own.

The demand for evidence applies to this book

§7 opposes airy constructions to the difficult record of morality actually lived. Nietzsche asks for historical evidence that can be established, rather than a story assembled from contemporary preferences and an assumed development. He also describes an ongoing search for collaborators and recalls hoping for help from Rée, whose keen and impartial eye he respects despite their disagreement. Polemic and intellectual cooperation can coexist.

That demand supplies a standard for reading the essays themselves. When Nietzsche moves from a word's history to a claim about a whole people, or from a psychological possibility to a reconstruction of prehistoric life, ask what supports the transition. His announced method does not certify every result of its use.

At the same time, the passage does not identify seriousness with permanent solemnity. Nietzsche imagines an eventual lightness earned through sustained investigation. Being able to question a familiar morality without remaining entirely captivated by it might change one's tone as well as one's conclusions.

The order matters. Quick mockery of another person's values would not achieve the demanding historical work he has just described. Nor does an entertaining explanation become better supported because it makes its reader feel liberated.

Reading begins again after the sentence is understood

§8 warns that a well-formed aphorism has not been deciphered simply because its words have been read. Interpretation must unfold what those words do. Nietzsche presents the third essay as an example of that work and ends with the image of rumination.

For this course, that means returning to a claim after seeing what follows it. A sentence about cruelty will acquire different implications when read beside its proposed social conditions, its psychological consequences, and the value Nietzsche assigns to them. The next section may qualify what first looked like the complete answer.

He also makes familiarity with his previous writings part of his expectation, describing the experience of Zarathustra in terms of both injury and enchantment. The combination resists the idea that understanding consists simply in admiring a message. A text may attract us and expose something we would rather leave alone.

You can begin here while keeping that expectation in view. This course supplies connections and reads the entire preface and all three essays in order. Its questions are intended to make your own account more precise, including where you judge Nietzsche to be wrong.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can Nietzsche evaluate morality without shielding his own values from evaluation?

The preface asks us to question judgments whose authority has been taken for granted. Yet it appeals to flourishing, strength, creativity, and a richer human future as reasons for questioning them. Those appeals make the investigation purposeful. They also need explanation and defense.

A genealogy that traces a value to fear has not thereby shown it to be worthless. A practice formed under frightening conditions might still deserve support for reasons that can withstand scrutiny. Equally, a value associated with pride or creative power does not become justified merely because its origin sounds admirable.

The historical, psychological, and evaluative questions must remain in conversation. Ask both whether Nietzsche has accurately explained a practice and whether the explanation supports the judgment he makes of it. Turning his demand back on him is one way to take it seriously.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why begin with knowers who do not know themselves?

The opening makes the inquirer part of the problem. Intense attention to acquiring knowledge can coexist with neglect or misunderstanding of one's own experience. The reader who diagnoses other people's values must also examine the commitments directing that diagnosis.

Does discovering an unattractive origin automatically refute a value?

No. Nietzsche uses histories of formation to pursue a question about worth. Establishing an origin, describing present effects, and defending a standard of evaluation are connected tasks, but a result in one does not automatically settle the others.

Is the preface neutral about the future it wants?

No. Nietzsche values forms of flourishing, strength, and human possibility that he fears prevailing morality may inhibit. The preface opens a challenge to established rankings while giving us reason to scrutinize the rankings guiding its own challenge.

Does the demand for historical evidence establish the accuracy of Nietzsche's histories?

No. It states a methodological requirement. His specific transitions from evidence to explanations still need assessment. A rival theory's weakness and the vividness of his alternative are insufficient to establish that alternative.

What does rumination ask of the reader?

To return to a formulation and interpret it through its connections, development, and difficulties. Recognizing its immediate meaning is a beginning. The essays' changing examples and arguments can transform the account you first gave of it.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one moral value, such as honesty, fairness, compassion, or self-discipline. Write 200–300 words that distinguish four questions:

  1. What action or disposition are you praising, and what do you currently regard as a good reason for praising it?
  2. What historical or psychological explanation of its authority would you want to investigate? Identify evidence you would need before accepting that explanation.
  3. Whom does the value help, what might it make difficult, and how would you assess those effects?
  4. What standard are you using to decide whether it is valuable, and what could lead you to revise that standard?

End with: A history of this value would change my judgment if it showed…

Keep this response. The final lesson will return to it after Nietzsche has made the pursuit of truth itself a subject of the inquiry.

Continue the reading

Reading 2: Who First Called Themselves Good? →

Read Essay I, §§1–7 next. Nietzsche challenges an explanation that derives goodness from useful, unselfish actions and locates the initial act of valuation among those who call themselves good.

Carry this question forward: What difference does it make whether a value begins in the gratitude of its recipients or in the self-affirmation of those who name it? Keep the preface's demand for evidence directed at both accounts.
