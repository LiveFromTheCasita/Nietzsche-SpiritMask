Before you read

The previous lesson distinguished Nietzsche's Jesus from the priestly type. Jesus's death, on that interpretation, displayed a way of living without blame. The present sections ask how people devoted to that example could turn it into a message organized around judgment, guilt, and future recompense.

Follow three levels of explanation: the disciples' response to catastrophe, Paul's organization of its meaning, and the effects Nietzsche attributes to the resulting doctrines. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others. Notice when a psychological possibility becomes a confident historical assertion.

The main question: How can devotion to a teacher transform the meaning of his example, and what does Nietzsche think is lost in that transformation?

Three things to notice

  1. Claimed integrity Sections 36–38 oppose Nietzsche's intellectual conscience to inherited sacred claims. Ask how his asserted neutrality fits his anger, and why he treats contemporaries as more responsible than earlier believers.
  2. The explanation demanded by a death In §§40–41 the disciples move from grief and injury to enemies, judgment, sacrifice, and a unique divine son. Track each transition instead of treating resentment as a complete explanation by itself.
  3. Where value is placed Sections 42–43 connect resurrection and immortality with priestly power and the devaluation of earthly life. Notice that §43 also directly defends rank and attacks equal rights; its target exceeds religious hypocrisy.

A way to read the passages

The reader claims an advantage over the believer

Section 36 presents intellectual integrity as a condition for recognizing Christianity's reversal of its original message. Nietzsche distrusts interpretations organized around what someone needs to believe. He describes the Church as sanctifying practices the original teacher had left behind. The irony depends on the portrait established in §§28–35; without that portrait, a reversal has not yet been shown.

Section 37 expands the claim into a history of increasing literalization and accommodation. On Nietzsche's account, symbolic experience becomes supernatural narrative, while expansion changes the teaching to meet new audiences' demands. Growth is no guarantee of fidelity. But showing that a movement could succeed by changing its original message does not establish Nietzsche's sweeping history.

Section 38 changes the distribution of blame. Nietzsche grants the past a measure of tolerance, then attacks contemporaries who, he insists, have the intellectual means to know better. His charge includes an inconsistency between Christian identification and conduct such as warfare, judging, or defending honor. Its force depends both on his strict definition of Christian practice and on his unproved assumption that modern believers share his conclusions while suppressing them.

A belief can misdescribe the life it accompanies

The provocative claim in §39 that only one Christian existed is a challenge to the criteria of membership. Nietzsche refuses to let assent to salvation through Christ settle what counts as Christianity. The decisive question is whether the person lives the nonresentful practice attributed to Jesus. Public profession can coexist with contrary conduct because a person's explanation of their motives is not necessarily their action's effective cause.

He gives instincts explanatory priority over conscious acceptance of a proposition. Faith can function as a flattering account under which different motives act. This goes beyond occasional failure to follow a rule: the description can conceal a life's dominant orientation. For example, a declared commitment to forgiveness might accompany a continuing demand that opponents suffer. The declaration would not resolve which tendency governs the person's actions.

Yet §39 also says this way of living remains possible in every age. His claim that the original message died with Jesus therefore cannot simply mean that no one could ever live similarly again. It marks a judgment about the historical movement and its defining interpretation. The possibility of practice survives the alleged corruption of its institutional name. Keep that conceptual distinction visible when evaluating his deliberately absolute language.

Grief becomes a demand for a different ending

Section 40 locates the turning point in the disciples' response to the death. Nietzsche imagines bewilderment, humiliation, and the fear that the catastrophe refutes their cause. Devotion cannot tolerate an accidental or meaningless outcome, so the event requires a reason proportionate to the teacher's importance. This is an account of how love and wounded commitment can produce reinterpretation, rather than a claim that the disciples began without attachment to their teacher.

The search for meaning becomes a search for an enemy. Nietzsche attributes to the community a reaction against the ruling Jewish authorities and a retrospective image of Jesus as their militant opponent. That attribution belongs to his conjectural narrative; it does not establish historical responsibility for the execution. Within the argument, the crucial reversal is psychological: followers respond with the resentment that the teacher's conduct had supposedly excluded.

Judgment and recompense then promise to reverse the humiliation. A kingdom experienced now becomes a future event at which enemies will be defeated. Elevating Jesus into a uniquely privileged son also replaces the shared divine sonship described earlier. Devotion has not disappeared; its form has changed. What looks like a higher tribute to the teacher may preserve the followers' demand for vindication by sacrificing the feature that distinguished his example.

The cross acquires a doctrinal function

Section 41 describes sacrifice as a solution to the question of how God could permit the death. If the innocent dies to obtain forgiveness for others, the catastrophe becomes part of an intended saving arrangement. Nietzsche's objection is that this explanation restores precisely the distance of guilt that his Jesus had dissolved. The death no longer demonstrates a present unity; it becomes the transaction needed to repair separation.

Paul enters as the figure who gives the transformation a compelling doctrinal structure. Resurrection becomes necessary to the faith, and blessedness is displaced into existence after death. In §42 Nietzsche interprets this change as serving priestly power: an authority that defines future judgment can organize followers through promises and conditions. Paul's importance in the argument is thus not exhausted by personal villainy. He represents a mode of institution building that converts a life into a system of dependence.

Nietzsche nevertheless makes highly personal allegations, including deliberate falsification and insincerity. The text does not independently demonstrate Paul's private intentions. Nor does calling Paul’s reported vision a hallucination establish a retrospective medical explanation. Separate the institutional question—how a doctrine might authorize its interpreters—from the biographical accusation that its organizer knowingly invented it. One can investigate the first mechanism without granting the second claim, or accepting Nietzsche's compressed account of later religions.

Immortality changes both priorities and rank

Section 43 argues that assigning decisive value to an afterlife drains earthly projects of weight. Cooperation, inherited obligations, and care for a common future become secondary if individual salvation possesses infinite importance. Nietzsche interprets this as an expansion of self-concern under religious language. The claim is about the ordering of values, not simply the factual existence of an afterlife. A critic could ask whether belief in immortality must weaken earthly responsibility or can sometimes intensify it.

His second objection is explicitly aristocratic. Equality of souls, he argues, undermines distinctions of rank and supplies a religious origin for political equal rights. He defends special rights, dominion, and the distance between different kinds of people as conditions of cultural development. Do not soften this into a criticism only of empty promises of equality. The section attacks equality itself because Nietzsche regards hierarchy as valuable.

There is also a tension with his earlier treatment of Jesus's inclusive sonship. Nietzsche can value a practice that refuses enmity without endorsing a political doctrine of equal standing. But the move from criticism of self-importance to defense of unequal rights requires an additional evaluative commitment. Showing that someone seeks cosmic reassurance would not by itself show that the person deserves fewer rights. Section 43 exposes the rank-based standard doing work beneath his genealogy.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does a possible psychological explanation establish the origin or falsity of a belief?

No. Grief might make a consoling account attractive without proving that grief created it; an attractive account might still be true. Nietzsche must distinguish explaining appeal, reconstructing historical formation, and assessing content. Sections 40–42 move across these tasks. Ask what evidence each requires: records of development, grounds for attributing intention, or an independent assessment of the belief.

Even if a follower sincerely believes in future judgment, that orientation can differ from a practice excluding judgment and resentment. But showing a difference does not establish corruption. Nietzsche's conclusion depends on assigning priority to the original type and on his wider valuation of earthly life and hierarchy.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why does §38 distinguish contemporary believers from earlier ones?

Nietzsche thinks modern intellectual resources remove the excuse of ignorance, making continued profession culpable. His accusation depends on the contested claim that believers already know what he takes critical inquiry to have established.

How can §39 say there was only one Christian while allowing Christianity to remain possible?

It separates an exemplary way of living from the historical movement called Christianity. Nietzsche says the movement reversed that example, while the nonresentful practice remains available in principle to later individuals.

What connects the death to future judgment in §40?

In Nietzsche's reconstruction, followers experience the death as injury and apparent refutation. Their demand for meaning and vindication turns enemies into future objects of punishment, replacing present blessedness with anticipated recompense.

Why does the sacrificial interpretation contradict Nietzsche's Jesus in §41?

Sacrifice for forgiveness presupposes guilt and separation requiring repair. Nietzsche's Jesus embodies unity without that distance, so the new interpretation restores the problem the original practice had supposedly removed.

What two objections to immortality appear in §43?

Nietzsche says it devalues earthly cooperation by magnifying individual salvation, and that equal immortal souls undermine rank and special rights. The second objection is an explicit defense of hierarchy, not merely a concern about selfishness.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 250–350 words answering: When does preserving a teacher's authority change the meaning of the teacher's practice?

  1. State a claim about one transition in §§40–42: from example to judgment, from unity to sacrifice, or from present blessedness to resurrection and immortality.
  2. Support it with specific evidence from that transition and connect it to the distinction between acts and professed faith in §39.
  3. Raise the strongest objection: a later doctrine might express loyalty rather than betrayal. Answer by identifying the criterion Nietzsche uses to distinguish faithful development from reversal, and assess whether that criterion is justified.

End by separating your judgment of the conceptual change from any historical claim the available evidence does not establish.
