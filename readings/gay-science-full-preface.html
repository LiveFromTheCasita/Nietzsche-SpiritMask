The Later Preface: Convalescence and Surfaces

Optional full-text route · Station 35 of 35 (proposed) · Last station

Review draft. This lesson belongs to a proposed full-text route that has not been published. All 35 stations are drafted and await editorial review. For the complete published introduction, use the ten-session guided reading path.

Read: the four-line motto on the free edition’s title page, then the Preface to the second edition, §§1–4, to its date line, “Ruta, near Genoa, Autumn, 1886.” Stop before the verse prelude, “Jest, Ruse and Revenge.” The editor’s “Editorial Note” (1910), printed between them, is not Nietzsche’s and is not part of the assignment. Both the motto and the preface belong to the expanded edition of 1887.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a written response. This is an editorial estimate, not a measured reading time. The preface is about 2,400 words in the free edition; §2 is the longest. If you need two sittings, stop after §2.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (The Joyful Wisdom; prose translated by Thomas Common, poetry rendered by Paul V. Cohn and Maude D. Petre): the title-page motto · begin Preface §1 · §2 · §3 · §4.

Prose quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation. The free edition credits Cohn and Petre for its poetry generally, on the title page, but does not identify the individual translator of the title-page motto; this page therefore names no translator for it. Kaufmann’s wording differs, and none of it is quoted here; this draft has not been compared with Kaufmann’s text. Where a word drives the interpretation, this page notes the German, from the Projekt Gutenberg-DE text. The preface’s four sections are numbered separately from the aphorisms and poems: “Preface §2” is not §2 of Book I or poem 2.

Before you read

The free edition prints the preface first, as most editions do. This route reads it last, because it was written last. It is dated autumn 1886, after Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–85) and Beyond Good and Evil (1886), and it looks back on a book first published in 1882.

Keep three voices apart.

  • The 1882 book: the verse prelude and Books I–IV, ending at §342 (stations 1–25).
  • Book V and the songs, added in 1887: a later author extending the book (stations 26–34).
  • The preface: the same later author describing the 1882 book’s author, sometimes in the third person, as a convalescent.

The preface’s portrait of illness and recovery is its own self-portrait. This page reads it as the preface presents it, and does not test it against biographical sources.

The main question: What can the later convalescent’s portrait explain or recast?

Keep using the marks: P, H, V, and ? for Nietzsche’s own hedge only. Add one more for this station: R, where the preface reinterprets the earlier book.

What to notice

  1. Which book it describes. The preface mentions the songs “given along with the book on this occasion,” but it never mentions Book V, and it treats §342’s “Incipit tragœdia” as the book’s conclusion.
  2. Health and the body. Watch the preface turn Book V’s tests (§370’s hunger or superfluity, §382’s great health) on philosophy as a whole.
  3. Truth and surfaces. §4 turns away from the will to “truth at all costs.” Compare Book V’s §344 (station 26) and the 1882 book’s §2 (station 2).

Close reading

The motto

The title page of the free edition prints four lines: “I stay to mine house confined, / Nor graft my wits on alien stock; / And mock at every master mind / That never at itself could mock.” This motto belongs to the 1887 edition. The German text used prints a line beneath it, Ueber meiner Hausthür (over my front door), which the English title page omits. Its first line says only Ich wohne in meinem eigenen Haus, I live in my own house; “confined” is the translator’s. Its second is a colloquial triple negative, Hab Niemandem nie nichts nachgemacht: never copied nothing from nobody. The last two lines keep the German’s sense: the speaker laughed at every master who had not laughed at himself. Compare the self-mockery of “The Poet’s Call” and §383 (stations 33–34). the motto

The 1882 edition had a different motto, from Emerson. The German text used prints it and labels it as the 1882 motto: Dem Dichter und Weisen sind alle Dinge befreundet und geweiht, alle Erlebnisse nützlich, alle Tage heilig, alle Menschen göttlich (to the poet and sage all things are friendly and hallowed, all experiences useful, all days holy, all men divine). The free edition does not print it. It is 1882 paratext for comparison, not part of this assignment, and its source in Emerson’s essays has not been checked here.

Preface §1: the convalescent’s book

§1 opens with a hedge: “Perhaps more than one preface would be necessary for this book” (German vielleicht; mark ?). The book “seems to be written in the language of the thawing-wind”; the German word, Thauwind, is the one §377 used for “the thawing wind blows” (station 33). Winter’s defeat “has perhaps already come” (vielleicht; mark ?), and the book is full of “the gratitude of a convalescent.” “‘Joyful Wisdom’: that implies the Saturnalia of a spirit which has patiently withstood a long, frightful pressure.” Common’s “patiently, strenuously, impassionately” renders geduldig, streng, kalt: patiently, strictly, coldly. Mark it R: the whole 1882 book is recast as “a revel after long privation and impotence.” Preface §1, convalescence

What lay behind it was “desert, exhaustion, unbelief,” pride against pain, “radical isolation,” and a diet of bitterness that came from “imprudent spiritual diet and pampering—it is called Romanticism.” Compare §370’s retraction (station 31). The songs are named: the author asks forgiveness for “the handful of songs which are given along with the book on this occasion,—songs in which a poet makes merry over all poets.” Then comes the ending: “Incipit tragœdia, it is said at the conclusion of this seriously frivolous book.” That is §342, the end of the 1882 book (station 25); in the edition this preface introduces, the book goes on through Book V and the songs. “incipit parodia, there is no doubt.” Preface §1, incipit parodia

Preface §2: health and philosophy

“But let us leave Herr Nietzsche; what does it matter to people that Herr Nietzsche has got well again?” The author names himself in the third person and turns to a general question, “the relations of health to philosophy.” “With the one it is his defects which philosophise, with the other it is his riches and powers.” The sick thinkers, “perhaps” the majority in philosophy’s history (German vielleicht; mark ?), are led toward “the sun, stillness, gentleness, patience, medicine.” So “every philosophy which puts peace higher than war,” every ethic with “a negative grasp of the idea of happiness,” every longing for “a beyond,” permits the question whether sickness inspired it. Mark it P and V. Preface §2, health and philosophy

Note the form of the boldest claim. The author has “often enough asked myself” whether philosophy has been “merely an interpretation of the body, and a misunderstanding of the body.” Behind the highest valuations lie misunderstandings of bodily constitution, “either of individuals, classes, or entire races” (German von Einzelnen, sei es von Ständen oder ganzen Rassen); state the word plainly. And he still expects a “philosophical physician” to dare the judgment that philosophising “has not hitherto been a question of ‘truth’ at all, but of something else,—namely, of health, futurity, growth, power, life.” Common’s “namely” renders sagen wir, let us say. The claim is framed as a question he asks himself, a suspicion, and an expectation of someone else’s courage; mark it ? as well as V. Compare §370’s “inference from the work to its author” (station 31): the preface extends that test from art to all philosophy. Preface §2, the body

Preface §3: pain and the question mark

§3 refuses to be ungrateful to sickness. A philosopher who passes through many states of health passes through as many philosophies: “this art of transfiguration is just philosophy.” “We are not thinking frogs”; thoughts are “born to us out of our pain,” and “we must, motherlike,” give them all we have (German mütterlich; compare §369’s “mother” and §376’s “maternal species,” stations 31–32). “Should we not be almost tempted to ask” whether we could do without sickness (German fast zu fragen versucht; mark ?). “It is great pain only which is the ultimate emancipator of the spirit.” The note on the joke of an X made out of every U is Common’s. Preface §3, transfiguration

“I doubt whether such pain ‘improves’ us; but I know that it deepens us.” Keep the doubt and the knowledge apart. Mark the doubt ? (German Ich zweifle): it is Nietzsche’s own hedge on whether pain improves. Leave the contrasting assertion unmarked: “I know that it deepens us” (aber ich weiss) is stated without hedging. The section offers two ways of meeting pain: pride, “doing like the Indian who, however sorely tortured, revenges himself on his tormentor with his bitter tongue,” or withdrawal “into the oriental nothingness—it is called Nirvana.” The German Indianer means a Native American; state plainly that the image is a stock picture of the tortured captive, used as an example of pride. “Confidence in life is gone: life itself has become a problem.” Yet love of life remains, “only one loves differently. It is the love of a woman of whom one is doubtful.” Compare §339’s life as a veiled woman (station 25). Mark it P and V.

Preface §4: surfaces

The convalescent comes back “new-born, with the skin cast,” with “a second and more dangerous innocence in joy.” He now scorns the pleasures of “our ‘cultured’ classes, our rich and ruling classes,” and wants another art: “an art for artists, only for artists!” What it needs is “cheerfulness” (German Heiterkeit). Then the turn: “we are not likely to be found again in the tracks of those Egyptian youths” who unveil temple statues at night (the note on Schiller’s “The Veiled Image of Sais” is Common’s). “No, we have got disgusted with this bad taste, this will to truth, to ‘truth at all costs,’ this youthful madness in the love of truth.” “We no longer believe that truth remains truth when the veil is withdrawn from it.” Mark it R and V: the will to truth that §344 examined (station 26) is here called a youthful taste the author has outgrown. Preface §4, the will to truth

A little girl asks her mother whether God is everywhere: “I think that is indecent”—“a hint to philosophers!” Then two hedged questions: “Perhaps truth is a woman who has reasons for not showing her reasons? Perhaps her name is Baubo, to speak in Greek?” (German Vielleicht, twice; mark ?). In Greek myth Baubo made the mourning Demeter laugh, in some versions by an obscene gesture; that note is this guide’s. State plainly that truth is figured as a woman who hides, as life was in §339 and as Truth was an “old woman” in §377 and “In the South.” The section ends: “Those Greeks were superficial—from profundity!” and asks, “Are we not precisely in this respect—Greeks? Worshippers of forms, of tones, and of words? And precisely on that account—artists?” In 1888 Nietzsche reused §§3–4, revised, as the epilogue of Nietzsche Contra Wagner; the site’s guide to that book records the changes. Preface §4, Baubo

A difficulty

Does the preface explain the book, or recast it?

The preface tells you how to read what you have read: as a convalescent’s gratitude. But it was written four years later, after Zarathustra and Beyond Good and Evil. It treats §342 as the book’s end, never mentions Book V, and turns Book V’s own tests on philosophy as such.

Three readings are possible.

  • An explanation. The author knows best what the 1882 book came from. The prelude’s jokes, Book IV’s New Year’s wish, and §342’s departure really are a convalescent’s.
  • A recasting. The later author reads the 1882 book through his later concerns: the body, the will to truth, surfaces. By §2’s own suspicion, the preface is also a symptom, and its portrait needs testing like any other.
  • A test for the reader. §370 asked of every work, “Has hunger or superfluity become creative here?” The preface claims superfluity. You have now read the whole book, and can check the claim against it.

Decide which the text best supports. Name one sentence from the preface and one section from the 1882 book.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does this route read the preface last?

Because it was written last, in autumn 1886, and looks back on the book of 1882. Reading it last keeps its retrospective voice distinct from the book it describes.

Which “conclusion” of the book does §1 mention?

“Incipit tragœdia,” the title of §342, the last section of the 1882 book. The preface does not mention Book V.

What does §2 suspect about philosophy so far?

That it may have been an interpretation, and a misunderstanding, of the body; that it was about health, growth, and life rather than “truth.” It is framed as a suspicion and a question.

What does §3 say pain does, and what does it doubt?

It says great pain deepens us and emancipates the spirit; that is asserted without a hedge. It doubts that pain “improves” us, and that doubt is Nietzsche’s own hedge.

Why does §4 turn to surfaces?

Because the author no longer believes that truth stays truth when unveiled. The Greeks, it says, were “superficial—from profundity.”

Writing prompt

This is the route’s last writing task. Write 250–300 words answering:

Choose one claim the preface makes about this book. Test it against one section of the 1882 book and one section of Book V. Does the preface explain them, or recast them?

  • Quote no more than three short phrases.
  • Give the date of each text you cite: 1882, 1887, or autumn 1886.
  • Keep the preface’s claim, your evidence, and your judgment distinct.

A successful response names the three texts exactly, keeps their dates apart, and says what the preface gets right and what it leaves out.

The end of the route

This is the last station. The route has now assigned every part of the book once, in this order:

  • 1882: the verse prelude, poems 1–63 (station 1); Books I–III, §§1–275 (stations 2–19); Book IV’s opening verse and §§276–342 (stations 20–25).
  • 1887: Book V’s Turenne epigraph and §§343–383 (stations 26–33); the fourteen songs (station 34); the title-page motto and Preface §§1–4 (this station).

If you came here early from station 1, to read the preface in your edition’s printed order, return to station 1 and begin the route.
