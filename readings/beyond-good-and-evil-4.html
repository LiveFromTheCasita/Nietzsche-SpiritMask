Before you read

The preceding lesson’s tests of independence turn here toward religious formation. Read §§61–62 together so that the benefits Nietzsche credits to religion remain visible beside his criticism.

Separate three questions in your notes: whether religious claims are true, what religious practices do to people, and whether those effects are desirable. This session concentrates on the latter two. Nietzsche’s judgments of desirable human types are part of the argument and must themselves be examined.

The main question: How can religion cultivate strength and depth while also, in Nietzsche’s account, obstructing human possibilities?

Three things to notice

  1. Experience and distance In §45, notice why intimate acquaintance with religious experience and the ability to interpret it are both required.
  2. Changing objects of sacrifice In §55, trace the move from sacrificing people, to instincts, to the consolations supplied by belief itself.
  3. Who chooses the goal? In §§61–62, distinguish Nietzsche’s praise of religious effects from his claim that philosophers should direct their use.

A way to read the passages

Understanding an experience from near and far: §45

Nietzsche opens Part III by making religious experience a major field for psychology. Its range exceeds what one investigator could easily know. Pascal represents a demanding case: the investigator needs some grasp of the depth and conflict of such a religious conscience, together with sufficient distance to survey and interpret it. Detached classification alone may miss the experience; immersion alone may prevent analysis.

This complicates the assumption that Nietzsche dismisses religion simply because its beliefs are mistaken. He wants to know what human beings have become capable of through religious life, including painful forms of self-examination. The hunting imagery also reminds us that the investigator brings purposes of his own. Explaining religious experience will not place Nietzsche outside the book’s questions about intellectual motives.

Faith as a transformation of value: §§46–47

Section 46 presents early Christian faith as a costly submission of an already developed intellect. Nietzsche contrasts this with a simpler picture of unquestioning belief. The crucified God overturns an established association between divinity, dignity, and worldly power: humiliation acquires religious authority. Nietzsche interprets this reversal through conflict between rulers and the subordinated, giving a polemical psychological account rather than a complete history of Christianity.

Section 47 examines the apparent transformation from sinner into saint. If psychology assumes good and evil are completely opposed kinds of motive, such a change can appear inexplicable or miraculous. Nietzsche proposes questioning that assumption. He also associates religious states with bodily practices while admitting uncertainty about causal direction. His speculative medical language belongs to this argument’s historical setting; the lesson’s point is his demand for better interpretation, not a diagnosis of religious people.

Sacrifice can survive the loss of God: §55

The ladder of religious cruelty describes successive objects of sacrifice. First people are offered to a god; then people sacrifice their strongest impulses; finally they surrender the consoling beliefs that made those sacrifices meaningful. On this account, an uncompromising rejection of God can continue the practice of making oneself suffer for a higher demand, even after the object of worship has disappeared.

The sequence is a provocative genealogy of an attitude, not a demonstration that every unbeliever secretly follows a religion. Its force lies in a question: has the rejection of a belief also changed the need that sustained it? In our example, someone renounces providence yet treats every remaining hope as a temptation that must be punished. The doctrine has changed, while the discipline of self-denial persists.

The opposite ideal: §56

After the ladder reaches the sacrifice of consolation, Nietzsche imagines a different possibility: a person who wants the entire course of existence again, rather than merely enduring it. This connects with the problem of affirmation encountered in Zarathustra. The scope matters. Wanting selected pleasures repeated is easier than willing the whole sequence, including what made those pleasures possible.

Section 56 presents an ideal of radical affirmation; it does not establish a physical theory that events actually recur. Nor does it show how someone could affirm a whole life without minimizing particular losses or injuries. The passage’s position nevertheless matters: the rejection of comforting belief need not end in the rejection of existence. Nietzsche makes room for an affirmative response and leaves its difficulty intact.

Religion’s different uses: §61

Nietzsche next imagines philosophers responsible for humanity’s development using religion as an instrument of formation. Its effects vary. Rulers can gain obedience through shared belief. Contemplative elites can secure distance from political management. Groups seeking advancement can acquire discipline and self-command. People burdened by ordinary service can find consolation, dignity, and a more bearable relation to suffering.

These are real credits in Nietzsche’s account, not concessions he immediately retracts. Yet their arrangement assumes unequal social purposes. Some people receive training for command, others support for remaining in service. His remark that the majority are entitled to exist insofar as they are useful exposes the severity of that assumption. Asking whether religion works as a formative power is therefore inseparable from asking who authorizes its goal and whose flourishing counts.

The damage he sees: §62

Section 62 changes the question from religion as one means among others to religion exercising supreme authority over human goals. Nietzsche credits Christianity and Buddhism with preserving suffering people and acknowledges that exceptional people may benefit from this care. His objection is that a morality organized around suffering can then treat other experiences as illegitimate, turning confidence, earthly beauty, and independent ambition into sources of guilt.

The argument goes beyond a complaint about unnecessary guilt. Nietzsche condemns the preservation of lives he ranks as failures and presents equality before God as an obstacle to human elevation. His praise of care and his hostility toward its universal extension stand together. A reader should ask why protection of vulnerable lives must suppress excellence, whether the alleged conflict has been established, and how Nietzsche determines which possibilities deserve preservation.

A difficulty to keep in view

Would placing religion under a philosopher’s direction solve the problem of domination?

Nietzsche’s proposal replaces a religious authority’s final goal with the philosopher’s judgment about human development. It does not remove the problem of someone shaping other people without their agreement. The philosopher’s supposed responsibility and insight require justification, especially when the people being formed receive unequal opportunities and purposes.

There is also an internal tension. Section 62 acknowledges that care for sufferers preserves exceptional people, whose development is often precarious. That acknowledgment weakens any simple opposition between protecting vulnerability and cultivating excellence. The best question is therefore about institutions and practices that might support both, rather than accepting Nietzsche’s ranking as the only available choice.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why does §45 require more than detached observation?

The investigator needs some understanding of religious experience’s depth, as well as the distance necessary to interpret it.

What uncertainty does §47 preserve?

The direction, and even existence, of causal relations between certain religious states and associated bodily practices.

How can sacrifice continue after belief in God ends?

In §55, the demand to sacrifice turns toward consolation and belief themselves, potentially preserving an attitude of self-directed cruelty.

What does §56 affirm?

The ideal of wanting existence again in its entirety. It offers no demonstration of physical recurrence.

Why must §§61 and 62 be read together?

They credit religion with discipline, consolation, and preservation while criticizing its authority to define human goals and its effects on exceptional possibilities.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 300–400 words assessing Nietzsche’s proposal that religion can serve human development when philosophers direct it.

  1. Use §61 to identify one benefit of religious formation and specify who receives it.
  2. Use §62 to explain one danger Nietzsche attributes to religion exercising supreme authority.
  3. Ask whether his proposed alternative avoids that danger or transfers the authority to a new group. Address the standing of the people whose lives would be shaped.
  4. End with a judgment that preserves one insight from Nietzsche while challenging one assumption on which his proposal depends.

Give both the credited benefit and the objection their strongest form. A general statement that religion is good or harmful will not answer this exercise.
