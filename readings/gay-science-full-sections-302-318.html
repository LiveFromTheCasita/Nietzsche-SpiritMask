Doing, Restraint, and the Wisdom of Pain

Optional full-text route · Station 22 of 35

Read: Book IV, §§302–318, in full, from the original 1882 edition. Begin at §302 and stop before §319.
Allow: About 65–85 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. This is an editorial estimate, not a measured reading time. The seventeen sections total about 3,000 words in the free edition; §§303, 311, 310, and 302 are the longest, and §§312–315 are very short. If you need two sittings, stop after §309.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Thomas Common, The Joyful Wisdom): begin at §302 · §304 · §307 · §311 · §318.

Quotations on this page follow Common’s free translation, including his section titles. Kaufmann’s wording differs, and none of it is quoted here; this lesson has not been compared with Kaufmann’s text. Where a word drives the interpretation, this page notes the German, from the Projekt Gutenberg-DE text.

Before you read

Station 21 ended with “we” as the poets of life. These seventeen sections ask what governs such a life from the inside: which moral systems to accept, how much self-control to exercise, which errors to shed, and how to listen to pain. Several are spoken in a voice set apart by quotation marks, the wanderer’s or a weary “we,” so notice who speaks before deciding what the book claims.

The main question: What disciplines interpretation and restraint, if not the moral commands “Renounce! Overcome thyself!”?

Keep using the marks: P, H, V, and ? for Nietzsche’s own hedge only.

What to notice

  1. Negative virtues. §304 rejects moralities that say “Do not do this!” Find the sections that still recommend holding back.
  2. Speakers. §§309 and 311 are laments in quotation marks, and §311 ends by distancing itself: “we do not think thus when we are brave.”
  3. Storms. §§310, 315, and 318 end the station with waves, storms, and a captain’s command.

  • Happiness and its dangers: §§302, 303.
  • Against negative virtues: §§304, 305, 306.

Happiness and its dangers

§302 describes “the happiness of Homer”: fine senses, a bold soul, readiness “for the worst as for a festival.” It was the condition of him “who invented the Gods for the Greeks,—nay, who invented his Gods for himself!” But such happiness makes one “more liable to suffering than any other creature under the sun!” The section ends with the ancient legend that Homer could not solve “a foolish little riddle which some young fishers proposed to him.” The legend is told in ancient lives of Homer; this guide has not checked which version Nietzsche drew on. §302, Homer’s happiness

§303 sets two “happy ones” side by side. One, a young improviser, seems never to make a mistake, because he can fit “the most accidental tone” into the score. The other’s plans always fail, and he says, “I am under more obligation to thank my failures than any of my successes.” Compare station 20’s §277 on “beloved Chance.”

Against negative virtues

§304, “In Doing we Leave Undone”: “all those moral systems are distasteful to me which say: ‘Do not do this! Renounce! Overcome thyself!’” The speaker prefers moralities that make him do something well, “from morning till evening.” Things that do not belong to such a life fall away “without hatred or antipathy,” “like the yellow leaves.” His motto: “Our doing must determine what we leave undone; in that we do, we leave undone.” He rejects “the negative virtues whose very essence is negation and self-renunciation.” Mark it V; the “I” states a taste. §304, doing and leaving undone

§305 extends the argument to “Self-control.” Teachers who order a man “to get himself into his own power” produce “a curious infirmity.” He stands “armed against himself,” “the eternal watcher of his stronghold.” “Yes, he can be great in that position!” But he is cut off “from the finest accidents of his soul,” and from learning: “we must be able to lose ourselves at times, if we want to learn something of what we have not in ourselves.” Mark it P. §305, self-control

§306, “Stoic and Epicurean,” complicates both. The Epicurean “selects the situations, the persons, and even the events” that suit his sensitive constitution, and “renounces the rest—that is to say, by far the greater part of experience.” The Stoic learns “to swallow stones and vermin, glass-splinters and scorpions.” The section compares him to “the Arabic sect of the Assaua, with which the French became acquainted in Algiers,” a comparison drawn from French colonial contact with North Africa and used as a curiosity; this guide has not checked what Nietzsche knew of the group. The verdict: Stoicism suits those “who live in violent times,” but intellectual workers who expect to spin “a long thread” do better in the Epicurean way. Notice that the Epicurean renounces too. The difficulty below returns to this. §306, Stoic and Epicurean

Criticism and conscience

§307, “In Favour of Criticism,” addresses “thee.” When a former truth now looks like an error, “thou pushest it from thee and imaginest that thy reason has there gained a victory.” But perhaps that error was once as necessary as your present “truths,” like a skin (German vielleicht; mark ?). “Thy new life, and not thy reason, has slain that opinion for thee.” Criticism is “a proof that there are lively, active forces in us, which cast a skin.” Compare station 21’s §296, where the thinker declares himself against his former opinions; here the change is explained by life, not by argument. §307, in favour of criticism

§308 asks whether the habits that make up a day come from “numberless little acts of cowardice and laziness” or from “bravery and inventive reason.” The praise may be the same either way. That is enough for someone who wants only “a good conscience,” but not for “the ‘trier of the reins,’ who hast a consciousness of the conscience!” The phrase echoes Psalm 7:9 (King James Version: the righteous God trieth the hearts and reins); the German is Nierenprüfer. The identification is this guide’s.

§309, “Out of the Seventh Solitude,” gives the wanderer a scene. He shuts a door behind him, stands still, and weeps: “Oh, this inclination and impulse towards the true, the real, the non-apparent, the certain! How I detest it!” Everywhere there are “gardens of Armida” to detain him, the enchantress’s gardens in Tasso’s Jerusalem Delivered, and so he casts “grim glances back at the most beautiful things which could not detain me.” The speaker is the wanderer, not the book’s “I.” Compare §299 (station 21): here the drive to truth is a taskmaster that forbids resting among beautiful appearances. §309, the wanderer’s lament

Will, weariness, and self-portraits

§310, “Will and Wave,” watches the waves rush in and retreat: “Thus live the waves,—thus live we who exercise will!—I do not say more.” Then it speaks to the waves: “You and I are indeed of one race! You and I have indeed one secret!” Mark it P and V. The section claims a kinship and then refuses to explain it.

§311, “Broken Lights,” is a lament in quotation marks, spoken by “people of our stamp” when they are weary: “It is so hard to cause pain to men—oh, that it should be necessary!” It considers living “in the madding crowd,” offers its critics “my defects and mistakes … my contradictions!” to laugh at, and ends: “We are none of us indispensable!” Then the frame: “As we have said, however, we do not think thus when we are brave; we do not think about it at all.” The section presents a mood and then disowns it. Do not read the lament as the book’s settled view. §311, broken lights

§§312–315 are short self-portraits.

  • §312: the speaker names his suffering “dog” and vents his bad humour on it, “as others do with their dogs, servants, and wives.” State it plainly: the comparison sets wives beside servants and dogs as targets of bad humour (German Frauen, women or wives). It attributes the practice to “others,” but uses it as the model for how he treats his pain.
  • §313: “I will take my cue from Raphael, and not paint any more martyr pictures,” for “my ambition would not be gratified in the least if I aspired to be a sublime executioner.” A refusal to find sublimity where it is linked with cruelty.
  • §314: the speaker wants “my lion and my eagle about me,” to show him “the amount of my strength or weakness.”
  • §315: how will it end? In a storm, “just as Oliver Cromwell perished in his storm,” in burning out, or in blowing oneself out.

Suffering and retrospect

§316, “Prophetic Men,” uses a simile. Animals sense the weather because they suffer from the electricity in the air: “it is their sufferings—that are their prophets!” The example is the apes at Gibraltar. Mark it P, offered as a simile rather than as evidence. §317, “Retrospect,” distinguishes ethos from pathos, “to speak and distinguish like the Greeks.” While we live through a period of life we think it “altogether ethos and not pathos,” and only later see that it was passion. The footnote in the free edition, signed P. V. C. (Cohn), glosses the distinction as “that between internal character and external circumstance.” A few notes of music recall “a winter and a house, and a life of utter solitude.” §317, retrospect

§318, “Wisdom in Pain,” ends the station. “In pain there is as much wisdom as in pleasure”: pain preserves the species, and “that it is hurtful is no argument against it.” In pain the speaker hears “the commanding call of the ship’s captain: ‘Take in sail!’” We must “know how to live with reduced energy.” But some men hear the opposite call, and are never “more proud, more martial, or more happy, than when the storm is brewing”: “These are the heroic men, the great pain-bringers of mankind.” The section defends them: they “need just the same apology as pain generally,—and verily, it should not be denied them!” Mark it H and V. The German Schmerzbringer means those who bring pain, not only those who bear it. §318, wisdom in pain

A difficulty

Which restraint does the book allow?

§304 dislikes moralities that say “Renounce! Overcome thyself!,” and §305 finds self-control an infirmity. Yet §306 recommends the Epicurean, who renounces “by far the greater part of experience”; §313 refuses to paint cruelty; and §318 hears in pain the call to “Take in sail!”

Three readings are possible.

  • A by-product, not a command. In §304 things fall away because one is doing something else. §306’s selection and §318’s reduced sail work the same way: they serve a life of work and are not virtues valued for their own sake.
  • Taste, not morality. The restraint the book accepts is prudence or taste, grounded in a person’s constitution (§§306, 313). What it rejects is renunciation presented as a moral law.
  • A tension. The Epicurean’s renunciation looks like exactly the negative virtue §304 rejects, and §318’s praise of the pain-bringers, who never take in sail, undercuts its own advice.

Decide which the text best supports. Name one sentence from §304 or §305 and one from §306, §313, or §318.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What does §304 mean by “in that we do, we leave undone”?

What we leave aside should follow from what we are doing, and fall away without hatred, rather than being the aim of a moral command to renounce.

Why does §305 call self-control an infirmity?

It makes a person permanently defensive against his own impulses, unable to trust any instinct, and cut off from what he could learn only by losing himself at times.

According to §307, what kills an old opinion?

Not a victory of reason but “Thy new life, and not thy reason”: forces in us that need to cast a skin. The old error may once have been as necessary as present truths.

What two responses to pain does §318 describe?

Most people must take in sail and live with reduced energy. A rare few, “the great pain-bringers of mankind,” are proudest when the storm approaches, and the section defends them.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Choose one section from §§304–306 and one from §313 or §318. Does the restraint they allow differ from the renunciation §304 rejects?

  • Quote no more than two short phrases.
  • Say whether each claim is psychological (P), a valuation (V), or both.
  • If you use §311, keep its weary voice distinct from the book’s settled view.

A successful response defines renunciation as §304 uses the word, states what each chosen section recommends, and does not treat a mood or a simile as an argument.
