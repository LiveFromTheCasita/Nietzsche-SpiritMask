Whose Life Are You Living?

Untimely Meditations — Essential Reading Path · Reading 1 of 10

Read: §1 of Schopenhauer as Educator, the third meditation.
Allow: About 35–45 minutes, including the passage, this guide, and a short written response.

Open the reading: Schopenhauer as Educator, §1

The free text uses Adrian Collins’s translation in Thoughts Out of Season, Part II. Read the section headed I immediately beneath Schopenhauer as Educator, stopping at II. Before you read

Think of someone you have admired deeply.

Perhaps it is a writer, a musician, a teacher, or a person whose conduct during a difficult time stayed with you. Try to identify what drew you to them. Was it their recognition and success? Their independence? Their patience with something that other people found tedious? Their willingness to speak honestly when silence would have been easier?

Now ask what that admiration has done. Has it changed anything you practice, attend to, or expect of yourself? Or does admiring the person supply a satisfying feeling that leaves your life much as it was?

Nietzsche’s opening gives these questions an unexpected importance. He wants to know how someone can distinguish their own possibilities from the manners, opinions, and ambitions they have absorbed from others. Yet he turns to other people as a means of finding an answer.

The main question: How can another person help you become yourself without simply giving you another life to imitate?

Read §1 once before continuing. You do not need to know Schopenhauer’s philosophical system to begin. Nietzsche introduces him at the end as the educator whose significance the rest of the essay will explore.

What to notice

  1. The difficulty inside the command to be yourself. Why does Nietzsche suggest that what you currently think and desire may fail to express you?
  2. The bridge that nobody else can build for you. What responsibility does this image place on the individual? Does accepting it require refusing help?
  3. The movement from digging to climbing and cultivation. Nietzsche moves from searching inside ourselves to examining what we have loved, then to images of a ladder and a garden. What changes with each image?

Close reading

A borrowed life can be a busy life

Nietzsche begins with the suggestion that people hide their singularity behind accepted manners and opinions. He considers fear, then emphasizes the desire to avoid the burdens of honesty. What he calls sloth can coexist with considerable activity. A person might work constantly while leaving the question of what deserves that work almost entirely to others. §1, opening discussion

Consider a person who pursues every recognized sign of success but has scarcely considered why those signs matter. Their problem, on this reading, concerns the authority directing their effort. Being busy does not establish that they have taken responsibility for their purposes.

Nietzsche’s language is severe. He describes people who evade their own possibilities as emptied of substance. That rhetoric is meant to disturb the reader, but it does not prove his diagnosis of everyone who lives conventionally. Agreement with others can follow reflection, and disagreement can be borrowed just as easily as agreement.

A useful question to put to yourself is therefore specific: Which of my reasons have I examined, and which become difficult to explain once I stop appealing to what people normally do?

Keep a further limit in view. Nietzsche’s emphasis on avoidance does not establish that financial pressure, dependence, exclusion, or danger are merely excuses. The guide’s question concerns what we can examine within our circumstances; the passage’s sweeping judgments remain open to challenge.

The bridge: accepting help without surrendering responsibility

Nietzsche then imagines a bridge across the river of life that no one else can build for you. Other paths promise to carry you, but at the cost of handing yourself over. §1, the bridge image

Read this beside the section’s eventual turn toward educators. If the point were that other people have nothing to teach us, that ending would be difficult to explain. The bridge instead helps us distinguish receiving assistance from transferring responsibility for the life we lead.

A musician can learn from another musician’s phrasing, discipline, and attention. Copying those qualities may even be part of learning. But choosing what to make of that instruction remains a task for the learner. Admiration alone cannot decide which possibilities to pursue or which judgments to accept.

This is a way to work with Nietzsche’s image. It also leaves a question for the essay: how much authority can an educator possess before guidance becomes a substitute for the learner’s judgment?

What you have loved may reveal a direction

The next turn is easy to miss. Having urged us to become ourselves, Nietzsche questions whether looking ever more deeply inward will reveal an unmistakable self. Peeling away one layer can leave another. Introspection offers no assured stopping point at which the true person finally appears. §1, self-knowledge and admiration

His proposed alternative is to examine what we have truly loved and honored. The important work is comparison. How does one admired person, work, or achievement extend or correct another? What pattern becomes visible across them?

Suppose you admire a novelist’s precision, a teacher’s patience, and a friend’s refusal to simplify an uncomfortable truth. Their activities differ, yet your admiration might disclose a concern for attending carefully before judging. That gives you something to investigate and practice. It is more informative than merely collecting impressive names.

The example is ours; the method comes from Nietzsche’s invitation to study the relationships among what we have valued. Treat the pattern as a possibility to test. A flattering interpretation of your admirations is still an interpretation.

The ladder: a self above the familiar self

Nietzsche writes that “thy true being lies not deeply hidden in thee, but an infinite height above thee.” The objects of admiration form a ladder through which a person approaches themselves. §1, the ladder image

This helps explain why the instruction to become yourself cannot simply mean obeying your current impulses. Your familiar identity may fall short of the possibilities disclosed by what you love. An admired example can make that distance visible. It can call something out of you that is not yet a settled part of your conduct.

But Nietzsche also speaks of an underlying nature that educators reveal and cannot manufacture. Both claims belong to the passage. The self requires development, while that development is presented as releasing something with a nature of its own.

Keep the tension visible. The section does not give us an already finished identity that requires no formation. Nor does it give us unlimited freedom to invent any identity whatsoever. Its images ask how the release of a nature and the work of becoming might belong together.

The garden: what an educator makes possible

The closing account of education uses the language of cultivation. An educator removes impediments and provides conditions in which something can grow: light, warmth, rain, protection from what damages the shoots. Education understood as the attachment of fashionable accomplishments seems superficial by comparison. §1, education and cultivation

Notice that nature is not simply benevolent in this account. Nietzsche presents it as nurturing and destructive. Culture can assist, protect, and complete what nature leaves exposed. The image therefore gives no easy instruction to trust everything merely because it seems natural.

Return to the person you chose before reading. What might they have helped to remove: a fear of asking questions, a habit of rushing to conclusions, an assumption about what someone like you could attempt? What conditions did their example help establish?

These questions give the word educator a concrete meaning. They ask what has become possible through an influence. The rest of the essay will test that possibility through Nietzsche’s account of Schopenhauer.

A difficulty

What if the people we admire are themselves choices made for us by public opinion?

This objection arises from Nietzsche’s own argument. If our opinions can be inherited without examination, the same may be true of our heroes. We might escape one set of approved ambitions only to adopt the tastes and language of a prestigious writer. Admiration could reproduce conformity while giving it the appearance of independence.

The section offers a way to begin answering: compare your admirations, examine their relation to your life, and ask what they help you develop. None of this requires accepting everything an admired person believes. Yet §1 supplies no infallible test that separates a liberating influence from a new dependence.

Try asking about one admired figure: What could I disagree with while preserving what I have learned from this person?

If the answer is nothing, that does not settle the matter. It does give you a reason to examine how much of the authority you have granted them depends on their example, and how much depends on your reluctance to question it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before reading each suggested response.

Why does becoming yourself involve more than doing what you presently want?

Because Nietzsche questions whether familiar desires and opinions already express your possibilities. What you admire may reveal demands that your present habits do not meet. Explain the difference between a current preference and a direction of development.

Why does the bridge image leave room for educators?

Because receiving help and surrendering responsibility are different relations. An educator can disclose possibilities or remove obstacles, while the learner still has to undertake the life in question. Use the bridge and the garden together in your answer.

Does Nietzsche think we discover ourselves or create ourselves?

The passage resists that simple choice. He describes an underlying nature that education releases, while placing the true self above the familiar identity. A careful answer should preserve both the discovery of a possibility and the formation needed to approach it.

Does deep admiration prove that an influence is liberating?

No. Admiration may itself be shaped by accepted opinion. Its intensity does not establish its value. Ask what it enables you to understand, attempt, or judge—and whether it leaves room for disagreement.

Writing prompt

Choose two people or works you have deeply admired. They need not be famous. Write six to eight sentences that do four things:

  1. Identify one capacity each example draws your attention toward.
  2. Explain how the two capacities support or challenge one another.
  3. Describe one practice through which you could develop a capacity, without merely copying the person’s manner.
  4. Name one judgment you would still need to make independently of these examples.

Finish with this sentence:

The part of Nietzsche’s account I most want him to defend is…

A successful response connects admiration with a possible practice and leaves a question open. You do not need to identify your final purpose or produce a complete account of who you are.

We will move from the individual’s borrowed opinions to a society’s shared confidence in its own achievements. Nietzsche asks whether the signs by which a culture congratulates itself establish that anything worthwhile has been achieved.

Carry this question forward: What if the standards by which I judge my life are also the standards my society has failed to examine?
