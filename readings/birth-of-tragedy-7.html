Music, Suffering, and Tragic Renewal

Essential Reading Path · Reading 7 of 8

Read: The final two paragraphs of §16, then §24 in full, in The Birth of Tragedy
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Haussmann): §16 (ending) · §24.

In §16, begin where Nietzsche draws on Schopenhauer to explain music’s power to generate images and myth. Read the final two paragraphs, then §24 in full.

Before you read

A piece of music reaches a tense chord. You want it to move, but you also want to keep listening to exactly what it is doing. Removing every disturbance would leave a different, perhaps much less compelling piece.

That familiar experience gives us a foothold in Nietzsche’s difficult account of tragic pleasure. A tragedy presents suffering and destruction. Why might the experience nevertheless make life feel richer? What does the suffering contribute that a reassuring story could not?

Reading 6 brought us to the limits of theoretical optimism. This session considers what art might offer there. We jump from the end of §16 to §24 to bring Nietzsche’s explanation of music alongside his return to tragic myth.

Between our selections, §17 restates music’s power to generate myth and argues that theoretical optimism must lose its authority before tragedy can return. §18 credits Kant and Schopenhauer with exposing limits to knowledge; it also makes modern theoretical culture depend on a slave class and treats demands for equality as a threat. §19 attacks opera’s subordination of music to intelligible words and its faith in naturally good humanity, then turns toward Bach, Beethoven, and Wagner. §20 asks German readers to expect renewal through tragic music. §§21–22 explain how myth protects the listener from music’s overwhelming force while music draws the listener beyond the visible hero. §23 makes myth a condition of a culture’s unity and imagines a German recovery through exclusion of foreign influences. These are Nietzsche’s connected philosophical and political claims; §24 carries both into its closing hope.

The main question: How can tragedy affirm life while showing an individual life being destroyed?

Read the assigned passages once. Beside each appearance of pleasure or joy, note whose pleasure Nietzsche is describing: the spectator’s, the artist’s, or something he takes to lie beneath the whole world.

What to notice

  1. Music and the image. In §16, does music merely accompany a story, or does Nietzsche give it a more fundamental role in making tragic myth possible?
  2. The spectator’s divided desire. In §24, why do we want both to contemplate the visible drama and to reach beyond it? What work remains for Apollo?
  3. The change of scale. Where does §24 move from an account of artistic experience to a hope about German culture? What evidence would that larger hope require?

Close reading

Music gives the image a deeper significance

The selected ending of §16 begins with a premise borrowed from Schopenhauer: we understand music as the language of the will. Here will means the underlying reality expressed in the world’s striving and changing forms. It reaches beyond a person’s conscious intentions or passing mood.

Nietzsche gives music two related powers. It stimulates the imagination to create symbolic images, and it makes those images appear more significant. The tragic story gives visible form to something music communicates more universally. A hero’s particular conflict becomes an image of a condition that exceeds that one person.

Recall the account of chorus and stage in Reading 3. The visible drama allows a Dionysian experience to appear in figures we can follow. The explanation now gains a stronger metaphysical claim: music brings us into relation with the will underlying those figures. Notice how much rests on accepting that starting point. §16, concluding discussion

What survives the hero’s destruction?

At the end of §16, the individual hero is a manifestation of the will. The hero can be destroyed while the life expressed through that individual continues. Tragic pleasure becomes intelligible, Nietzsche thinks, when the spectator’s attachment reaches beyond the particular figure to that underlying creative life.

This is the structure of metaphysical consolation: the loss of an individual does not exhaust what exists. The assurance concerns the continuing life of the will; it gives no promise that this hero will personally survive or receive compensation. We still see a terrible loss onstage.

Keep consolation distinct from aesthetic justification. Consolation describes an assurance that can sustain us through suffering. Aesthetic justification is the larger claim that existence, including its destruction, can appear justified through art. §24 asks us to consider the world’s creating and destroying as an artistic activity. Feeling comfort and establishing that claim are separate achievements. §16, the hero and the life of the will

Apollo still gives us something to see

§24 opens by returning to the visible drama. The Apollonian image protects the spectator from immediate oneness with Dionysian music. It provides a middle world: characters, gestures, and actions through which musical excitement becomes something we can apprehend.

The image also seems to disclose more than it contains. Nietzsche compares it to a curtain we want to draw aside, even as its clarity holds our attention. We want the scene before us and want to reach through it. The two desires coexist.

This helps us refine the first reading’s question about the cooperation of Apollo and Dionysus. In tragedy, the distinct image makes an encounter with what exceeds individuality possible. Apollo therefore remains essential even when the hero’s destruction draws us beyond the individual. Ask what would be lost if we replaced the whole drama with an abstract announcement that everything passes away. §24, the visible middle world

Dissonance makes pleasure harder to explain

Nietzsche now asks how the ugly and discordant can produce aesthetic pleasure. Pity and admiration for moral courage may accompany tragedy. He thinks those responses still leave its specifically artistic attraction unexplained. Why do we want to experience the work again?

Musical dissonance supplies his central analogy. We can delight in an artistically shaped tension while feeling a longing that presses beyond it. His point concerns pleasure within this troubled movement; he gives no simple rule that a pleasant final chord must cancel everything difficult.

He then takes what he calls a “daring bound” into a metaphysics of art. Tragic myth lets the world’s suffering appear within the will’s creative play. The image of a child building and overthrowing sandhills makes the proposal vivid: construction and destruction belong to one activity.

A chord belongs to a composition. A destroyed individual belongs to the world. Can pleasure in the first teach us to affirm the second? Mark the point where the musical example begins carrying that larger claim. §24, dissonance and aesthetic justification

A theory of tragedy becomes a cultural hope

In §24’s final paragraphs, Nietzsche turns to Germany. He imagines its spirit as a sleeping knight whose Dionysian strength might awaken. The names Brünnhilde and Wotan locate this hope in the mythic world of Wagner’s music dramas.

The awakening also involves slaying dragons and destroying “malignant dwarfs.” Renewal is imagined through the defeat of enemies as well as the recovery of music and myth. §24, the awakening knight

The shift matters. Nietzsche wants renewed music and myth to transform how a culture lives with suffering. He is dissatisfied with an existence governed by concepts and art reduced to pastime. His proposed renewal depends on music recovering a power he associates with Greek tragedy.

These paragraphs give us an impassioned hope, expressed through a national myth. They do not by themselves establish that modern German music recreates Greek tragic experience, or that a whole culture can be diagnosed this way. Keep the closing knight beside the opening dissonance: how did an observation about listening become this prediction? The final reading will confront Nietzsche’s later objection to that very move. §24, the hope of renewal

A difficulty

Does making suffering meaningful to a spectator justify it for the person who suffers?

A musical tension gains significance from its place in a work. Nietzsche asks us to see individual destruction within a larger creative life. The difficulty is that the spectator and the suffering individual occupy different positions. A compelling whole may delight someone who can contemplate it while offering very little to someone enduring the loss.

Give Nietzsche his strongest reply: tragedy can change our relation to existence without promising to repair every individual fate. Then press the question again. Does this explain how an artwork sustains its audience, or show that the world itself is justified? Identify what would have to connect those claims.

Check your understanding

Why does §16 give music more importance than an emotional accompaniment?

Because music is treated as the language of the underlying will. It stimulates symbolic images and deepens their significance, making tragic myth possible. Calling it background mood leaves out the metaphysical premise and its role in generating the visible drama.

What distinguishes metaphysical consolation from aesthetic justification?

Consolation is the assurance that underlying life continues despite an individual’s destruction. Aesthetic justification is the stronger claim that existence can appear justified as an artistic phenomenon. An experience of reassurance does not by itself establish that larger claim.

Why does Apollo remain necessary in §24?

The visible drama gives Dionysian excitement a form the spectator can encounter. Its figures both hold our attention and suggest a reality beyond them. Erasing the image would remove the mediation Nietzsche says makes musical tragedy possible.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

How far does the comparison with musical dissonance help explain our pleasure in tragedy?

Begin with one precise observation about a tense musical passage, real or imagined. Explain Nietzsche’s comparison using §24, then connect it to the hero and the continuing life of the will in §16. Finish by identifying one step that still needs an argument.

A successful response distinguishes an experience of artistic pleasure from a claim about reality. It also explains why the hero’s suffering remains part of the work’s power, rather than something the account simply removes.

Continue

The final session moves fourteen years forward. Nietzsche returns to this book and challenges its borrowed philosophy, its cultural hopes, and its need for consolation. Bring your own judgments to that confrontation.
