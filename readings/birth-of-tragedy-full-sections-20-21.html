From Exhortation to Analysis

Optional full-text route · Station 15 of 19

Read: §20 and §21 of The Birth of Tragedy, both in full.
Allow: About 55–85 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Before you read

§19 ended with Nietzsche’s claim that “we experience a re-birth of tragedy.” §20 turns that claim into an appeal. It begins with Germany’s failure to reach the Greeks and ends by addressing its readers directly: believe, dare, be redeemed. §21 then opens with the words “Gliding back from these hortative tones into the mood which befits the contemplative man.” It gives the book’s clearest account of how Apollo and Dionysus act on each other in tragedy.

The two sections belong together because they show the book in two registers, prophecy and analysis. Ask how each one supports, or fails to support, the other.

The main question: What changes when Nietzsche turns from calling his readers to believe in a rebirth to explaining how Apollo and Dionysus act on each other in a listener?

Read §20 and §21 once. In §20, underline every imperative verb. In §21, mark where Nietzsche says the Apollonian is only an illusion, and where he says it has done real work.

What to notice

  1. The failure of the German classics. Why does Nietzsche think that even Goethe and Schiller could not reach “the core of the Hellenic nature”? What does he expect to succeed instead?
  2. India and Rome. In §21, what two dangers does Nietzsche say the Greeks escaped, and what does tragedy have to do with it?
  3. Tristan without words. What does Nietzsche ask “genuine musicians” to imagine, and what does he think the imagined listener would need to survive it?

Why the classics fell short

§20 opens with a judgment on German culture. The German spirit’s most resolute effort to learn from the Greeks belongs, Nietzsche says, to “the noblest intellectual efforts of Goethe, Schiller, and Winkelmann.” Since them, the effort has “grown feebler and feebler.” Perhaps even they could not penetrate the core of the Greek nature. Meanwhile there is empty talk of “Greek harmony,” “Greek beauty,” “Greek cheerfulness.” The teachers in the higher schools have made their compromise with the Greeks, and the scholar has become at best “a trustworthy corrector of old texts.” §20, the German effort

Nietzsche’s criticism reaches his own profession and his own time. The “journalist,” “the paper slave of the day,” has triumphed over the academic teacher. Never, he says, have “so-called culture and true art” been so opposed: “We understand why so feeble a culture hates true art; it fears destruction thereby.” Goethe and Schiller got no further than Iphigenia’s longing gaze from “barbaric Tauris.” The gate to the Greeks may open by itself, “amidst the mystic tones of reawakened tragic music.” §20, culture against art

Faith in a rebirth

Then comes the demand. “Let no one attempt to weaken our faith in an impending re-birth of Hellenic antiquity; for in it alone we find our hope of a renovation and purification of the German spirit through the fire-magic of music.” §20, faith in rebirth

Compare §19, where Nietzsche said “we experience” a rebirth. Here it is “impending,” an object of “faith” and “hope.” The book moves between a claim of present experience and an appeal to believe. Notice also the phrase “purification of the German spirit,” which §23 will develop.

Dürer’s knight

Against a desolate culture of “dust, sand, torpidness,” Nietzsche sets an image: Dürer’s Knight with Death and the Devil, riding on without hope. “Our Schopenhauer was such a Dürerian knight: he was destitute of all hope, but he sought the truth.” Then the Dionysian magic touches the wilderness. A hurricane sweeps away what is decayed, and tragedy sits amid exuberant life, listening to a song of the Mothers of Being, “Wahn, Wille, Wehe.” §20, the knight and the hurricane

The appeal

The section ends in the second person. Nietzsche asks his friends to “believe with me in Dionysian life and in the re-birth of tragedy,” and declares: “The time of the Socratic man is past.” Crown yourselves with ivy, take the thyrsus. “Dare now to be tragic men, for ye are to be redeemed!” §20, the appeal

Mark the register. There are imperatives, the vocabulary of faith and redemption, and the ritual objects of the Dionysian procession. This is exhortation, not argument. Ask what an appeal like this is for, in a book that has spent nineteen sections explaining.

Gliding back: the Greeks between India and Rome

§21 begins by leaving that tone behind. “Gliding back from these hortative tones,” Nietzsche returns to the Greeks. It was “the people of the tragic mysteries who fight the battles with the Persians,” and such a people needed tragedy “as a necessary healing potion.” Dionysian ecstasy loosens the individual and so weakens political instincts. The “state-forming Apollo” is also the genius of individuation, and the state needs individual personality. §21, tragedy and the state

Nietzsche then offers two extremes:

  • Ecstasy leads a people on “the way to Indian Buddhism,” with its longing for nothingness.
  • The unconditional dominance of political impulses leads to “extremest secularisation,” whose most magnificent and terrible expression is “the Roman imperium.”

“Placed between India and Rome,” the Greeks devised “a third form of life.” Nietzsche credits tragedy, “exciting, purifying, and disburdening the entire life of a people,” with making that balance possible. §21, India and Rome

Treat India, Rome, and Greece here as Nietzsche’s ideal types. His characterizations of Buddhism and of Rome are his own historical and cultural claims, stated without evidence. The site does not endorse them. What they do in the argument is clear. They make tragedy a political achievement, the art that lets a people be both Dionysian and a state.

“The myth protects us from the music”

Now the analysis. Tragedy absorbs the highest musical ecstasy and then places beside it the tragic myth and hero. The hero, “like a mighty Titan,” takes the whole Dionysian world on his shoulders and frees us of it. “The myth protects us from the music, while, on the other hand, it alone gives the highest freedom thereto.” In return, music gives the myth a metaphysical significance that word and image alone could never attain. §21, myth and music

The Tristan thought-experiment

Nietzsche offers an example, and chooses his audience. He will not appeal to those who approach music through scenes, words, and emotions. He names Gervinus among those who never reach even the threshold. He asks only “genuine musicians”: can they imagine hearing “the third act of Tristan und Isolde” as pure music, without word or scene, and not collapse? Such a listener would hear the cries of joy and sorrow from the “vast void of cosmic night.” §21, the thought-experiment

What saves us is Apollo. The Apollonian power bursts forth “with the healing balm of a blissful illusion.” Suddenly we see only Tristan and hear him ask why “the old tune” wakes him. Kurwenal stands between us and the music’s jubilation. The Apollonian fills us “with rapture for individuals.” It makes us believe we are seeing “a detached picture of the world” that music helps us see more clearly. §21, the Apollonian rescue

Who finally speaks whose language?

Nietzsche then reverses the appearance. In truth, he says, “music is the adequate idea of the world,” and drama is only its reflex. The Apollonian illusion is dissolved at the decisive point: in tragedy’s total effect, “the Dionysian gets the upper hand once more.” His formula for the relation is: “Dionysus speaks the language of Apollo; Apollo, however, finally speaks the language of Dionysus; and so the highest goal of tragedy and of art in general is attained.” §21, the fraternal union

The theme page on the Apollonian and Dionysian assigns this section as the book’s central positive claim. It asks whether §21 describes tragedy, or what Nietzsche wants art to be. Keep that question in view as you weigh the word “finally.”

A difficulty

Who is qualified to test the Tristan thought-experiment?

Nietzsche appeals to experience, but only to the experience of “genuine musicians,” for whom music is a mother tongue. Those who reach music through words and scenes are excluded in advance. If someone reports that they can hear the third act as pure music without collapse, the argument has room to say they are not a genuine musician.

Write down what would count as evidence against Nietzsche’s claim. Then ask whether his description of the listener’s divided experience could stand without the restriction on who may testify. The experience is absorption in the individual while being drawn beyond it.

A second difficulty is political. The account of India, Rome, and Greece makes large claims about whole civilizations to support a thesis about art. Which part of §21 depends on those claims, and which part could stand without them?

Retrospect checkpoint: an 1886 comparison

If you are following the chronological alternate, skip this until after the 1886 preface lesson.

  • In self-criticism §3, Nietzsche says the book was written “as ‘music’ for those who are baptised with the name of Music,” “a book for initiates.” §21’s appeal to “genuine musicians” is a clear case. Does the later charge describe the passage fairly?
  • In self-criticism §6, Nietzsche calls contemporary German music “Romanticism through and through and the most un-Grecian of all possible forms of art.” He asks what a truly Dionysian music would be. In 1872 his model for tragic music is Wagner’s Tristan.

Record both. Note that the later judgment is evidence of a change of mind, not a retraction that makes the 1872 argument disappear.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

How does §21’s opening connect tragedy to politics?

Dionysian ecstasy loosens the individual and weakens political instincts; Apollo, as the genius of individuation, is “state-forming.” Nietzsche claims that tragedy let the Greeks avoid two extremes, Indian withdrawal and Roman secularization, and devise “a third form of life.” These are his ideal types and claims, not established history.

What does the Apollonian do in the Tristan thought-experiment?

It rescues the listener from being overwhelmed by pure music. By presenting Tristan, his words, and Kurwenal, it gives us individuals to care about, and the illusion that music serves a picture we are watching. Nietzsche calls this a healing illusion. He then says that in tragedy’s total effect the Dionysian prevails.

How does §20 differ in register from §21?

§20 ends as exhortation: imperatives, faith, redemption, ritual imagery, and a call to “be tragic men.” §21 explicitly turns back into contemplation (“Gliding back from these hortative tones”) and offers an analysis of how myth and music work on a listener. The two sections show the book’s prophecy and its theory side by side.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Does the analysis in §21 support the exhortation in §20, or could you accept one and refuse the other?

  • Quote one phrase from the appeal at the end of §20.
  • Explain the “fraternal union” of §21 using the Tristan example.
  • Then say whether the analysis gives any reason to expect the rebirth §20 demands faith in, and what, if anything, would.

A successful response keeps the two registers apart, states the thought-experiment accurately with its restriction to “genuine musicians,” and reaches a judgment that separates the account of tragic experience from the cultural prophecy.
