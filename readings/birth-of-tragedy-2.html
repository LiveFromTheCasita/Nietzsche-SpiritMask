Before you read

Think of a song that makes you want to remain with a painful experience. It might give loss a shape, make a memory more vivid, or let you hear something in sorrow that ordinary conversation misses. The sorrow remains, yet your relation to it changes.

Keep that possibility in mind as you read §3. Nietzsche approaches Greek beauty through the question of what made it necessary. A world of radiant gods and heroic stories might look like the creation of people who found existence easy. He proposes that their beauty answers an unusually intense awareness of suffering.

Reading 1 introduced Apollo through dreams, clear forms, and the boundaries of the individual. This reading asks what work those beautiful appearances do. The optional §2 develops the artistic powers of nature and Greek festivals; you can follow the present argument without having settled every example there.

The main question: Why would people who understood suffering deeply create gods whose lives made existence seem desirable?

Read §3 once. Notice when Nietzsche changes from describing the gods to asking what kind of need their existence might answer.

What to notice

  1. The gods disappoint a particular expectation. What would a reader looking for holiness, duty, or moral perfection fail to find among the Olympians? What does Nietzsche find there instead?
  2. Silenus reverses the value of being alive. What answer does Midas force from him? Notice the movement from that judgment to the claim that the gods make life desirable.
  3. Grief changes its meaning. Near the end of the discussion of Homeric life, why can a lament over death express attachment to existence? What has changed since Silenus spoke?

§3 — complete section

In order to comprehend this, we must take down the artistic structure of the Apollonian culture, as it were, stone by stone, till we behold the foundations on which it rests. Here we observe first of all the glorious Olympian figures of the gods, standing on the gables of this structure, whose deeds, represented in far-shining reliefs, adorn its friezes. Though Apollo stands among them as an individual deity, side by side with others, and without claim to priority of rank, we must not suffer this fact to mislead us. The same impulse which embodied itself in Apollo has, in general, given birth to this whole Olympian world, and in this sense we may regard Apollo as the father thereof. What was the enormous need from which proceeded such an illustrious group of Olympian beings?

Return to commentary: §3, opening question

Whosoever, with another religion in his heart, approaches these Olympians and seeks among them for moral elevation, even for sanctity, for incorporeal spiritualisation, for sympathetic looks of love, will soon be obliged to turn his back on them, discouraged and disappointed. Here nothing suggests asceticism, spirituality, or duty: here only an exuberant, even triumphant life speaks to us, in which everything existing is deified, whether good or bad. And so the spectator will perhaps stand quite bewildered before this fantastic exuberance of life, and ask himself what magic potion these madly merry men could have used for enjoying life, so that, wherever they turned their eyes, Helena, the ideal image of their own existence "floating in sweet sensuality," smiled upon them. But to this spectator, already turning backwards, we must call out: "depart not hence, but hear rather what Greek folk-wisdom says of this same life, which with such inexplicable cheerfulness spreads out before thee." There is an ancient story that king Midas hunted in the forest a long time for the wise Silenus, the companion of Dionysus, without capturing him. When at last he fell into his hands, the king asked what was best of all and most desirable for man. Fixed and immovable, the demon remained silent; till at last, forced by the king, he broke out with shrill laughter into these words: "Oh, wretched race of a day, children of chance and misery, why do ye compel me to say to you what it were most expedient for you not to hear? What is best of all is for ever beyond your reach: not to be born, not to be, to be nothing. The second best for you, however, is soon to die."

Return to commentary: §3, the Olympians and Silenus

How is the Olympian world of deities related to this folk-wisdom? Even as the rapturous vision of the tortured martyr to his sufferings.

Now the Olympian magic mountain opens, as it were, to our view and shows to us its roots. The Greek knew and felt the terrors and horrors of existence: to be able to live at all, he had to interpose the shining dream-birth of the Olympian world between himself and them. The excessive distrust of the titanic powers of nature, the Moira throning inexorably over all knowledge, the vulture of the great philanthropist Prometheus, the terrible fate of the wise Œdipus, the family curse of the Atridæ which drove Orestes to matricide; in short, that entire philosophy of the sylvan god, with its mythical exemplars, which wrought the ruin of the melancholy Etruscans—was again and again surmounted anew by the Greeks through the artistic middle world of the Olympians, or at least veiled and withdrawn from sight. To be able to live, the Greeks had, from direst necessity, to create these gods: which process we may perhaps picture to ourselves in this manner: that out of the original Titan thearchy of terror the Olympian thearchy of joy was evolved, by slow transitions, through the Apollonian impulse to beauty, even as roses break forth from thorny bushes. How else could this so sensitive people, so vehement in its desires, so singularly qualified for sufferings have endured existence, if it had not been exhibited to them in their gods, surrounded with a higher glory? The same impulse which calls art into being, as the complement and consummation of existence, seducing to a continuation of life, caused also the Olympian world to arise, in which the Hellenic "will" held up before itself a transfiguring mirror. Thus do the gods justify the life of man, in that they themselves live it—the only satisfactory Theodicy! Existence under the bright sunshine of such gods is regarded as that which is desirable in itself, and the real grief of the Homeric men has reference to parting from it, especially to early parting: so that we might now say of them, with a reversion of the Silenian wisdom, that "to die early is worst of all for them, the second worst is—some day to die at all." If once the lamentation is heard, it will ring out again, of the short-lived Achilles, of the leaf-like change and vicissitude of the human race, of the decay of the heroic age. It is not unworthy of the greatest hero to long for a continuation of life, ay, even as a day-labourer. So vehemently does the "will," at the Apollonian stage of development, long for this existence, so completely at one does the Homeric man feel himself with it, that the very lamentation becomes its song of praise.

Return to commentary: §3, why the Greeks needed their gods · §3, the reversal and the lament

Here we must observe that this harmony which is so eagerly contemplated by modern man, in fact, this oneness of man with nature, to express which Schiller introduced the technical term "naïve," is by no means such a simple, naturally resulting and, as it were, inevitable condition, which must be found at the gate of every culture leading to a paradise of man: this could be believed only by an age which sought to picture to itself Rousseau's Émile also as an artist, and imagined it had found in Homer such an artist Émile, reared at Nature's bosom. Wherever we meet with the "naïve" in art, it behoves us to recognise the highest effect of the Apollonian culture, which in the first place has always to overthrow some Titanic empire and slay monsters, and which, through powerful dazzling representations and pleasurable illusions, must have triumphed over a terrible depth of world-contemplation and a most keen susceptibility to suffering. But how seldom is the naïve—that complete absorption, in the beauty of appearance—attained! And hence how inexpressibly sublime is Homer, who, as unit being, bears the same relation to this Apollonian folk-culture as the unit dream-artist does to the dream-faculty of the people and of Nature in general. The Homeric "naïveté" can be comprehended only as the complete triumph of the Apollonian illusion: it is the same kind of illusion as Nature so frequently employs to compass her ends. The true goal is veiled by a phantasm: we stretch out our hands for the latter, while Nature attains the former through our illusion. In the Greeks the "will" desired to contemplate itself in the transfiguration of the genius and the world of art; in order to glorify themselves, its creatures had to feel themselves worthy of glory; they had to behold themselves again in a higher sphere, without this consummate world of contemplation acting as an imperative or reproach. Such is the sphere of beauty, in which, as in a mirror, they saw their images, the Olympians. With this mirroring of beauty the Hellenic will combated its talent—correlative to the artistic—for suffering and for the wisdom of suffering: and, as a monument of its victory, Homer, the naïve artist, stands before us.

Close reading

Begin beneath the beautiful surface

Nietzsche opens by imagining Apollonian culture as a building to be dismantled stone by stone. The shining gods stand at its summit. His question concerns the foundations: what enormous need could have produced them? This changes the task of interpretation. Describing a culture’s beautiful creations is only the beginning; we also have to ask what those creations made possible for its people. §3, opening question

Apollo appears as one god among others, but the Apollonian names a power that Nietzsche thinks produced the whole divine world. Keep the distinction clear. The argument concerns the shaping of experience through images, not Apollo’s place in a divine family tree.

The gods make human life shine

A reader who brings expectations of holiness and self-denial will misunderstand these gods, Nietzsche says. Their world displays desire, rivalry, pleasure, and power in an intensified form. It glorifies life, including aspects that a moralizing religion might condemn. §3, the Olympians and Silenus

What does this give their human admirers? Nietzsche’s answer is recognition raised to a higher brightness. Humans encounter their own mode of existence living among gods. The divine world makes that existence seem worthy of desire. This is different from saying that every divine deed supplies a model of good conduct. The argument concerns the desirability of living, including its passions, rather than a list of actions to imitate.

For a moment, return to the song. You might value the way it makes an experience available to you while judging some of the actions it describes harshly. That distinction helps you approach Nietzsche’s question about artistic glorification. It does not yet establish his account of a whole culture.

Silenus gives the beauty something to answer

Midas wants to know what is best for a human being. He captures Silenus, the companion of Dionysus, and forces him to answer. Silenus replies that the best possibility is never to have existed, with an early death the next best. Notice the cost of Midas’s demand for wisdom: the answer makes the life of the questioner appear undesirable.

This story blocks an easy inference from Greek artistic beauty to uncomplicated happiness. Nietzsche places the harsh judgment inside the culture whose apparent cheerfulness puzzled his spectator. The Greeks’ creation of beautiful gods becomes a response to an awareness they already possess.

In the long paragraph continuing on §3, why the Greeks needed their gods, the Olympian world stands between human beings and the terrors of existence. The examples include overpowering nature, inescapable fate, and the suffering of mythic figures. You need not master every myth on a first reading. Follow the proposed relation: an experience of vulnerability gives rise to an artistic world through which life can be endured and desired.

How a lament can become praise

The decisive change appears when Nietzsche reverses Silenus’s judgment. For the Homeric person he describes, the terrible thing is now having to leave life. The brevity of a hero’s life, the passing of generations, and the decay of the heroic age can provoke lament precisely because existence has acquired such value. Suffering persists, but its presence no longer settles the question of whether life is worth living. §3, the reversal and the lament

Ask what supports the reversal. Nietzsche does not present an argument showing that every misfortune eventually produces a compensating good. The gods offer an image in which human existence is glorified. That image changes the force of the question. We can now grieve because we want more life, rather than conclude from grief that life should never have begun.

Apparent innocence can be an achievement

The closing discussion calls Homer “naïve” in an artistic sense associated with Schiller. The word here describes absorption in beautiful appearance. It is easy to mistake such harmony for humanity’s original, effortless condition. Nietzsche presents it as a difficult cultural achievement over a threatening depth. §3, Homeric naïveté

His account ends with a transfiguring mirror: in the gods, human beings see their lives reflected as worthy of glory. That lets us specify the work of Apollonian beauty more carefully. It selects, shapes, and heightens existence until people can become attached to it. The next lesson asks how tragedy can bring a more direct encounter with destruction into this artistic relationship to life.

A difficulty

What is the difference between making life bearable and hiding something we need to face?

Nietzsche’s own verbs keep this difficulty visible. The Olympian world can overcome terror, but it can also veil it or withdraw it from sight. A beautiful image might give someone the strength to remain engaged with a difficult life; it might also protect an arrangement that deserves to change.

Choose one example of each possibility. Then return to the text: what would let you distinguish them? The song analogy can help formulate the question, but Nietzsche is making a much larger claim about the needs and development of Greek culture. Ask what historical evidence would support that claim beyond the myths and images he selects.

You can find the proposed function of beauty persuasive while leaving its historical explanation open to challenge. Keep both judgments available.

Check your understanding

Does Nietzsche think Greek beauty proves that the Greeks knew little suffering?

He argues in the opposite direction: their sensitivity to suffering helps explain their need for beautiful gods. Support that answer by placing Silenus’s judgment beside the paragraph about the Olympian world standing between humans and terror.

How do the gods justify human life in this section?

They live an intensified and glorified version of it. Human beings can recognize their own existence in a higher sphere and find it desirable. This differs from being instructed to imitate every action attributed to a god.

Why can grief over death count as an affirmation of life?

Someone can mourn having to leave existence because it has become something they want to continue. The grief remains real; its meaning changes when the object of desire is life itself. Explain how that differs from Silenus’s preference for never having existed.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

How does the Olympian world change the meaning of suffering without making suffering disappear?

Begin with Silenus’s judgment. Explain the work Nietzsche assigns to the gods, then use the example of lament to show what has changed. Finish with one difficulty about the relation between beauty and truth.

A successful response will identify a change in someone’s attachment to life, use two specific details from §3, and distinguish that change from the claim that life has become painless. Keep your final objection precise enough that a defender of Nietzsche could try to answer it.

Continue

The next reading asks how music and a transformed chorus can give rise to the visible world of a play. Carry forward the question of what art helps its audience face.

In §4, Nietzsche argues that Apollo’s beautiful forms both answer suffering and impose boundaries that Dionysian experience breaks open. §§5–6 use lyric poetry and folk song to show how the two powers can work together: the poet loses the ordinary self in a musical experience, then gives that experience images and words. On his account, the poem’s speaking ‘I’ is already such an image; this prepares the next step, in which a singing chorus generates the visible figures of tragedy.
