How Is Greatness Cultivated—and Judged?

Twilight of the Idols — Complete Reading Course · Reading 13 of 14

Before you read

Keep the previous lesson’s question about unequal standing open. The figures admired here are not offered as average examples of a flourishing society. Nietzsche asks what makes exceptional achievement possible and what conventional judgments fail to recognize.

Distinguish an explanation of a person’s development from an appraisal of that person’s actions. The text repeatedly crosses between these levels. Note each crossing before deciding whether it is justified.

The main question: Can we recognize exceptional strength and achievement without making their possessors exempt from criticism?

Three things to notice

  1. Accumulation and expenditure Skirmishes §§44 and 47 describe exceptional qualities as results of work accumulated across generations. Section 44 then portrays genius as expenditure rather than prudent self-preservation. Track the tension between conserving conditions and consuming their result.
  2. Social judgment can help make its object In Skirmishes §45, condemnation does not merely name a preexisting criminal nature. Secrecy, exclusion, and shame help shape the person condemned. Ask how far this account explains criminality and where Nietzsche generalizes beyond his evidence.
  3. Goethe is a model and a problem Skirmishes §49 presents Goethe as an achieved integration of powerful capacities. Section 50 asks why a culture that shares some of his aspirations can still produce fragmentation. An exemplary individual does not automatically supply a reproducible social recipe.

A way to read the passages

Genius spends an inheritance: Skirmishes §44

Nietzsche rejects the picture of a great individual as a simple product of the immediate environment. Genius represents an accumulation extending further back than the age in which it appears. His explosive imagery makes the present occasion a trigger rather than a complete cause. Napoleon supplies his example: Nietzsche treats him as the beneficiary of older, stronger conditions than revolutionary France itself. This is not a claim that history is irrelevant. It is a dispute over which history carries explanatory weight.

The account is historical and physiological in Nietzsche’s terms, but its inherited reserves should not be mistaken for an established scientific mechanism. It also makes greatness costly. Exceptional achievement spends what has accumulated, potentially leaving exhaustion rather than a steadily improving culture. Nietzsche rejects the moralized description of this expenditure as self-sacrifice for a noble cause: the drive overflows rather than calculating its own renunciation. That interpretation separates greatness from saintliness, but it cannot by itself evaluate the consequences of a great person’s actions. Explaining the force of an eruption does not tell us what it has built or destroyed.

The criminal as strength under hostile conditions: Skirmishes §45

Section 45 asks whether traits condemned in one environment might function differently in another. Nietzsche’s criminal type is a strong person whose dispositions become unhealthy under restrictive social conditions. Concealment, fear, and shame enter the person’s relation to those dispositions. The person then begins to experience as dangerous or disgraceful capacities that might have been honored elsewhere. The claim is partly about institutions helping produce the damaged character they subsequently condemn.

Dostoevsky matters as a psychological witness. Nietzsche values the writer’s encounter with Siberian convicts because it challenged expectations about the human material found among condemned people. This is Nietzsche’s use of Dostoevsky, not evidence that the two writers share a moral conclusion. He extends the pattern to artists, explorers, and other pioneers, then imagines a reversal in which priests become discredited outsiders. His caste language recalls the earlier chapter on moral improvement and makes the reversal sharper: he challenges who receives degradation without consistently rejecting degradation itself. The account neither establishes that all criminals possess exceptional strength nor removes responsibility for injuries. It directs attention to development and exclusion before turning a legal category into a complete explanation of a person.

When devotion compromises the seeker: Skirmishes §46

The brief §46 refuses simple inferences from outward conduct to inner character. Silence might express nobility; contradiction might arise from love; a falsehood might result from courtesy. Nietzsche’s point is not that each behavior actually has this elevated source. It is that apparently obvious moral signs can admit different psychological explanations. He then imagines a seeker whose love of knowledge may cost something ordinarily regarded as essential to humanity.

This places pressure on the ambition admired throughout the book. If a pursuit consumes the qualities that once made it admirable, when should its intensity cease to count in its favor? The paragraph offers analogies rather than a criterion for deciding. Its comparison with a loving woman depends on a gendered code of honor, while its closing religious image uses Jewish identity within a demeaning hierarchy. Those assumptions belong to the rhetoric’s operation, rather than being neutral illustrations. Explain the uncertainty Nietzsche introduces about motives, then ask what would justify the sacrifice he evokes. Intensity of devotion is not already an answer.

Culture begins in trained bodies: Skirmishes §47

Nietzsche locates cultivated beauty in sustained practice rather than accident or inward good intentions. Gesture, bearing, diet, company, and bodily discipline form the person. The distinction between the one who acquires a quality and the one who inherits it makes apparently effortless achievement the visible end of effort that may have occurred elsewhere or earlier. This also illuminates artistic training: fluency can conceal the repetitions that made it possible.

But §47 ranges beyond a modest claim about acquired skill. It speaks about families and peoples, regulated sexuality, selection, and transmission over generations. Nietzsche’s account combines culture and physiology in ways the paragraph does not substantiate through evidence. Its preference for aristocratic bearing and selected company also determines which bodies count as beautiful. Readers should distinguish the strong observation that thinking is embodied from the much stronger assumption that a preferred social style measures human flourishing. His polemic against Christian contempt for the body concludes the paragraph; it is a sweeping assessment of a tradition, not a demonstration that every Christian practice disregards bodily cultivation.

A return to nature that refuses equality: Skirmishes §48

Nietzsche’s return to nature points upward toward greater capacity, not backward to an innocent original condition. Napoleon again embodies his preferred movement: cultivated force capable of taking on formidable tasks. Rousseau supplies the opposing image, combining ideals of nature and dignity with a psychology Nietzsche describes through hostile personal characterization. Keep the reconstruction modest. This paragraph gives us Nietzsche’s polemical Rousseau; it does not adequately establish Rousseau’s own position.

The argument against equality must nevertheless be faced directly. Nietzsche says justice should distinguish equals from unequals, and he treats revolutionary equality as a concealment of genuine differences in rank. His principal objection here is not the Revolution’s bloodshed; he explicitly gives greater weight to the morality it advances. A reply therefore needs to distinguish equal moral or political standing from identical abilities, achievements, or rewards. Recognition of unequal accomplishment need not imply unequal protection against domination. Nietzsche would challenge the value and consequences of that equality too, but the distinction exposes an argument still needed: why should difference in excellence decide who is entitled to basic standing?

Goethe’s freedom is achieved integration: Skirmishes §49

Goethe becomes Nietzsche’s example of a person who works upon the contradictory tendencies of his own age. History, natural science, antiquity, philosophy, and practical activity become materials for formation. The achievement is neither withdrawal from life nor indiscriminate accumulation. Goethe creates limits and horizons within which different capacities can be organized. Reason, feeling, will, and sensuality become parts of a working whole rather than rival jurisdictions.

This helps explain Nietzsche’s distinction between tolerance through strength and tolerance through exhaustion. A person may welcome difference because it enlarges an already organized life, or cease to judge because judgment has become too difficult. The difference cannot be inferred merely from someone’s permissive behavior. Goethe’s culminating attitude affirms existence as a whole while allowing particular things to be bad. Nietzsche calls this Dionysian. It is neither the claim that every event is pleasant nor the rule that every harmful act should be approved. Yet its relation to judgment remains difficult: how can comprehensive affirmation preserve effective resistance to particular wrongs? The final lesson will make that tension central.

An exception is not a cultural solution: Skirmishes §§50–51

Section 50 grants that the nineteenth century sought some qualities Nietzsche admires in Goethe: breadth, realism, and receptivity. Why, then, does he diagnose fragmentation rather than another integrated person? Similar ingredients need not produce the same organization. The question resists turning §49 into a list of habits guaranteed to generate greatness. Nietzsche also rejects immediate public usefulness as the measure of an exceptional individual. That prevents a reductive assessment of art, but it leaves unresolved how a culture should assess the resources and costs devoted to exceptional achievement.

Section 51 moves from Goethe to Nietzsche’s own literary ambition. He seeks writing that endures rather than immediate national approval, and he treats the concentrated sentence and aphorism as forms capable of such endurance. This returns us to the book’s compression: shortness is meant to store thought, not excuse a lack of it. His extraordinary self-assessment and praise of Zarathustra are claims the reader must evaluate, not credentials that settle the issue. The section closes by promising another independent book. Its forward look also raises the course’s question: can admiration for intellectual power coexist with sustained scrutiny of what that power asks us to value?

A difficulty to keep in view

Does explaining an outsider sympathetically amount to excusing everything the outsider does?

No. Section 45 asks how a person becomes what a social category says the person already is. Understanding secrecy, exclusion, and shame can change an explanation of conduct without making every action defensible. Conversely, acknowledging harm does not require believing that a legal label exhausts a person’s character.

Nietzsche complicates this distinction by repeatedly valuing exceptional force and using Napoleon as a positive example. A responsible reading should neither remove that admiration nor accept it as a complete assessment. Ask separately what capacities exist, how conditions shape them, what actions follow, and whose lives those actions affect. This preserves the psychological challenge while refusing the unsupported inference that unusual strength automatically carries its own justification. The same discipline should govern the reading of Nietzsche himself.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Does §44 claim that genius has no historical causes?

No. Nietzsche disputes explanation by the immediate environment and attributes genius to a longer accumulation of forces. The age supplies an occasion for discharge rather than the whole source of the capacity.

What work does Dostoevsky do in §45?

He supplies a valued psychological witness whose experience of convicts challenged conventional expectations. Nietzsche draws on that testimony for his account of strong dispositions under hostile conditions; he does not thereby establish agreement with Dostoevsky’s ethics.

What connects the difficulties in §§46–47?

Neither elevated motives nor cultivated appearance transparently guarantees value. Section 46 complicates judgments of conduct and devotion; §47 explains embodied formation but adds hierarchical standards and inherited claims that require separate scrutiny.

Why is an answer about revolutionary violence insufficient as a response to §48?

Nietzsche explicitly centers his objection on the Revolution’s egalitarian morality. A response must address his account of justice and rank, distinguishing unequal capacities from the separate question of equal standing.

What prevents Goethe from functioning as a simple formula for success?

Section 49 presents a singular integration of capacities. Section 50 asks why an age with related aspirations still becomes fragmented, while §51 extends the problem of enduring achievement to Nietzsche’s own compressed writing.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 350–500 words on whether Nietzsche’s admiration for exceptional people supplies an adequate standard of value. Use Skirmishes §45 or §48, and §§49–50.

  1. Explain one achievement Nietzsche values: transformed dispositions, integrated capacities, or the power to undertake exceptional tasks. Give the relevant section reference.
  2. Show how the chosen achievement affects his judgment of criminals or equality. Distinguish the actual claim from a more agreeable version you might prefer.
  3. Present the strongest objection that considers another person’s standing or the consequences of exceptional action. Then formulate a serious Nietzschean reply.
  4. End by explaining what you would need to know before applying this standard to a person you admire. Include one condition under which your admiration would change.

An effective essay can recognize an achievement while contesting the authority Nietzsche grants it. Aim for a judgment precise enough to survive both admiration and disappointment.
