Why Isn’t Rebellion Enough?

Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Essential Reading Path · Reading 2 of 10

Read: Prologue, §§6–10, stopping at the end of §10 before Zarathustra’s Discourses. Then read Part I, “The Three Metamorphoses” in full, stopping before “The Academic Chairs of Virtue.”
Allow: About 75–100 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann in The Portable Nietzsche. Free alternative: Prologue §§6–10 · The Three Metamorphoses (Thomas Common, Thus Spake Zarathustra).

Chapter names follow Common’s edition; see the edition guide for alternate titles. Its Roman numerals I–LXXX number chapters across the book, while the assignment above specifies parts and internal sections. If highlighting fails, locate the stated chapter and section. Before you read

In Reading 1, Zarathustra presented a future beyond comfortable conformity. The crowd asked for the comfort he intended to make contemptible. His attempt to teach failed, and he began to question his distance from his listeners.

Now the performance they came to watch becomes an emergency. Follow what Zarathustra does before returning to what he teaches. A living person will test the images of crossing, danger, and going under that filled his speech.

The next discourse introduces the camel, lion, and child. It is tempting to turn them immediately into three instructions for independence. First let the completed Prologue make independence difficult: Zarathustra needs other people, changes his plans, and speaks in ways the surrounding story can unsettle.

The main question: What must someone become capable of, beyond rejecting an inherited command, if they want to create values?

Read Prologue §§6–10, then Part I, “The Three Metamorphoses.” Keep the narrator’s observations distinct from Zarathustra’s interpretations of himself.

Three things to notice

  1. The crossing becomes an event. What causes the performer to fall? How does Zarathustra respond to his account of his life?
  2. A revised audience, an unresolved danger. Why does Zarathustra seek living companions, and what remains troubling about where he expects them to go?
  3. Three different powers. What can the camel bear, the lion refuse, and the child begin? Notice why the power of refusal cannot yet create values.

A way to read the passages

The performer falls when someone leaps over him

Prologue §6 interrupts the speeches with the tightrope walker’s fall. A buffoon follows him, insults him, and leaps over him. The performer loses his composure and footing. The event is caused by a rival’s intervention, not simply by the inherent difficulty of crossing. Prologue §6

That distinction matters beside Zarathustra’s language of overcoming. A leap beyond someone can be an act of cruelty. The fallen performer should not be turned automatically into an illustration proving the teacher’s account of human development.

While the crowd scatters, Zarathustra remains and kneels beside him. The injured man fears that the devil will take him to hell. Zarathustra denies both devil and hell, and denies a soul surviving the body.

This removes one terror but creates another difficulty. The performer interprets the denial as meaning that his life was scarcely more than an animal’s training through hunger and blows. Zarathustra rejects that contempt: making danger his calling gave the life something worthy of respect.

He promises to bury the man himself. The narrator describes the dying man moving his hand as though seeking Zarathustra’s in gratitude. A possible gesture replaces the crowd’s noisy misunderstanding. Yet neither that gesture nor the death establishes that the performer became the overman.

The teacher now carries a burden

By evening the spectators have gone. Zarathustra stays beside the dead man, then describes his day’s catch bitterly: he has gained a corpse. His intended meaning has failed to reach people, and he sees himself suspended between the roles of fool and dead man.

He still wants to teach the overman as the meaning of human existence. His failure has not yet changed that aim. It has forced the giver of abundant wisdom into an unanticipated task: carrying someone who cannot receive or answer a teaching.

Remember the saint’s advice in Prologue §2 about taking up other people’s burdens. The narrative now gives that advice a literal counterpart. Zarathustra’s care exceeds the public speech, even while he interprets the encounter through disappointment at his own undertaking.

The night includes mockery, hunger, and assistance

In §8, the buffoon reappears and warns Zarathustra to leave. He claims that established believers and the good and just hate the teacher. These are the buffoon’s reports, not judgments independently confirmed by the narrator. He threatens to leap over Zarathustra next. Prologue §8

Gravediggers mock the burden he carries. Zarathustra continues without replying, until his own hunger interrupts the journey. Having forgotten food while attending to the day’s events, he must ask someone else for sustenance.

At an isolated house another old man gives him bread and wine. This host is not identified as the saint from §2. He comically insists that the dead companion should eat too: his rule of hospitality outruns his understanding of the visitor’s circumstances.

The scene makes dependence concrete. The teacher who arrived with gifts now receives ordinary help. After continuing through the night, he places the corpse in a hollow tree to protect it from wolves and sleeps nearby. Preserve that detail: the burial he later declares accomplished is this sheltering of the body, not a separately narrated grave.

Living companions must be able to answer

On waking in §9, Zarathustra announces a discovery to his own heart. He needs living companions rather than bodies he can carry wherever he chooses. He resolves to seek fellow creators rather than a mass audience, a herd, or obedient believers. Prologue §9

The distinction transforms his idea of reception. A companion should follow because they want to follow themselves. Receiving the teaching would involve a capacity to create, not merely acceptance of the teacher’s authority.

But listen to the additional condition: they should go where he wants to go. The difficulty has not disappeared. What happens if a companion’s own path leads elsewhere? His harvest images emphasize his need for collaborators, while the crop is already ripe according to his judgment.

He parts from the dead man and renews his resolve. His closing promise to leap over those who delay him also echoes the buffoon’s leap. The echo does not prove that the two figures are identical. It makes the teacher’s language answerable to the injury we have just witnessed.

The animals accompany an unfinished teacher

At noon in §10, an eagle appears with a serpent around its neck. The narrator explicitly presents the serpent as a friend, not prey. Zarathustra calls them his animals and associates the eagle with pride and the serpent with wisdom. Prologue §10

He wants them to guide him through dangers among human beings. Remembering the saint’s words, he wishes he possessed greater wisdom. His final request is stranger than a promise of enlightenment: if wisdom deserts him, may pride accompany even his folly.

Confidence and acknowledged vulnerability remain together. The narrator closes by repeating that Zarathustra’s going down began thus. The Prologue has prepared an undertaking still to be tested, rather than certified that its teacher has completed his own transformation.

The camel wants a difficult burden

Part I opens with Zarathustra describing three metamorphoses of the spirit. The camel represents a strong, reverent capacity to bear demanding things. It kneels because it wants a load through which its strength can prove itself. Part I, “The Three Metamorphoses”

Its examples are more demanding than passive compliance. They include humiliating pride, displaying one’s folly, leaving a cause when it triumphs, and enduring the disappointment or unpleasantness that a search for truth can bring.

The camel can therefore carry disciplines that challenge its own satisfactions. Someone who learns to test a cherished argument may endure a burden their vanity would prefer to avoid. This is our illustration of the capacity, not a claim that any particular student occupies a fixed spiritual stage.

Yet strength still understands itself through the question of what it can take upon itself. Reverence gives the burden authority. The strongest bearer has not thereby acquired the right to decide whether the commanding standard should continue to govern.

The lion must win freedom from something it loved

In the wilderness the camel becomes a lion. Its opponent is the dragon of inherited commands. On the dragon’s scales glitter the values of centuries; it claims that all values have already been created and that no independent willing may remain.

The conflict therefore concerns authority over possible value. The dragon does not merely prohibit one activity. It denies that the spirit may begin anything outside the completed order it represents.

Common names the opposing voices “Thou shalt” and “I will.” The lion must claim permission to create even when that claim feels like an offence against what it formerly revered.

This is why the struggle takes courage. The spirit must find illusion and arbitrariness within things it once loved as holy. Freedom requires separation from its own attachment, not simply contempt for an authority it never respected.

Imagine leaving a profession whose standards once supplied your whole measure of achievement. Rejecting an imposed demand is only part of the difficulty. You may still want its approval. The lion’s struggle helps name that inward obstacle.

Refusal opens a possibility it cannot fill

Zarathustra expressly denies that the lion can create new values. It can win freedom for creation. Its refusal is a necessary power within this image, but opposition alone cannot determine what deserves a new commitment.

A person who organizes every choice around doing the opposite of an old teacher may still let the teacher set the agenda. The rejected command continues to supply the question and the terms of the answer.

The lion’s achievement should nevertheless retain its force. Zarathustra permits refusal even toward duty and holiness. This is more than negotiating a lighter workload inside the same unquestioned order. He imagines the authority of the order itself becoming something the spirit can challenge.

The child’s affirmation is a further demand

The child brings innocence, forgetting, beginning, and play. The image of a wheel moving from itself suggests an activity no longer defined solely by the push of the former command or the effort of resisting it.

Forgetting here need not mean erasing every memory or skill. Within the sequence, it helps picture release from a relationship that keeps the past in command. Play introduces an affirmative involvement: the spirit begins to make and inhabit a world of its own.

The child is consequently not an instruction to recover literal childhood or follow every passing impulse. The image comes after the capacities for bearing and refusing. Zarathustra gives it the difficult task of a yes to life that can create.

The speech does not explain how this transformation occurs or guarantee that every life follows its sequence. Read the animals as distinctions among powers required for the undertaking. They are not a demonstrated developmental law or a personality test.

Pause here: Name something the camel can already do that the child would need, and something the lion cannot accomplish merely by saying no.

A difficulty to keep in view

What makes a newly created value worth following?

Escaping an inherited command does not by itself justify what replaces it. A new purpose can be shallow, destructive, or dependent on making other people into instruments. Calling it creative does not answer those objections.

The Prologue prevents this question from becoming merely abstract. The buffoon performs a spectacular leap that destroys another person’s life. Zarathustra later uses a similar image for his advance. The story gives us reason to scrutinize the costs concealed by attractive language about going beyond.

The child’s yes supplies an orientation toward affirmative creation, but not a complete test for distinguishing worthwhile creations from cruel ones. Nor does the proposed companionship resolve whose judgment should govern when creators disagree.

Zarathustra’s own response to the performer offers another consideration: an individual life deserves attention even when it does not deliver the future the teacher hoped to announce. Whether that care can remain joined to his demand for overcoming is an active question.

Take the sequence seriously without treating it as a certificate of superiority. Someone can admire the child’s freedom while remaining attached to the applause given to rebellious lions. Our response to these images belongs within the inquiry.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why should the performer’s fall not be treated as a simple illustration of progress?

A rival’s taunts and leap cause his loss of balance. Overpassing someone here destroys a life. Zarathustra’s later language of leaping must be read beside that event rather than automatically accepted as admirable overcoming.

What changes in Zarathustra’s idea of an audience?

He seeks living fellow creators rather than a crowd or obedient followers. Yet he wants them to follow themselves toward his destination. That unresolved condition keeps his approach to companionship open to examination.

Why is the camel more than a picture of mindless obedience?

It actively seeks demanding burdens, including disciplines that wound pride and require difficult truthfulness. Its limitation is that bearing a command does not yet give it authority to challenge the command’s standing.

What can the lion accomplish, and what can it not yet do?

It can refuse inherited authority and win freedom for creation, including freedom from what it once revered. Zarathustra explicitly says that it cannot yet create new values. Opposition does not supply an affirmative purpose by itself.

Does the child establish a guaranteed final stage of maturity?

No. It is an image of beginning, play, and affirmative creation within Zarathustra’s speech. The text supplies neither a universal developmental law nor proof that the speaker has achieved the condition he describes.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to the aspiration and objection you recorded in Reading 1. Write 200–300 words:

  1. Explain how one event from Prologue §§6–10 changes your assessment of Zarathustra as a teacher.
  2. Identify an inherited demand connected with your chosen aspiration.
  3. Distinguish bearing that demand, winning freedom from it, and forming an affirmative purpose.
  4. Say what could justify the new purpose beyond its difference from the old one.
  5. Use the performer or the companions to identify another person whose claims your account must consider.

A successful response uses the three images without assigning yourself or someone else a permanent spiritual rank.
