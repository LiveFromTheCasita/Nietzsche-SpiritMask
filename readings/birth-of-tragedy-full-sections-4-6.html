From the Naïve Artist to the Lyric "I"

Optional full-text route · Station 6 of 19

Read: §§4–6 of The Birth of Tragedy, in full.

Before you read

Reading 2 ended with Homer as the "naïve" artist: an achievement built over a knowledge of suffering. §4 begins by returning to "this naïve artist" and asks what dreaming can teach us about him. §5 then asks where the seed of tragedy first appears among the Greeks. Nietzsche's answer is not Homer but Archilochus, the lyric poet, whose poems cry out his love and hatred in the first person.

That creates a problem Nietzsche takes seriously. If art requires release from the ego, as the aesthetics of his time held, then a poet who keeps saying "I" looks like the opposite of an artist. How could the Greeks have honoured him beside Homer?

The main question: How can a poet who loses his ordinary self in music still produce images and words, and what does that "I" imply for the claim that the world is justified as art?

Read the three sections once. In §5, mark every "I": ask each time who is speaking.

What to notice

  1. The dreamer who wants to keep dreaming (§4). Why would anyone value appearance above waking life? Notice where Nietzsche calls his answer an "assumption."
  2. Archilochus's "I" (§5). Whose passions do his poems express? Follow the image of the sleeping reveller touched by Apollo's laurel.
  3. Melody before words (§6). Why does one tune serve many stanzas? What does that suggest about which comes first?

Close reading

Why appearance might matter more than waking life

§4 starts from a dreamer who says to himself, in the middle of the dream, "it is a dream, I will dream on." Ordinarily we rank waking life above dreaming. Nietzsche proposes the reverse. The more he perceives nature's longing for "redemption through appearance," the more he feels driven to what he calls "the metaphysical assumption" that the Primordial Unity, eternally suffering and self-contradictory, "requires the rapturous vision, the joyful appearance, for its continuous salvation." §4, the dreamer and the metaphysical assumption

On this assumption, our everyday world is already appearance, and the dream is "an appearance of appearance," a still higher satisfaction of nature's desire for appearance. Nietzsche illustrates it with Raphael's Transfiguration. The lower half shows the suffering of the possessed boy and the despair around him; the upper half, a radiant vision rising out of it. Beauty and the wisdom of Silenus appear together, and each needs the other. §4, Raphael's Transfiguration

Use your E and R marks. The dreamer's wish to keep dreaming is an E: an experience you can recognize. That a suffering world-ground needs appearance for its salvation is an R, and Nietzsche himself calls it an assumption. Much of the book's metaphysics enters through this sentence.

Apollo's measure, and what it cannot keep out

Apollo now appears as the god of individuation: the individual who sits calmly in his boat on the sea, an image you met in Reading 1. As an ethical god he demands measure: "know thyself" and "not too much." Excess counts as "titanic" and "barbaric." Nietzsche reads Prometheus and Oedipus as the Apollonian interpretation of the past, heroes destroyed for excessive love and excessive wisdom. You will meet both again in §9 (Reading 4). §4, Apollo's measure

But the Apollonian Greek cannot keep his distance. He recognizes that his beauty and moderation rest on a hidden ground of suffering and knowledge, which the Dionysian discloses to him again. "Apollo could not live without Dionysus!" When the Dionysian festival sounds into this measured world, the wisdom of Silenus cries out against the cheerful Olympians, and the individual forgets the Apollonian precepts. §4, the Dionysian breaks in

A history in four periods

The section ends with a history of Greek art as a struggle between the two drives. An age of Titans gives way to the Homeric world of beauty. That world is overwhelmed by the Dionysian, and the Apollonian answers with the austerity of Doric art. Nietzsche explains "the Doric state and Doric art as a permanent war-camp of the Apollonian," with "a constitution so cruel and relentless." Attic tragedy then appears as the goal of both drives, their child, "at once Antigone and Cassandra." §4, the four periods

Two features deserve attention. First, the history is a construction: four periods, each explained by the drives, arranged so that tragedy becomes the goal. That arrangement is Nietzsche's interpretation, not a record he reports. Second, he uses the aesthetic drives to explain a state and its harshness. The book will do that again on a larger scale in its last sections.

Homer, Archilochus, and the problem of the lyric

§5 sets Homer, "the aged dreamer," beside Archilochus, whose poems pour out hatred, scorn, and desire. Modern aesthetics calls one the first objective artist and the other the first subjective one. Nietzsche rejects the pairing. If art requires the conquest of the subjective, a merely subjective poet would not be an artist at all. Yet the Greeks honoured Archilochus, and the Delphic oracle itself revered him. §5, Homer and Archilochus

Nietzsche's first clue is Schiller's report that a musical mood came before his poetic ideas. His second is that ancient lyric poets were also musicians. §5, Schiller's musical mood

Whose "I"?

Nietzsche's answer uses the two drives. As a Dionysian artist, the lyric poet first becomes one with the Primordial Unity, its pain and contradiction, and produces its copy as music. Then, under Apollonian dream-inspiration, the music becomes visible to him again as an image. His conclusion: "The 'I' of the lyrist sounds therefore from the abyss of being." When Archilochus declares his love and contempt, we are not watching his private passion. We are watching a Dionysian reveller asleep, whom Apollo touches with the laurel, and whose enchantment gives off images as poems. §5, the lyric "I"

On this account, the everyday, desiring Archilochus "can never at any time be a poet." His passions appear in the poem as images the world-genius uses. Compare the sleeping reveller of §2: the figure Nietzsche pictured there in general terms now has a name. §5, the empirical person and the genius

Parting from Schopenhauer, in his name

Nietzsche then turns to Schopenhauer, who also found the lyric poet a difficulty. Schopenhauer "thought he had found a way out of it," Nietzsche writes, "on which, however, I cannot accompany him." The long quotation that follows describes song as a mixture of personal desire and will-less contemplation. Nietzsche objects that this makes lyric poetry a "semi-art," and he rejects the subjective/objective opposition by which Schopenhauer still ranks the arts. §5, Schopenhauer on song

Yet Nietzsche also says that Schopenhauer alone, through his metaphysics of music, held the means to remove the difficulty, and that he has removed it "in his spirit and to his honour." Mark this passage carefully. Nietzsche departs from Schopenhauer's account of lyric while relying on Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music to replace it. §5, the objection to Schopenhauer

The formula's first appearance

The section's conclusion is deliberately humbling. Nietzsche says that "the entire comedy of art" is not performed for our improvement, and we are not its authors. Its true author "uses us as pictures and artistic projections," and our highest dignity lies in our significance as works of art, "for only as an æsthetic phenomenon is existence and the world eternally justified." Our awareness of that significance, he adds, is about as great as that of "the soldiers painted on canvas" of the battle they appear in. Only when the genius merges with this world-artist does he become "at once subject and object, at once poet, actor, and spectator." §5, the aesthetic justification

This is the first statement of the book's most famous claim. Notice where it arrives. It comes at the end of an argument about the lyric "I," and it is stated from the standpoint of a world-artist, not from ours. The claim's force depends on that change of standpoint. The book returns to the claim near its close, and the theme page on tragedy and aesthetic justification compares the occurrences.

Melody first

§6 extends the argument to folk song, which Archilochus is credited with bringing into literature. Nietzsche calls folk song the "perpetuum vestigium," the lasting trace, of a union of the two drives. The claim that follows is his: "Melody is therefore primary and universal." It generates poems, which is why a single tune can carry many stanzas. §6, folk song and melody

He draws on the differences between the diction of Homer and that of Pindar, and on listeners who reach for images to describe a Beethoven symphony. Titles such as "pastoral," he argues, are symbolic images born out of the music, not objects the music imitates. §6, Beethoven and figurative speech

The section ends with a careful distinction. When the lyric poet interprets music in images, music appears as will, as passion and longing. Yet "music, according to its essence, cannot be will," because the will is what is unaesthetic in itself. Language can therefore "only be in superficial contact with music." It can imitate music, but never disclose music's innermost essence. §6, music and will

Keep this distinction for later. At station 13, §16 will speak of music in stronger Schopenhauerian terms. You will want to know whether Nietzsche is keeping or dropping the careful line he draws here.

Retrospect checkpoint: an 1886 comparison

If you are following the chronological alternate, skip this until after the 1886 preface lesson.

In 1886 Nietzsche wrote that the book's proposition "recurs time and again": the existence of the world is justified only as an aesthetic phenomenon (self-criticism §5). He also regretted having expressed his new valuations in Schopenhauerian formulas (§6). Test both claims against what you have just read. §5 of the book does state the formula, from the standpoint of a world-artist. It also explicitly refuses Schopenhauer's account of lyric poetry, while claiming to solve the problem "in his spirit." Record both facts in your ledger. The evidence cuts both ways.

A difficulty

For whom is existence justified?

At §5, the justification arrives through the claim that we are pictures made by a world-artist, as unaware of our meaning as painted soldiers are of their battle. If that is right, the justification is available from the artist's standpoint, not ours. Our dignity lies in being works of art, not in experiencing the justification.

Is this a justification of suffering, or a relocation of the standpoint from which suffering would need to be justified? Put the question beside Reading 2's lament: there, a Homeric figure's grief showed attachment to life from within a life. Put it beside the question Reading 7 will ask about the spectator and the one who suffers.

A second difficulty concerns the kinds of claim in these sections. Some are aesthetic: the lyric "I" is not the empirical person; melody generates poems. Some are historical: Archilochus brought folk song into literature, "established by critical research"; Greek art falls into four periods; the Doric state was an Apollonian war-camp. Some are metaphysical: the Primordial Unity needs appearance. Each needs different evidence. The theme page on the Apollonian and Dionysian frames the same distinction for §§5–7.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does Nietzsche reject the division between "objective" and "subjective" artists?

Because a merely subjective, desiring individual cannot be an artist at all. In his account, art begins only when the individual will has been left behind. The lyric poet who says "I" has already been released from the ordinary self; his "I" is an image of the world-genius speaking through him.

What does Nietzsche mean by "an appearance of appearance"?

If the everyday world is already appearance, a representation of the Primordial Unity, then a dream or a naïve work of art is appearance at a second remove. Nietzsche claims it is a higher satisfaction of nature's longing for appearance. He calls the premise a metaphysical assumption.

Does §6 say that music is will?

Not in its essence. §6 says music appears as will when the lyric poet interprets it in images of passion and longing. It denies that music can be will according to its essence, since the will is unaesthetic in itself. Language can only touch the surface of music.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

Who is speaking when Archilochus says "I," and what does Nietzsche's answer do to the claim that existence is justified as art?

  • Begin with the problem of the lyric poet in §5.
  • Explain Nietzsche's two-step answer, music and then image, using one detail from the text, such as the sleeping reveller or the laurel.
  • Then show how the same reasoning leads to the formula about aesthetic justification, and say from whose standpoint the justification is stated.

Finish with one question you would put to Nietzsche about that standpoint.

A successful response distinguishes the empirical person from the lyric genius, identifies which step is metaphysical, and connects the lyric argument to the formula without treating the formula as a slogan.
