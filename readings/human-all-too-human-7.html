Can Understanding Others Make Us Kinder?

Human, All Too Human — Essential Reading Path · Reading 7 of 10

Read: Volume I, §293, then §376 (1878). Read each in full, stopping before §294 and §377 respectively.
Allow: About 45–60 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternatives: I §293 · I §376 (Helen Zimmern).

I means Volume I; AOM means Assorted Opinions and Maxims (called Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions in the free edition); WS means The Wanderer and His Shadow. Numbers restart in each collection. The numbered 1886 prefaces are separate texts. If highlighting fails, use the collection and numbered heading.

Before you read

A friend tells a story about a success. You suspect that its details have grown in the telling because your friend wants reassurance. You could point out the exaggeration, ask about the insecurity beneath it, or simply let the story pass. Seeing a possible motive does not yet decide what to do.

I §293 asks whether social life requires acting as though we have not understood another person's motives. I §376 examines the misunderstandings, silences, and differences within close friendships.

The question in this reading's title is deliberately open. Understanding may soften resentment. It may also make us complacent about our supposed insight, or give us more effective ways of controlling people. Nietzsche's passages offer a possibility of kindness while leaving uncomfortable questions about concealment.

Reading 6 asked the free thinker to examine their own intellectual habits. That demand becomes personal here. Someone who sees through everyone else but overlooks their own shifting motives has missed an essential movement in I §376.

The main question: Can insight into the conditions of a person's character make us more forbearing without making us dishonest or indifferent to harm?

Three things to notice

  1. Seeing and showing that we see. I §293 concerns how we behave toward someone whose motives we believe we understand. Notice the qualification that the dissimulation is well meant.
  2. Difference within apparent agreement. At the start of I §376, even the same opinion has a different place and force in different people. Agreement in words does not guarantee agreement in experience.
  3. The turn toward oneself. Follow the movement from disappointment in friends through the necessity of opinions to self-knowledge. How does acknowledging one's own limitations alter the relation to others?

A way to read the passages

Not every insight must become an announcement

I §293 consists of a single observation: dealing with people often requires a kindly concealment of our understanding of their motives. Nietzsche distinguishes an insight from its social display. We may understand something and choose not to make the other person confront our understanding. I §293

Return to the friend telling a success story. Announcing that they crave admiration might add nothing useful. It could turn an ordinary moment of pleasure into humiliation, while letting you enjoy your own cleverness. There may be room to respond warmly without endorsing every embellished detail.

The aphorism goes beyond a general recommendation to be polite. It allows a discrepancy between what one understands and what one lets the other person see one understands. That discrepancy is deliberate. We should not erase it by paraphrasing the passage as an uncomplicated celebration of honesty.

Yet the word often also matters. Nietzsche does not say that motives must always remain unspoken. If an exaggeration affects someone's decision, reputation, or access to something important, the reason for speaking changes. The short passage supplies no rule for resolving all such cases.

There is another preliminary question: do we understand the motive? What looks like vanity could be relief, nervousness, or a clumsy attempt to share good news. This uncertainty is a caution we should bring to the observation, which assumes that we have seen through the person.

The same opinion can mean different things

I §376 begins by asking us to consider the differences among close acquaintances. Feelings diverge, opinions divide, and even apparently shared opinions differ in their aspect and strength. The intimacy of a relationship does not remove these variations. I §376, the opening reflection

Two friends might agree that people should be independent. For one, independence means escaping a controlling household. For the other, it means never asking for help, a rule learned in a family that regarded need as shameful. Their agreement may conceal a serious difference about whether accepting assistance is compatible with freedom.

Nietzsche's point reaches beyond people misunderstanding a word. An opinion occupies a place among attachments, fears, habits, and other opinions. It may be central to one person's self-respect and peripheral to another's. The same objection can therefore feel like an interesting suggestion to one friend and an attack to the other.

In the example, discovering those histories would help explain a quarrel. It would not decide which account of independence is better. As in the previous reading, explaining the conditions of a belief and assessing its reasons remain distinct tasks.

Friendship now seems fragile: people cannot simply transfer the full significance of their thoughts into each other's lives.

Why the history of an opinion might soften resentment

Nietzsche then asks us to recognize opinions as necessary and beyond ultimate responsibility, just as actions are. Their nature and force arise from an interweaving of character, occupation, talent, and environment. This is stronger than the familiar advice that everyone has a different perspective.

Recall the question of blame in Reading 3. The claim here is that someone has not ultimately made themselves into the person for whom this opinion has this particular force. Their way of judging belongs to a network of conditions. Nietzsche extends the challenge to ultimate moral desert into the inner life of belief.

If a friend's disagreement feels like a freely chosen rejection of you, recognizing its conditions may reduce its sting. The disagreement could express a history and a pattern of attachments that were not created for the purpose of wounding you. Understanding can interrupt an immediate passage from difference to personal accusation.

The aphorism says this recognition may remove bitterness. It does not supply a demonstration that everyone who accepts necessity will become kinder. Someone could use the same explanation to feel superior or to give up trying to understand. The movement toward forbearance requires something more than a causal vocabulary.

Nor does necessity mean that opinions cannot change. A conversation, an experience, or a correction can enter the conditions shaping them. What Nietzsche challenges is the picture of an opinion detached from such conditions and simply chosen by an entirely self-created individual.

Friendship can depend on error and silence

The next movement is harder to welcome. Nietzsche imagines admitting that friends were attracted by mistaken impressions of one's character, and that remaining friends required learning to leave some things unsaid. Friendship can persist partly because certain judgments never become topics of conversation.

This is not merely a claim that nobody knows everything about another person. Nietzsche attributes a constructive role to error and concealment. An impression that helped a friendship begin may have been misleading; knowledge that later develops may need to remain unspoken for the relationship to continue.

Suppose you admire a friend's generosity while also noticing their eagerness to be publicly thanked. The generosity can be real even when its motives are mixed. A friendship might survive partly because neither person demands an explicit inventory of everything the other finds vain, irritating, or disappointing.

But change the example. Suppose one person conceals a pattern of exploiting the other's resources. Now silence protects an arrangement the other might reasonably refuse. The fact that disclosure could end a friendship would not, by itself, justify concealment. Preserving a relationship and treating its participants well are different aims.

Nietzsche's sweeping suggestion that intimate knowledge would deeply injure almost anyone needs scrutiny. Some candid conversations deepen trust. His image captures the vulnerability of affection to judgment; it does not establish that every friendship depends on avoiding every difficult truth.

Self-knowledge changes the observer's position

The passage turns inward. We learn to see ourselves as changing collections of opinions and moods. The person evaluating a friend's vanity is also changeable, inconsistent, and vulnerable to an unflattering interpretation. Psychological insight must include its user.

Nietzsche describes the resulting correction in abrasive terms: learning to despise ourselves a little restores balance with other people. He does not ask us to discover that everyone is secretly admirable. We retain reasons for adverse judgments, but cease to reserve them exclusively for others.

Imagine resenting a friend for changing plans when you remember making comparable changes yourself. Your own choice had reasons that felt urgent from inside your life. Recognizing that the friend also acts from within a life can temper the assumption that their inconvenience to you exhausts its meaning.

This comparison need not establish that the cases are equally serious. It changes the judge's starting position: one fallible person is considering another.

The passage connects bearing with others to the tolerance we already extend toward ourselves. Its route to kindness passes through diminished self-importance. This is a more difficult proposal than telling someone to overlook faults because friendship is pleasant.

The changed voice at the end

I §376 begins with the possibility of declaring that real friends do not exist. Its closing voice imagines a happier moment in which the category of the enemy also loses its force. The final speaker presents himself playfully as a fool, rather than announcing a finished system of social ethics.

Exposure first threatens friendship, then self-knowledge makes reciprocal endurance conceivable. Nietzsche asks whether our relation to the discoveries about difference, error, and silence can change.

Reading the conclusion as a command to deny real conflict would flatten it. People can still oppose our purposes or cause harm. The more modest possibility is that understanding loosens the bitterness with which we make another person wholly identical with their opposition to us.

Read I §293 again after this ending. A decision to keep an insight to oneself can now come from recognition of shared limitations. It can also still come from condescension. I §376 gives us a way to question the observer's posture, without making every instance of silence innocent.

A difficulty to keep in view

When does tact become manipulation, and when does understanding become an excuse?

Calling a concealment kind does not settle whose interests it serves. Someone might avoid a conversation to spare a friend unnecessary embarrassment. They might also avoid it to preserve their own comfort, protect an advantage, or deny the friend information needed for a choice.

Ask what is withheld, what the other person needs to know, and who bears the cost of silence. Concealing a passing judgment of vanity differs from concealing an ongoing deception. These questions help evaluate the examples; the two aphorisms do not formulate a complete test.

Explaining the conditions of cruelty does not make its effects disappear. One can reject ultimate moral blame while still needing protection, changed conduct, or an end to a relationship. The text's attack on bitterness does not specify adequate responses to serious harm.

Finally, Nietzsche may overstate his access to what friendship rests on. His bleak observations could describe certain relationships more accurately than others. A reader should ask what evidence would distinguish a friendship sustained by concealment from one strengthened by respectful candor.

Keep the difficult possibility intact: some affection may depend on not making every judgment explicit. Then keep its limit equally clear: maintaining affection cannot be the sole standard for deciding what another person should be told.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What does the dissimulation in I §293 conceal?

It conceals our understanding of another person's motives: we behave as though we have not seen through them. Nietzsche describes this as sometimes necessary and well meant. He does not establish that every judgment about a motive is accurate or every concealment justified.

How can two friends share an opinion while differing?

The opinion can have a different significance, strength, and connection to other commitments in each life. Its place within character and experience matters alongside its verbal content. Agreement in formulation can therefore coexist with sources of misunderstanding.

What is stronger than ordinary perspective-taking in I §376?

Nietzsche treats opinions as necessary products of interwoven conditions and denies ultimate responsibility for them. He suggests that recognizing this may moderate resentment. That causal claim is stronger than saying we should listen politely to different views.

Why does the passage turn from friends' faults to oneself?

Self-knowledge reveals our own changing opinions, moods, and grounds for criticism. This can correct a posture of superiority and support mutual forbearance. It does not require concluding that every fault or every harmful act is equally serious.

Does the ending require pretending that nobody causes harm?

No. Its changed tone suggests a possible release from hostility through understanding and self-scrutiny. The passage leaves questions about responding to harm unresolved. Reduced bitterness can coexist with disagreement, correction, or limits on a relationship.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose a small disagreement between friends, real or imagined. Avoid using an example that requires disclosing private details. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Describe the disagreement and one opinion the friends appear to share. Explain how that opinion might have a different significance in each life.
  2. Offer a possible account of one person's motive or history. Mark what is known and what is a hypothesis.
  3. Describe something it might be tactful to leave unsaid, then identify information that would need to be spoken for the other person to make a fair choice.
  4. Bring the observer into the account: what comparable limitation might they recognize in themselves?

End by explaining whether understanding should change the response, the degree of resentment, or both. Use I §293 for the question of disclosure and I §376 for the movement toward mutual forbearance.
