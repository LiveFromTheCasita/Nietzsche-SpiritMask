What Could Humanity Become?

Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Essential Reading Path · Reading 1 of 10

Read: Prologue, §§1–5, stopping before §6. These are internal sections of the Prologue, which continues through §10.
Allow: About 60–80 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann in The Portable Nietzsche. Free alternative: Prologue §§1–5 (Thomas Common, Thus Spake Zarathustra).

Chapter names follow Common’s edition; see the edition guide for alternate titles. Its Roman numerals I–LXXX number chapters across the book, while the assignment above specifies parts and internal sections. If highlighting fails, locate the stated chapter and section. This guide uses overman where Common writes superman. The introduction and appended notes by other writers are distinct from Nietzsche’s text; the explanations below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Imagine a future in which people are comfortable, secure, and rarely disturbed. Work is pleasant. Conflict is quickly settled. People have small pleasures every day and take good care of their health.

Now add another condition: difficult aspirations have become unintelligible. People no longer understand why anyone would risk their comfort to create something unfamiliar, pursue a demanding question, or change the standards by which they live.

Would that future still seem desirable? What would have been gained, and what would have been lost? Could someone reasonably defend its security against an ideal of perpetual striving?

Zarathustra will describe a future resembling this one as a warning. His listeners will ask him to bring it about. Their response gives us a way into both the philosophy and the story: the teacher and his audience do not agree about what makes a human future worth wanting.

The main question: What should humanity aspire to become—and what would justify that aspiration?

Read Prologue §§1–5 once before continuing. Keep track of who speaks. We hear a narrator, Zarathustra addressing different listeners, the old saint, members of the crowd, and Zarathustra speaking privately to his own heart.

Three things to notice

  1. The overflowing cup. Why does Zarathustra need people to receive his wisdom? What has he assumed about giving before meeting an audience?
  2. The earth and the bridge. What does it mean to seek a possibility beyond present humanity while remaining faithful to earthly life?
  3. The crowd’s request. Why does Zarathustra’s portrait of the last man produce the opposite of its intended effect?

A way to read the passages

§1: wisdom needs someone to receive it

Zarathustra has spent ten years in the mountains, enjoying his solitude. His departure begins when his heart changes. He addresses the sun, comparing its giving of light with his own need to give away what he has gathered. His wisdom resembles honey accumulated by a bee; his happiness resembles an overflowing cup. Return to Prologue §1

The images make abundance the motive for leaving. Yet they also reveal a dependence. What would the sun’s happiness be without those for whom it shines? What can the teacher do with wisdom that nobody receives?

Consider someone who has worked on an idea for years and now wants to share it. The depth of that work does not guarantee that others will understand its significance. Nor does the desire to give establish what the intended recipients need. The analogy helps us notice a question the story is about to test.

Common calls the departure Zarathustra’s “down-going.” Initially, that means descending from the mountain toward people. The language returns in §4, where giving, sacrifice, decline, and preparing a future become entangled. Follow how the meaning develops as the situation changes.

§2: the saint has something to teach the teacher

The saint lives apart from humanity and praises God through song. Zarathustra, by contrast, wants to return to people with gifts. Their exchange puts two uses of solitude beside one another: withdrawal into devotion and preparation for a return. Return to Prologue §2

The saint asks whether people will accept Zarathustra’s treasures. He suggests taking some of their burdens and warns that they distrust solitary wanderers. His advice raises a practical problem: a gift is not received as a gift merely because the giver understands it that way.

Zarathustra does not tell the saint that God is dead. They part laughing, and only afterward does he express his surprise privately. This distinction matters. The announcement appears within a scene in which devotion remains alive and meaningful to someone. It cannot simply mean that nobody believes in God anymore.

A useful way into the phrase is to consider the weakening of divine authority as a foundation for shared judgments about how to live. Zarathustra approaches the human future from a position the saint does not share. These opening pages dramatize that difference; they do not provide a complete historical argument for the diagnosis.

The saint’s religious commitment therefore need not prevent him from seeing a difficulty Zarathustra has underestimated. Notice whether his warning about reception becomes more persuasive once the teacher reaches the town.

§3: the overman names a future to be sought

The crowd has gathered for a tightrope performance. Zarathustra addresses people who did not come to hear his philosophy. He tells them that humanity is something to be surpassed and introduces the overman as the meaning of the earth. Return to Prologue §3

Look closely at the movement from declaring what the overman is to asking what the listeners’ will should say. Zarathustra is calling for an orientation toward the future. The speech asks people to stop treating present humanity as the final measure of what is possible.

His comparisons with worms and apes place human beings within a history of becoming. They make the challenge vivid, but an analogy about previous transformations does not prove that the particular future he desires must arrive. The passage combines a claim about what humans have been with a demand concerning what they should become.

The overman is not identified here with a particular ruler, nation, or race. Neither does the speech give a detailed description of an achieved human type. Its hierarchy is nevertheless explicit: Zarathustra asks us to imagine a possibility in relation to which existing humanity could appear painfully inadequate. Understanding that demand includes questioning its standard of superiority.

Fidelity to the earth constrains the aspiration. Zarathustra attacks hopes that make earthly life and the body contemptible by comparison with an otherworldly destination. His imagined transformation is to give earthly existence a new significance. Simply abandoning belief in another world would leave the positive task unfinished.

§3: dissatisfaction reaches into our virtues

Zarathustra’s “great contempt” is directed at things people commonly prize in themselves: happiness, reason, virtue, justice, and pity. The sequence asks whether these achievements are as substantial as their flattering names suggest.

Someone may call themselves intellectually curious while avoiding questions that could unsettle them. Someone may praise their own justice while treating it as a possession that requires no further work. Such examples help explain why satisfaction with an admirable label might obstruct development.

Zarathustra’s demand goes further. He wants passion, intensity, and a happiness capable of justifying existence. Those are severe measures. We should examine what they reveal about complacency and what would justify demanding them of everyone. The emotional force of the speech cannot supply that justification on its own.

The sea, lightning, and frenzy give the overman an imaginative force before a precise account has emerged. They can awaken a sense of possibility. They also leave the reader with work to do: what would distinguish a valuable transformation from an intoxicating image?

§4: a bridge is valuable for what it makes possible

The rope stretched over an abyss connects the teaching with the performance for which the audience has assembled. Humanity is pictured as a dangerous crossing between the animal and the overman. Its significance lies partly in what it could prepare. Return to Prologue §4

The repeated declarations of love then describe people who give themselves to knowing, creating, inventing, or living through a virtue. Their activity reaches beyond preserving themselves exactly as they are. Some of their work is directed toward a future they may never inhabit.

The passage also repeatedly invokes sacrifice and perishing. These words should retain their difficulty. It would be too easy to translate all of them into ordinary improvement and lose the severity of the ideal. What could justify valuing a future possibility so highly, and how should the lives of those who prepare it count?

At the end of the section, Zarathustra calls himself the herald of the lightning; the overman is the lightning. He presents himself as announcing and preparing a possibility. The distinction gives us a clear reason to avoid simply treating Zarathustra as the overman already achieved.

§5: the last man cannot understand the longing

Zarathustra changes his approach. He hopes that an image of what is most contemptible will appeal to his listeners’ pride. The last man enters as a rhetorical attempt to make the alternative to transformation appear unbearable. Return to Prologue §5

This figure still works, enjoys things, values health, and lives among other people. The problem is what has happened to the range of possible aspirations. Love, creation, longing, and the star have become puzzling words. Anything too demanding is rejected as a burden.

The portrait makes conformity part of this contraction. Everyone wants the same things; difference becomes a reason to exclude oneself from ordinary life. Common’s line about going voluntarily into the madhouse gives a bitter edge to a world supposedly free from troubling conflict.

Health, security, and friendship are not sufficient by themselves to identify a person with this figure. The pattern concerns a way of living that has lost the capacity to recognize anything beyond its settled satisfactions. Yet the speech also expressly associates equality with that pattern. Its hostility to leveling deserves examination alongside its criticism of complacency.

The crowd’s request reverses Zarathustra’s intention: give us the last man, they say, and keep the overman. What he presents as a warning sounds to them like an improvement. His condemnation has made the prospect attractive.

The first lesson belongs to Zarathustra too

Zarathustra interprets the response as a failure of understanding. He also wonders whether he has lived too long among mountains, brooks, and trees to speak to these people. His closing reflection turns attention toward his own preparation for teaching.

The scene permits several questions to remain active. Have the listeners missed his meaning? Have they heard enough to prefer a different ideal? Has his rhetoric failed to show why the future he desires is worth its demands? The crowd gives a response, but not a developed argument; Zarathustra gives a judgment of that response, but not the narrator’s final proof that he is right.

This is why the opening should be read as an encounter as well as a statement of ideas. By the end of §5, the teacher has announced a human possibility and encountered people who want something else. What happens next will test his understanding of how to reach them.

A difficulty to keep in view

What would justify ranking a demanding future above a secure one?

Zarathustra’s language can make it tempting to identify with the exceptional creator and place other people among the last men. That response may spare us the scrutiny his speech directs toward our own happiness, knowledge, and virtue. Feeling above the crowd is an easy satisfaction of its own.

Give the opposing position a fair hearing. Reducing needless suffering, making work tolerable, and protecting ordinary pleasures can be serious achievements. A defender of these goods could ask why they should be made subordinate to a future whose content remains uncertain.

Zarathustra’s strongest challenge concerns what is lost when those goods become the limits of imaginable life. Can a community protect people while still making room for difficult creation and disagreement? Does his contrast reveal a real danger, or force possibilities into too sharp an opposition?

Keep both the attraction and the cost of his ideal visible. The opening gives us grounds for examining complacency. It also leaves the burden of defending its hierarchy and its language of sacrifice unresolved.

Check your understanding

Try answering before reading each suggested response.

Does Zarathustra announce the death of God to the saint?

No. He says it to his own heart after they part. The saint’s continuing devotion shows that the phrase cannot simply report the disappearance of all religious belief. The scene also leaves room for the saint to understand something about giving that Zarathustra has yet to learn.

Is Zarathustra presented as the overman?

Not in these passages. At the end of §4, he describes himself as the herald and the overman as the lightning he announces. He is teaching and preparing a future possibility, while the narrative begins to expose difficulties in his own undertaking.

Does enjoying comfort or caring about health make someone a last man?

Those features alone are insufficient. The portrait combines them with diminished aspiration, avoidance of demanding commitments, and conformity. A careful reading should examine that combination while also confronting Zarathustra’s explicit hostility to equality in the passage.

What does the crowd’s response establish?

It establishes that Zarathustra’s attempt to make the last man repellent has failed. It does not by itself settle whether the audience misunderstands him, reasonably disagrees, or has been poorly addressed. His own closing doubts make the capacity of the teacher part of the question.

What should I write in my notes?

Write six to eight sentences answering this question:

What does Zarathustra think a comfortable human future could lack, and how convincing is his judgment?

  1. Identify one good that the last men possess and give a serious reason to value it.
  2. Use a specific detail from §3, §4, or §5 to explain what Zarathustra thinks is missing.
  3. Give the strongest objection you can to his ranking of these possibilities.
  4. Explain what the crowd’s response reveals about his attempt to teach.

Finish with this sentence:

Before I could accept Zarathustra’s ideal, I would need to understand…

A successful response explains the attraction of both positions, supports an interpretation with a textual detail, and leaves a precise question for the next reading. You do not need to agree with Zarathustra to read him closely.

Continue the reading

First complete the Prologue. The public performance brings Zarathustra into a different kind of encounter, and he begins to reconsider whom he seeks. Then read the camel, lion, and child as images of different tasks involved in transformation.

Carry this question forward: What must change in the person who wants to create something new?
