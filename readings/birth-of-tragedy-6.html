Can Reason Explain Everything?

Essential Reading Path · Reading 6 of 8

Read: The final two paragraphs of §14, then §15 in full, in The Birth of Tragedy
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Haussmann): §14 (ending) · §15.

In §14, read the final two paragraphs, beginning with the question of whether Socrates must be opposed to art. Then read §15 in full.

Before you read

A recording sounds distorted. After an hour of searching, you find the cause, make an adjustment, and hear the music clearly. Understanding has changed what you can do. The pleasure of that discovery is real.

Now consider a larger promise: with enough understanding, every problem in existence could eventually be explained and corrected. Does the success of the first discovery warrant that promise?

Nietzsche places the joy of discovery beside the ambition to make existence comprehensible and justified. His name for the outlook joining them is theoretical optimism. Its representative is Socrates, tragedy’s opponent in our last reading. Here he also represents an extraordinary human achievement and a possible new relation to art.

The main question: Can the pursuit of explanation bring us to a need that explanation itself cannot satisfy?

Read the assigned passages once. Notice where Nietzsche admires the drive toward knowledge and where he challenges the promise attached to it.

What to notice

  1. Socrates’ change of mind. What does he initially think the dream’s instruction means, and what does he eventually do? Notice the questions Nietzsche imagines arising from that change.
  2. The pleasure of searching. Why might the search for truth sustain someone even without the final possession of truth? Follow the image of digging through the earth.
  3. The expanding circle. Why does accumulating knowledge lead Nietzsche’s investigator toward a boundary? What changes when the investigator reaches it?

Close reading

The philosopher who starts making verse

At the close of §14, Nietzsche asks whether Socratism and art must always stand opposed. He recounts a recurring dream that tells Socrates to practice music. Socrates first understands his philosophical activity as fulfilling the instruction. In prison he nevertheless composes a poem to Apollo and versifies Aesop’s fables. §14, Socrates and the dream

Notice what “music” includes in this passage: making poetry. Nietzsche is not describing Socrates learning an instrument. The episode matters because an apparently settled understanding of his vocation becomes questionable. Perhaps philosophy has left a duty to art unfulfilled.

Nietzsche then gives this doubt a philosophical voice. Something beyond the logician’s understanding might possess a wisdom of its own; art might supplement inquiry. These questions are Nietzsche’s development of the scene. They open a possibility that his fierce portrait of Socrates could have concealed. §14, the final questions

Why finding out makes life worth pursuing

In §15, the theoretical person enjoys discovering. Nietzsche distinguishes this pleasure from having finally acquired every truth. His example of digging through the earth makes the difference concrete: an impossible final destination need not erase the worth of discoveries made along the way. The investigator can find something precious without completing the tunnel. §15, discovery and the search for truth

That pleasure gives inquiry a power akin to art’s: it helps sustain a person’s attachment to existence. Nietzsche even imagines knowledge directing enormous human energies away from destructive pursuits. The account therefore gives Socrates a productive role in the very culture whose tragedy he supposedly helped destroy. §15, the cultural power of knowledge

Some of Nietzsche’s framing deserves resistance. His sweeping claim that every culture depends on the Greeks, and his shocking comparison involving Fiji, are assertions in his cultural narrative. They provide no adequate evidence for ranking peoples or making claims about an entire society. Follow the argument about inquiry’s sustaining force while questioning those claims.

When discovery becomes a promise of redemption

The stronger belief enters when thinking claims access to the deepest reality and the power to correct existence. Nietzsche calls this a metaphysical illusion. Metaphysical here signals a claim about reality as a whole: successful explanations are taken to promise that existence itself can be understood and put right. §15, the metaphysical promise

The image of Socrates facing death gives this promise its emotional force. Nietzsche presents him as someone whose knowledge and arguments have overcome the fear of death. Inquiry offers a way to live and die. Its authority reaches beyond solving particular questions into making existence appear justified.

Haussmann’s word science here covers the organized pursuit of knowledge Nietzsche describes through philosophical argument, concepts, and causal explanation. The passage concerns the hopes attached to rational inquiry across a culture, including hopes far larger than any particular experiment.

Return to the recording. Finding its technical fault gives you a reason to trust that explanation. What further argument would establish that all suffering is a correctable fault? Nietzsche presses the distance between those claims. His criticism concerns the extra promise.

At the boundary, art becomes necessary again

Near the end of §15, science appears as an expanding circle. More knowledge brings more contact with its circumference. The investigator eventually encounters something that explanation cannot master; Nietzsche pictures logic turning back on itself and biting its own tail. At that point, the optimistic outlook gives way to what Haussmann calls tragic perception. §15, the limits of inquiry

The term names an encounter with the failure of the promise that knowledge can make existence fully comprehensible and correct it. The investigator’s problem now includes how to endure that encounter. Nietzsche gives art a renewed role as a protection and remedy.

Art need not supply the missing explanation to serve this role. In the earlier readings, a formed image and the Dionysian experience made suffering available to an experience of life that included pleasure. Here Nietzsche returns to that power because argument alone no longer supplies the promised assurance.

The final questions leave the future unsettled. Could inquiry lead to another Socrates who practices music? Could its own development produce a need for art? §15, the possible return to art

A difficulty

Has Nietzsche demonstrated a permanent limit, or described the experience of reaching an unanswered question?

An investigator can fail to explain something today and find a better explanation tomorrow. That situation differs from showing that a question exceeds what causal inquiry could ever answer. The expanding circle and self-entangled logic make the crisis vivid, but an image does not establish which situation we face.

Separate three claims as you reread: inquiry encounters difficulties; inquiry cannot fulfill every promise made for it; art can help us live when that promise fails. Each claim needs its own support. Accepting one leaves the others open to examination.

Then return to Socrates’ dream. It makes room for a changed relation to art without demanding that the philosopher give up thinking. Can that modest possibility survive even if Nietzsche’s larger account of knowledge’s necessary collapse remains unproved?

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does the dream matter to Nietzsche’s portrait of Socrates?

It introduces doubt within the figure who had represented the authority of conscious understanding. Socrates first treats philosophy as the fulfillment of the instruction, then makes poetry as well. Nietzsche uses that change to ask whether art supplies something philosophical inquiry needs. The episode opens a question; it does not show Socrates accepting Nietzsche’s whole theory of tragedy.

What distinguishes the pleasure of discovery from theoretical optimism?

The pleasure concerns the activity and achievements of finding things out. The stronger optimism trusts thought to reach reality’s deepest nature and correct existence. One can value a discovery without believing inquiry will justify life as a whole. Nietzsche describes both, and the distinction explains how he can admire knowledge’s sustaining force while questioning its larger promise.

Why does art return when the investigator reaches a limit?

The loss of confidence in complete explanation creates a difficulty about how to endure existence. Nietzsche assigns art a role in making that encounter bearable through forms of experience. It does not automatically deliver the unknown facts. Whether inquiry reaches the permanent limit he describes, and whether art provides the proposed help, remain questions a reader can test separately.

Writing prompt

Write 150–200 words answering:

What could a Socrates who practices music retain from the pursuit of knowledge, and what would he have to reconsider?

Begin with the prison episode. Explain the difference between Socrates’ first interpretation of the dream and his eventual action. Then use §15 to distinguish the pleasure of discovery from the promise that knowledge can understand and correct existence as a whole. Identify something the imagined philosopher could continue to value in inquiry.

Next, explain why art might become necessary when that larger promise falters. Describe the work it would do without treating it as a source of unverified factual answers. End with one precise question about Nietzsche’s proposed limit to knowledge.

A successful response uses a detail from each assigned section, gives inquiry a positive role, and explains the change that makes room for art. Avoid equating tragic understanding with hopelessness. Your final question should identify something Nietzsche still needs to establish.
