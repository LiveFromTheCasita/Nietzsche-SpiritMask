What Does It Mean to Question Morality?

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 1 of 10

Read: Book II, §103. Read the whole aphorism, stopping before §104.
Allow: About 40–55 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §103 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Think of a rule you once accepted as part of being a good person.

Perhaps good people always put others first. Perhaps they obey without complaining, forgive every injury, or feel guilty whenever they disappoint someone. Choose a rule whose authority once seemed obvious to you, even if you now question it.

Imagine discovering that one of your reasons for accepting that rule was mistaken. What would follow? Would every action associated with it become worthless? Would the people who lived by it turn out to have been dishonest? Would you immediately stop feeling its pull?

These possibilities are easy to run together. Nietzsche’s short aphorism helps us separate them. We begin here because it explains the kind of challenge to morality that Daybreak asks us to consider.

The main question: If the beliefs behind our moral judgments are mistaken, what should change in our actions—and in our feelings?

Read §103 once before continuing. Try to identify the two different meanings Nietzsche gives to denying morality. Notice which one he claims as his own.

Three things to notice

  1. Two different targets. Is Nietzsche questioning whether people really act from moral beliefs, or whether those beliefs are true?
  2. The comparison with alchemy. What does it show about the relationship between conviction and error? What would Nietzsche still need to prove?
  3. The final movement toward feeling. Why does the passage continue beyond changing what we think and what we do?

A way to read the passage

Two questions about the same action

Suppose someone spends an afternoon helping a neighbor. When asked why, they say that helping is their duty.

One kind of critic suspects the explanation. Perhaps the helper wants admiration, fears disapproval, or enjoys feeling indispensable. Their professed moral reason may conceal another motive, even from themselves.

A different critic accepts that the helper acted from duty, then questions the belief that made this particular demand seem binding. The helper’s sincerity is compatible with a mistake about what they ought to do.

The example is ours. It illustrates the distinction Nietzsche draws at the opening of §103:

QuestionWhat is being examined?
Did the person act for the moral reason they give?The explanation of their conduct.
Does that moral reason rest on something true?The basis of the judgment guiding their conduct.

The second question identifies Nietzsche’s own position here. A judgment can actually move someone to act while resting on error. Discovering its influence does not establish its truth; discovering its error does not erase its influence.

This matters because a reader might otherwise hear every criticism of morality as an accusation of hypocrisy. Nietzsche leaves room for sincere devotion to a mistaken judgment. He also explicitly allows that suspicion of professed motives is justified in many cases. Both investigations remain possible, but they ask different questions.

Try applying the distinction to the rule you chose. You might discover that you have sometimes followed it for reasons you misunderstood. You might also discover that you followed it sincerely while accepting a questionable account of why it mattered. Either discovery would require further thought.

Why he compares morality with alchemy

Nietzsche next compares his rejection of morality with rejecting alchemy. Someone can deny alchemy’s assumptions while recognizing that people believed them and organized their activity around them. The existence of committed practitioners does not establish the truth of their theory. §103, the alchemy comparison

Applied to moral life, the comparison separates the force of a conviction from its justification. A belief can organize sacrifice, effort, and an entire way of life. Those effects are real even if the belief fails examination.

Pause before allowing the analogy to carry more weight than it can bear. Comparing morality with a discredited undertaking does not demonstrate that morality deserves the comparison. Nietzsche still owes us an account of the errors he attributes to moral judgment.

His claim is stronger than the modest observation that some familiar rules might need revision. He says that errors underlie all moral judgments. This aphorism identifies that sweeping position and explains what he means by it; it does not establish every part of the case.

Read it as an invitation to investigate a demanding claim. You can understand the distinction he draws while remaining unconvinced by the conclusion he wants to reach. A careful reader should be able to say both what Nietzsche is claiming and what evidence they would need before accepting it.

What can it mean to deny immorality?

The next move is more unsettling. Nietzsche also denies immorality. He acknowledges that people feel immoral, while denying the true basis for that feeling in the judgments he attacks. §103, the discussion of felt immorality

Consider someone who has been taught that refusing any request makes them selfish. They decline a request and feel guilty. The feeling is real. Whether the refusal warrants that judgment remains a separate question.

This example illustrates the distinction on a small scale. It does not establish Nietzsche’s much broader claim. Nor should an example of questionable guilt make us forget cases in which someone has caused an injury that needs attention.

Ask three questions separately: What happened? What consequences did it have? What judgment is the person making about themselves? A familiar moral description may combine these questions so tightly that we stop noticing the joins.

Nietzsche’s denial does not make the action or its consequences disappear. As the next sentence makes clear, he continues to think that many actions should be resisted. The passage gives us no complete account of how to respond to wrongdoing, but it prevents the easy inference that questioning a moral judgment means abandoning every practical distinction.

An action may survive a change in its reasons

Nietzsche explicitly retains two possibilities: many actions called immoral should be avoided, and many actions called moral should be encouraged. Their motives, however, would have to change. §103, the practical qualification

Return to the neighbor. Suppose the helper once believed that a good person must accept every demand. After questioning that belief, they may still decide to help. They might attend more closely to what the neighbor needs, what their assistance can accomplish, and what other commitments they have.

From outside, the afternoon might look much the same. Yet the changed explanation could matter tomorrow. It could make a particular refusal possible, redirect the help, or disclose that the original arrangement was doing little good.

These are possible applications of the distinction, rather than a replacement moral code supplied by Nietzsche. Section 103 does not tell us that consequences alone settle every question, or that personal preference becomes the final authority.

Notice his qualification: many actions, rather than every action. He leaves open how much conduct might change. We should therefore resist treating the passage as reassurance that all familiar practices will survive once their explanations have been adjusted.

The task is to reconsider what deserves encouragement or resistance, and on what grounds. Calling a reason new does not yet make it better.

Why changing your mind may leave your feelings behind

The aphorism ends with the prospect of learning anew and, perhaps much later, feeling anew. Its last two words in Kennedy’s translation are “feel anew.” §103, closing sentence

That delay matters. Imagine the person who has rejected the demand to accept every request. They can explain their objections clearly. Nevertheless, each refusal still brings an immediate sense of having failed. An argument they no longer accept continues to have an emotional afterlife.

This is one way to make sense of Nietzsche’s closing ambition. The work would have to reach beyond our stated opinions into what we instinctively approve, fear, resent, or feel ashamed of.

It also changes how we might judge our progress as readers. Understanding an argument does not guarantee that we have absorbed its consequences. We may recognize a difficulty intellectually while continuing to react as before.

The passage offers neither a timetable nor a technique for changing those reactions. Its qualification about how late the change might come makes the undertaking look demanding. And a new feeling would still need examination; novelty alone would not certify its worth.

Return to your original rule. Can you identify a difference between what you now believe about it and how it still makes you feel? That difference gives you something concrete to carry into the rest of the book.

A difficulty to keep in view

How will Nietzsche justify the new reasons he wants us to act from?

Rejecting an inherited justification creates work. If we continue to encourage some actions and resist others, we need to explain those choices. We also need to ask why the standards used in that explanation should withstand the scrutiny applied to the old ones.

Section 103 does not finish that task. Its value as an opening lies partly in making the task visible. We can now ask what would support the practices we retain, which ones should change, and how far our judgments depend on assumptions we have yet to identify.

Keep your demand for reasons directed toward Nietzsche too. It would be an odd result of studying this passage to grant his judgments immunity from the questions he teaches us to ask of other people’s.

Try writing one sentence beginning: For Nietzsche’s criticism to succeed, he would need to show…

You do not need to resolve the foundations of morality. You do need to distinguish understanding his proposal from accepting it.

Check your understanding

Try answering before reading each suggested response.

Can someone sincerely act from a moral belief that Nietzsche considers mistaken?

Yes. That possibility is central to his second meaning of denying morality. A belief’s ability to motivate someone and the truth of its assumptions are different matters. He also allows that people sometimes misrepresent or misunderstand their motives.

Does the alchemy comparison prove that moral judgments are false?

No. It clarifies how sincere commitment can coexist with error. To establish the corresponding claim about morality, Nietzsche must identify and criticize the assumptions on which its judgments depend.

If an old reason for helping fails, must the helping stop?

No. The passage allows many actions to continue from different motives. But it does not promise that every familiar action will survive, or supply a complete account of which new reasons should guide us.

Why does the passage end with feeling?

Because revising an explicit belief may leave established reactions in place. Nietzsche anticipates a deeper, possibly much slower change in how we experience moral judgments. The section announces that possibility without guaranteeing its achievement.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to one rule about being a good person. Choose an ordinary example you can examine closely. Write six to eight sentences that do four things:

  1. State the rule and identify one belief that gives it authority for you.
  2. Separate a question about your motives from a question about whether that belief is true.
  3. Describe an action you might retain, revise, or stop if the belief failed examination. Give a reason for your choice.
  4. Identify a feeling that might persist even after your judgment changed.

Finish with this sentence:

The reason I have offered would itself need defending because…

A successful response distinguishes motive, justification, action, and feeling. You can conclude that the rule deserves to be retained. What matters is making your reasons available for examination.
