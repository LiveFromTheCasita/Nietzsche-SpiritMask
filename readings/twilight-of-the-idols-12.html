What Kind of Freedom Does Nietzsche Want?

Twilight of the Idols — Complete Reading Course · Reading 12 of 14

Read: “Skirmishes of an Untimely Man,” §§38–43. Read every assigned section in full.

Before you read

Bring forward the distinction between strength and mere aggression from the previous lesson. Here Nietzsche attaches his preferred kind of strength to institutions and social arrangements. Watch who receives freedom and who supplies its conditions.

Keep two columns in your notes: what Nietzsche says an arrangement produces, and why he thinks that result deserves preference. A claim about consequences is not yet a justification of the distribution of those consequences.

The main question: Does developing powerful, responsible individuals require the political and social inequalities Nietzsche defends?

Three things to notice

  1. Two meanings of freedom In Skirmishes §38, freedom concerns a capacity acquired through resistance. Liberal freedom concerns institutions and protections. Track Nietzsche’s movement between them: the argument depends on his claim that established protections weaken the capacity they promise to secure.
  2. The people inside the institution In Skirmishes §§39–40, look beyond the institution’s durability to the position of wives, children, and workers. Nietzsche makes their unequal position part of his explanation. The costs are not an incidental detail added by a hostile reader.
  3. Neither liberal progress nor restoration Skirmishes §43 rejects a return to an earlier moral condition. Keep that claim beside §39’s praise of inherited authority. Admiring an institution’s former strength is not the same as believing its conditions can simply be brought back.

A way to read the passages

Freedom has to be exercised: Skirmishes §38

Nietzsche asks us to assess freedom through the resistance a person can confront and the responsibility that person can sustain. On this account, removing an obstacle and acquiring a capacity are different events. Someone can receive permission to act while remaining unable to direct competing impulses or endure the consequences of a decision. Conversely, a struggle against restriction can develop capacities that outlast the struggle itself. This is the plausible starting point of §38’s paradox: fighting for liberal institutions may develop qualities that comfortable life within those institutions does not automatically reproduce.

The paragraph goes considerably further. Nietzsche praises martial dispositions, aristocratic societies, distance from others, and readiness to sacrifice people, including oneself, for a cause. His examples operate politically as well as psychologically; this is not merely an exercise metaphor. Separate the claim that difficult responsibilities develop agency from the claim that liberal institutions necessarily destroy it. The latter requires evidence about institutions and alternatives. His contemptuous comparisons, including women and national groups, intensify the polemic without supplying that evidence. Ask whether protected freedom could support demanding activities rather than eliminate their difficulty.

What allows an institution to last? Skirmishes §39

The initial move of §39 places institutional weakness in the people who inhabit institutions. Rules cannot manufacture every disposition required to sustain them. A community that values only immediate advantage may struggle to keep promises extending beyond its present membership. Nietzsche therefore emphasizes inherited responsibility, authority, continuity, and a willingness to organize life for generations not yet born. His positive examples include imperial Rome and Russia; his assessment belongs to the political world of the text and should not be treated as a timeless verdict on any country.

The philosophical issue is whether durability warrants the authority that produces it. A long-lasting institution may preserve a valuable practice, but it may also preserve domination. Nietzsche largely treats the power to organize across generations as a sign of rank; he does not give equal weight to the experience of everyone organized. Test the unstated inference: because lasting institutions need commitments stronger than momentary preference, must they be authoritarian? Shared law, contestable offices, and durable voluntary commitments would need examination before the conclusion follows. These alternatives frame an objection; the paragraph does not itself settle their success.

Marriage makes the cost visible: Skirmishes §39

Nietzsche’s marriage example makes his institutional argument concrete. He locates its former rationale in male legal responsibility, indissolubility, family control of partner selection, inheritance, and continuity of possession and power. He explicitly includes wife and child within the property relation. Romantic attachment, by contrast, appears too changeable and individual to found an enduring institution. The contrast is not simply between shallow romance and mature commitment. It is between a modern justification of marriage and a patriarchal arrangement whose stability depends on unequal authority.

There is a question worth separating from that arrangement: what can sustain a commitment when feelings change? Yet extracting that question must not erase the text’s answer. Nietzsche treats the permanence of the family line and its acquired power as organizing ends; freedom of exit and equality between spouses do not have comparable standing. A good response should identify both the insight about unstable motivation and the additional values that make his preferred solution objectionable or defensible. Long duration alone cannot tell us whether remaining inside a particular marriage serves those who must remain.

Workers reveal whose freedom counts: Skirmishes §40

Section 40 objects to the political developments that enable workers to regard their condition as an injustice. Nietzsche names military participation, association, and voting as changes that cultivate expectations incompatible with a permanently subordinate laboring class. His final conditional exposes a contradiction: a society cannot coherently want obedient laborers while educating them for independent command. That observation can illuminate the instability of domination. In its setting, however, Nietzsche also regrets the loss of the dispositions that would make subordination tolerable to workers themselves.

Do not turn this into an unqualified argument for workers’ emancipation. His preferred prospect includes a compliant laboring type, supported by a racial stereotype of Chinese people. The passage’s hierarchy is explicit. Compare it with §38: some people’s capacities for command count as freedom, while the growth of such capacities among workers becomes a problem. Nietzsche can answer that different human types require different conditions; the reader must then ask how those types are identified and why political standing should follow. The text’s frustration with inconsistent means does not independently justify its intended end.

Formation or suppression? Skirmishes §41

Section 41 rejects the idea that leaving modern individuals to their instincts will naturally produce coherent persons. Nietzsche understands modernity as conflict among inherited systems of impulse. If those systems pull in incompatible directions, unrestricted expression can intensify disintegration. Education must establish order among drives, strengthening some by restraining others. This returns to a problem encountered earlier in the book: an organized character is an achievement, not the automatic expression of whatever one happens to feel.

His language of pressure and pruning also creates a question about authority. Who decides which drives should dominate, and whose judgment identifies a coherent life? A person practicing an instrument may accept demanding discipline because it develops an endorsed capacity. An authority suppressing another person’s possibilities may also claim to be creating order. Nietzsche does not erase that difference by making coherence valuable. Keep the potential insight and the political problem together: education involves selection and restraint, but the need for formation does not yet determine who should control it or which forms of individuality should survive.

Sincerity can serve a predetermined result: Skirmishes §42

The attack on saints and philosophers in §42 explains how conviction can serve a practical interest without conscious lying. If believing a proposition is more effective than merely pretending to believe it, motivated interpretation can become sincere. Nietzsche’s target is inquiry whose conclusion has been selected in advance because a profession or moral authority needs it. His joke about Kant’s practical reason belongs to this accusation; it is not a sufficient account of Kant’s actual argument.

This gives the lesson a test that also applies to its author. Does Nietzsche’s commitment to hierarchy influence which historical examples he admits as evidence? Does a reader’s commitment to equality similarly make some questions difficult to entertain? Identifying a motive does not automatically refute a proposition. A sincere belief can be true, and an interested argument can be valid. The useful question is procedural: what evidence would this person allow to count against the desired conclusion? Without that possibility, professed honesty can become another protection against inquiry.

Why conservatism cannot turn the clock back: Skirmishes §43

Section 43 refuses the conservative dream of restoring a previous moral condition. Nietzsche speaks with the authority of physiology, but his sweeping claim that reversal is impossible is asserted here rather than demonstrated through biological evidence. Social change has altered the people who would inhabit the supposedly restored arrangement. A code brought back into force therefore need not recover the forms of life that once made it effective. Resistance may delay or concentrate a process without reversing its underlying direction.

This complicates §39. Nietzsche admires enduring authority yet denies that historical loss can simply be repaired by reinstating an old standard. It also complicates the label progressive: the forward movement he describes is further decadence, not improvement. The reader should resist resolving these tensions into a ready-made party program. What emerges is a diagnosis with severe political preferences and uncertain prospects. Its lasting challenge is to explain how new forms of agency and commitment could be cultivated after inherited ones lose credibility, without pretending that either unrestricted choice or restored obedience answers every question.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can I accept the account of self-command while rejecting Nietzsche’s political hierarchy?

Yes, but that is an argued reconstruction, not a complete paraphrase. Nietzsche connects resistance, rank, institutional authority, and unequal standing throughout these sections. To preserve only the language of personal discipline would conceal that connection. State which premises you accept and which inference you reject: for example, the fact that capacities develop unevenly does not by itself establish that people should have unequal political rights.

Then give an alternative account of how demanding capacities could develop. If equal standing provides security for disagreement, education, and demanding shared projects, it may cultivate forms of responsibility Nietzsche underestimates. That reply needs examples and reasoning. Equally, simply labeling him elitist does not answer his question about whether institutions develop the dispositions needed to sustain them. A serious disagreement leaves the strongest question standing while challenging the proposed answer.

Check your understanding

Try answering before revealing each suggested response.

Why can the struggle for an institution have different effects from living within it?

Skirmishes §38 argues that struggle develops resistance, responsibility, and martial dispositions, whereas established security may favor comfort. This explains Nietzsche’s proposed contrast; it does not prove that every liberal institution weakens agency.

What makes the marriage example more than a criticism of romantic expectations?

Skirmishes §39 defends specific conditions: male authority, indissolubility, family selection, and the transmission of property and power. The inequality is part of the arrangement Nietzsche regards as institutionally rational.

Why is it misleading to read §40 simply as an attack on the exploitation of workers?

It identifies an inconsistency in cultivating political agency while expecting obedience, but also regrets the disappearance of a compliant laboring class. Nietzsche questions the means while retaining a hierarchical conception of the social end.

How do §§41–42 complicate the advice to trust your instincts and beliefs?

Section 41 argues that instincts may conflict and need formation. Section 42 argues that sincere belief may serve a predetermined practical conclusion. Neither spontaneity nor sincerity alone establishes coherence or intellectual honesty.

Why does §43 prevent a straightforward restorationist reading?

Nietzsche says past conditions cannot simply be recovered. He admires certain older forms of organization while treating the forces that dissolved them as historically irreversible, and he does not equate forward movement with improvement.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 350–500 words answering: Does Nietzsche’s account of freedom require unequal political standing? Use Skirmishes §38 and at least two of §§39–43.

  1. Reconstruct the strongest connection between self-command, resistance, and durable institutions. Avoid substituting your preferred definition of freedom before explaining his.
  2. Examine one concrete case: marriage, workers’ rights, or education. Identify who directs the arrangement, who bears its costs, and which outcome Nietzsche treats as valuable.
  3. Offer one precise objection to the inference from unequal capacities to unequal standing. Give the strongest response Nietzsche could make from the assigned sections.
  4. End with your revised position. Specify whether you reject a factual claim, a standard of value, or the inference connecting them.

Keep this argument for the final exercise. A difficult political passage must be part of your assessment of the whole book, rather than an awkward appendix to the passages you prefer.
