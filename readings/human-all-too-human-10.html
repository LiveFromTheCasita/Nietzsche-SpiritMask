Learning to Live as a Wanderer

Human, All Too Human — Essential Reading Path · Reading 10 of 10

Read: WS §§5–6 (1880), stopping before WS §7; then I §638 (1878), the final numbered aphorism of Volume I, ending before the verse epilogue. This session deliberately returns to the earlier volume.
Allow: About 65–85 minutes, including the passages, this guide, a return to earlier readings, and the final written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale. Free alternatives: I §638 (Helen Zimmern); WS §5 · WS §6 (Paul V. Cohn).

I means Volume I; AOM means Assorted Opinions and Maxims (called Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions in the free edition); WS means The Wanderer and His Shadow. Numbers restart in each collection. The numbered 1886 prefaces are separate texts. If highlighting fails, use the collection and numbered heading.

Before you read

Imagine someone who can discuss freedom intelligently but has never examined the conditions of an ordinary working day. They accept every interruption, arrange their time around other people's expectations, and leave the activity they value until they are too tired to begin it.

Now imagine another person who has perfected a routine and becomes angry whenever life disturbs it. They know how to protect their concentration, but their arrangements have become something they cannot question.

These examples give us two difficulties to carry into the final reading. Independence needs ordinary conditions that sustain it. It also needs room to reconsider those conditions. Paying attention to daily life can support inquiry without supplying a permanent arrangement that settles it.

Reading 9 examined the authority of conviction and the danger of turning limited observations into universal explanations. Here Nietzsche directs observation toward familiar activities, then asks what a life of continuing inquiry might feel like.

The main question: What would make intellectual independence a continuing practice within an ordinary life?

Three things to notice

  1. Words and priorities. In WS §5, compare what people call important with what actually occupies their concern. What does the mismatch prevent them from examining?
  2. The observer's own examples. In WS §6, distinguish the call for attention from the claims offered to illustrate it. Do those claims meet the standards we have learned to ask of an explanation?
  3. The changing weather of independence. In I §638, follow the closed gates, disappointing daylight, and later mornings. What remains possible without becoming secure?

A way to read the passages

When declared importance misdirects attention

WS §5 begins with a discrepancy between elevated language and ordinary attachments. People profess indifference to eating, housing, clothing, and social intercourse, while these matters remain deeply important to them. Conversely, their declared reverence for supposedly higher things exceeds what they actually feel.

Nietzsche calls this a double hypocrisy. The point goes beyond accusing a particular person of dishonesty. An inherited vocabulary can make certain concerns seem unworthy of serious thought, even while those concerns organize much of a person's life.

Consider a shared workspace. Its occupants might regard where people sit, when they can speak, and how interruptions are handled as trivial arrangements beneath the dignity of their intellectual work. Yet these arrangements shape who can concentrate and whose contributions are heard. Their apparent smallness can protect them from examination.

The example illustrates a misplaced division between important work and ordinary conditions. It does not establish Nietzsche's general claim about everyone's genuine priorities. To assess that claim, we would need to examine how particular people speak, act, and allocate attention.

The consequence he emphasizes is neglect. Habits and casual decisions govern matters that never receive sustained intellectual or artistic attention. The vocabulary of higher importance helps divert the very capacities that might improve everyday arrangements.

What kind of dependence could attention change?

The end of WS §5 makes a more forceful claim. Repeated neglect of bodily and mental conditions produces dependence on physicians, teachers, and clergy, whose influence Nietzsche describes as a burden on society. His attack reaches institutions and the authority of specialists.

We should distinguish the insight about neglected conditions from the sweep of that conclusion. Some dependence might be reduced through knowledge and better arrangements. Other dependence arises because people need expertise, care, cooperation, or resources they cannot provide alone.

Needing a physician or a teacher does not demonstrate a failure to attend properly to one's life. The passage provides no basis for a reader to dismiss necessary care. Its distinction between avoidable dependence and dependence that belongs to human vulnerability needs much more work than the closing accusation gives it.

Return to Reading 6. If independence involves examining the grounds of an inherited judgment, then receiving another person's assistance can be compatible with it. The relevant questions include how authority operates, what can be questioned, and which capacities assistance enables.

Nietzsche asks us to investigate daily arrangements. His own account of dependence is one of the judgments that such investigation should test.

The demand for observation must reach the observer

WS §6 continues with examples concerning eggs, weather, fragrance, taste, and digestion. Nietzsche presents these as things people fail to notice despite long familiarity. He then identifies ignorance about daily conditions as a cause of nearly all bodily and mental troubles.

The structure makes an evidential question unavoidable. Are the opening observations reliable? Would they support a conclusion about nearly all human difficulties even if they were? The aphorism gives us assertions about bodily life, not a demonstrated account of their causes.

Read this beside AOM §5 from the previous session. There we questioned the enlargement of limited observations into explanations that exceed their support. The same question belongs here, even though the recommendation to pay attention may attract us.

This is an opportunity to practice the course's method. We can separate a valuable question from an inadequately established answer. What effects do familiar arrangements have? That question survives our refusal to treat every physiological claim in the aphorism as trustworthy.

We should also avoid making responsibility for one's circumstances limitless. A person may understand a harmful working arrangement perfectly while lacking the power to change it. Poverty, illness, care obligations, and other people's decisions can constrain what attentive observation makes possible.

Reason redirected toward the near at hand

Nietzsche does not finish by blaming an absolute shortage of reason. He says that intellectual effort is directed elsewhere. The ambitions presented to a child concern salvation, service to the state, science, status, or property; the particular requirements of the child's day are treated as insignificant.

The contrast connects with Reading 1. A practice can acquire prestige through inherited judgments before anyone investigates what it does. Here the hierarchy of important and unimportant subjects shapes where inquiry is allowed to begin.

It also complicates a simple story in which science automatically replaces religious error. Scientific achievement can become another grand purpose that excuses neglect of particular people. The question concerns how a purpose organizes attention, not merely whether its name sounds modern.

Imagine changing a meeting schedule because its habitual timing excludes someone who provides care at home. The change could serve the shared work and the person's particular circumstances together. Attention to ordinary conditions need not require abandoning every collective undertaking.

The aphorism nevertheless leaves that relationship underdeveloped. How should a person's needs be weighed against a difficult shared task? Which conditions can be altered individually, and which require collective action? The value of the inquiry partly lies in making these questions visible.

Returning to the earlier wanderer

We now move back from The Wanderer and His Shadow, published in 1880, to the final numbered aphorism of the original 1878 Volume I. The order is deliberate. After examining the conditions of a day, we return to an earlier image of the life those conditions might support.

I §638 describes someone who has attained intellectual emancipation to some extent. That qualification matters. The wanderer is already underway without possessing complete freedom or an account of the journey's final destination.

Nietzsche distinguishes wandering from traveling toward a final goal. The figure wants to observe what happens and retain a capacity to enjoy change. Attaching the heart too firmly to a particular thing could obstruct that movement.

The absence of a final goal need not mean that someone cannot finish a project, keep a promise, or choose a destination. Those are particular undertakings within a life. The image questions whether the whole journey can be organized around a final arrival that would end the need to inquire.

Yet the warning against firm attachment creates a difficulty. Some understanding depends on staying with a person, place, or practice when novelty has disappeared. We should ask whether the wanderer's freedom can include the commitments through which difficult knowledge becomes possible.

The closed gates and the disappointing morning

The middle of the aphorism refuses an uncomplicated romance of wandering. A tired traveler finds the town closed, the desert extending to its gates, threatening animals, wind, and robbers. The heart becomes weary of the very movement that seemed to promise freedom.

Morning does not immediately repair the scene. The town opens, but its inhabitants can appear more insecure, deceptive, and desolate than the landscape outside. Nietzsche separates the arrival of daylight from the arrival of reassurance.

The details bring inquiry into contact with exhaustion and disappointment. Leaving a familiar certainty can remove shelter before a better understanding has emerged. Encountering other people does not guarantee the companionship the wanderer needs.

These are images within a philosophical portrait. They establish no requirement that a thinker should seek actual isolation or suffering. The force of the passage lies in refusing to make intellectual emancipation automatically comfortable.

Companionship without a final arrival

Other mornings nevertheless come. The muses appear near the mountains; trees offer bright gifts associated with free spirits who inhabit forests, mountains, and solitudes. The wanderer discovers a world in which thought and pleasure can meet again.

Notice that solitude does not erase companionship. The scene contains gifts from others who are also thoughtful, merry, and wandering. Their affinity does not depend on everyone having reached the same final conclusion.

The muses also reconnect the ending with Reading 5. Art has not simply vanished after criticism of inspiration and consoling beliefs. The ending draws on artistic images to make a possible relation to inquiry perceptible.

The final question concerns the clarity and cheerfulness of a particular portion of the day. It does not offer possession of an everlasting noon. The wanderers seek to understand these mornings while remaining people whose nights can go badly.

That gives the course an appropriately unfinished ending. We can leave with clearer distinctions and better questions, without claiming that either Nietzsche or his reader has reached a position beyond revision.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can independence include dependable attachments and assistance?

The selected passages create pressure from two directions. WS §§5–6 question neglect that makes us unnecessarily dependent; I §638 questions attachment that arrests inquiry. Taken carelessly, their combination could make needing anyone appear shameful.

But people develop their capacities through others. A friend can help us recognize an evasion; a teacher can introduce a method; an institution can protect time for inquiry. These relations can also constrain or dominate. Their character cannot be settled simply by calling them dependencies.

We can therefore ask how an attachment enables questioning and how it responds to disagreement. Does it require pretending to believe? Does it permit changed judgments? Does the freedom it gives one person depend on another person's unacknowledged work?

The last question returns to Reading 8. An account of independence remains incomplete when it ignores whose circumstances support it and whose voice is excluded. The wanderer's attractive image should face that question too.

A defensible response may preserve the practice of continuing inquiry while rejecting the suggestion that all dependence can or should disappear. Explain which part of Nietzsche's challenge survives that revision.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What does the mismatch between words and priorities do in WS §5?

It helps exclude ordinary concerns from serious reflection. People give elevated matters declared importance while leaving conditions that shape their lives to habit and casual decisions.

Why must WS §6's examples themselves be examined?

The recommendation to observe carefully does not establish the truth of the observations used to support it. Its broad explanation of human troubles also exceeds what a few examples could prove. Reading 9's demand for proportion between evidence and claim applies to Nietzsche here.

Does the wanderer's lack of a final goal prevent particular commitments?

The passage supplies no necessary inference from the absence of a final destination for inquiry to the impossibility of ordinary projects or promises. But its warning against attachment creates a real question about how sustained commitments belong within the ideal.

Why is the morning after the closed gates significant?

Daylight and access to the town fail to guarantee relief. Nietzsche keeps the costs and disappointments of wandering visible instead of treating departure from inherited certainty as automatically rewarding.

What does the ending allow without securing it permanently?

It imagines renewed pleasure in observation, artistic receptivity, and companionship with other free spirits. Its hopeful mornings remain episodes within a continuing journey, rather than proof that every difficulty has been overcome.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to your first judgment. Reread I §§2–3 from Reading 1. Then revisit either I §416 or WS §293 from Reading 8, and compare that passage with today's WS §§5–6 and I §638.

Write about 200–300 words answering: What can I now defend about the practice of intellectual independence?

  1. State one expectation you brought to the course. Use your original notes if you kept them.
  2. Explain how a specific claim in I §416 or WS §293 tests the historical modesty demanded in I §§2–3. Identify what you accept, reject, or find insufficiently supported.
  3. Use WS §§5–6 to describe one ordinary condition that could support inquiry, while naming a limit on what individual observation can change.
  4. Give a revised judgment about the wanderer in I §638. What attracts you, and what commitment or dependence must your version of independence accommodate?

A successful response shows how the texts changed the grounds of your judgment. Agreement with Nietzsche is optional. Your task is to defend what remains after applying his questions to his own claims.
