How Custom Becomes Conscience

Daybreak — Essential Reading Path · Reading 2 of 10

Read: Book I, §9, then §38. Read each in full, stopping before §10 and §39 respectively.
Allow: About 60–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. Free alternative: §9 · §38 (J. M. Kennedy, The Dawn of Day).

Use the aphorism numbers in either translation; page numbers and English headings vary. If highlighting fails, locate the numbered heading and read to the stated stopping point. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are the translator’s commentary, distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The explanations and everyday examples below are this guide’s commentary.

Before you read

Return to the rule you considered in Reading 1. Perhaps it was a demand to put others first, obey without complaining, or feel guilty whenever someone was disappointed. Who first made that rule seem authoritative?

Now imagine following it for a reason different from the one you were taught. You help someone because the help seems worthwhile, while refusing to accept that obedience itself makes you good. Would the people who taught you the rule regard this as the same action? Would you feel the same while doing it?

This is our opening example. It introduces a distinction that matters throughout §9: doing what tradition prescribes and doing it because tradition prescribes it.

Reading 1 separated a moral conviction’s sincerity from the truth of its assumptions. These passages investigate how conviction can acquire an inward voice, making an inherited judgment feel like an immediate fact about oneself.

The main question: How can an authority outside us become part of what obedience, independence, and even an impulse feel like?

Read Book I, §§9 and 38 in full. The first reconstructs the authority of custom; the second examines the feelings formed under moral judgments.

Three things to notice

  1. The ground of obedience. In §9, why can doing the customary thing for an independent reason still count as immoral?
  2. Who bears the violation. Notice the distinction between frequent and difficult obedience, then the movement from an individual transgression to the community’s guilt.
  3. What the judgment changes. In §38, ask whether a moral name merely describes an existing feeling or helps give the impulse its emotional character.

A way to read the passages

An unfamiliar meaning of moral

Section 9 begins by calling Nietzsche’s own age comparatively immoral. The initial meaning concerns the weakened power of custom. He is not simply saying that his contemporaries have become more cruel or less helpful. His reconstruction uses an older standard of judgment. Book I, §9

That standard is obedience to inherited ways of acting and valuing, whatever their particular content. A practice counts as morally binding because it belongs to tradition. Its authority does not first depend on an investigation showing that it benefits the people who follow it.

The claim is deliberately abrasive. Nietzsche defines morality through obedience to custom, while recognizing later moralists whose individual aims departed from that pattern. His account challenges our tendency to project present meanings of goodness backward into all human history.

Imagine a household in which everyone must attend a particular meal. Someone attends because they enjoy the company but openly denies that attendance is an unconditional duty. Under Nietzsche’s reconstructed standard, the outward conformity leaves something essential missing: submission to the command.

The invented household clarifies the distinction. It supplies no evidence that all early societies actually operated this way. Section 9 is a broad historical reconstruction, not established anthropology supported here by a systematic comparison of communities.

Tradition claims an authority beyond usefulness

Nietzsche asks what tradition is and answers in terms of a superior authority that commands. The fear surrounding it is more than an ordinary calculation that disobedience will have unwelcome practical consequences. He connects it with an incomprehensible, more-than-personal power.

Suppose a rule once helped a community coordinate agricultural work. People could obey it after examining whether the coordination still helps. They could also obey because questioning what has been handed down seems presumptuous or dangerous. Those relations to the same practice differ.

The first makes room for revising the arrangement when circumstances change. The second treats the authority of tradition as something the individual has no standing to reassess. Nietzsche’s account places superstition within the fear that guards that authority.

Its reach is wide. Education, medicine, marriage, agriculture, warfare, speech, and silence all fall under customary prescriptions. The older domain of morality therefore extends far beyond what a modern reader might classify as specifically ethical decisions.

Someone trying to depart from this order faces more than disagreement about one rule. Innovation threatens the standing of inherited authority. Nietzsche accordingly pictures the maker of new customs needing the extraordinary authority of a lawgiver or religious figure.

Two ways to excel at obedience

The middle of §9 distinguishes obeying most frequently from obeying under the greatest difficulty. Frequent obedience fills small intervals of life with occasions to observe a rule. Difficult obedience proves its authority against powerful desires and apparent advantages.

These are different ways of measuring moral distinction. One makes attention to every prescribed detail exemplary. The other prizes the scale of the sacrifice. They could favor different people and encourage different habits.

Why should hardship make obedience impressive? On Nietzsche’s account, the sacrifice demonstrates that tradition prevails even when the individual has strong reasons to resist. Self-conquest serves the supremacy of custom.

Think of someone who declines a valuable opportunity because accepting it would violate an inherited expectation. Observers might praise the cost as evidence of fidelity. The cost alone, however, tells us neither whether the expectation is justified nor whether the resulting sacrifice benefits anyone.

Nietzsche is not describing every demanding commitment as obedience to tradition. His explanation identifies what difficult obedience accomplishes within the morality of custom: the individual’s competing interests become occasions for displaying the command’s power.

The same discipline can become a departure

Nietzsche next introduces moralists associated with Socrates who recommend restraint and sobriety for the individual’s own advantage and happiness. We may regard this recommendation as ordinary moral advice. Within his earlier framework, its ground makes it exceptional.

The individual now asks what a practice does for their life. Even when the recommended conduct resembles inherited discipline, its justification has changed. Such a person can appear immoral to defenders of custom because they have claimed an independent basis for acting.

Nietzsche makes a related comparison between a Christian concerned above all with personal salvation and a traditional Roman. A religious pursuit that later becomes familiar can initially look like a dangerous departure from communal obligations.

Keep the viewpoint explicit. Calling these people evil reports how the reconstructed morality of custom would judge them. It does not tell us that Nietzsche endorses that verdict, or that every departure deserves praise.

This returns to Reading 1’s point that conduct and justification can change at different rates. Two people might practice similar restraint while understanding its authority very differently. The outward action alone does not settle their relationship to morality.

The community discovers the violation within itself

The closing movement of §9 explains why an individual departure can frighten an entire community. Supernatural punishment is imagined to fall on the group. Its extent and manifestations remain uncertain, so a violation can make otherwise unexplained misfortunes appear threatening.

Nietzsche distinguishes several responses. The community can demand compensation for harmful consequences. It can direct vengeance toward an offender by interpreting disaster as divine anger. More deeply, it can regard the individual’s guilt as its own: a world in which the transgression became possible must already have grown morally lax.

This is more than the group blaming a convenient outsider. Its members understand themselves as implicated in the breach. Their fear can be sincere, and the offender can share it. Custom’s power works through people’s interpretations of their own conduct.

The cost falls especially heavily on unusual minds. Someone with an original thought may experience that originality as evidence of danger or wickedness, even before anyone punishes them. The bad conscience of independence is the passage’s final concern.

The argument does not establish that every uneasy feeling about dissent comes from this history. It offers a demanding possibility: what seems to be an inward recognition of wrongness may bear the imprint of a community’s fear of departure.

A moral name can alter the experience it names

Section 38 shifts from custom’s authority to the formation of feelings. Nietzsche proposes that the same impulse can become painful cowardice under one judgment and pleasurable humility under another. Approval and condemnation enter how the impulse is experienced. Book I, §38

Imagine hesitating to speak in a meeting. In one setting, hesitation is treated as a disgraceful failure of courage. In another, restraint before speaking is praised as modesty. The bodily hesitation could acquire shame in the first setting and satisfaction in the second.

This is our illustration, not proof that the two cases have an identical underlying impulse. Nietzsche’s stronger claim is that impulses do not intrinsically possess the moral names and definite accompanying approval or displeasure that moral interpretation gives them.

He describes these acquired features as a second nature. That phrase matters: a learned judgment need not remain a sentence someone consciously repeats. It can become part of a spontaneous response, so familiar that its history disappears.

The mechanism includes association. An impulse acquires standing through its connection with other already valued impulses, or with people and beings whom a community admires or despises. Feelings enter an existing network of judgments.

Envy, hope, and anger change their standing

Nietzsche’s first historical example concerns envy. He points to Hesiod’s favorable association of one form of strife with emulation. Where competitive striving receives praise, something a later morality condemns under envy can belong to an admired pattern.

Hope provides a different example. Nietzsche contrasts Christian praise of hope with Greek suspicion that it is blind and misleading. He connects that suspicion with religious expectations that people consult seers and oracles. Hope can seem deficient where inquiry into the future is itself a duty.

The final example is sanctified anger. Nietzsche describes the authority of wrathful prophets and the image of a wrathful God within Jewish religion. An emotion can gain majesty through its association with figures already regarded as holy.

These contrasts concern the values that organize emotion, not merely vocabulary. But Nietzsche’s broad treatments of Greeks, Jews, and Christian Europeans compress differences within those traditions. They should open historical questions, not become timeless descriptions of entire peoples.

Pause here: Explain how §38 helps account for the guilty originality at the end of §9. Identify what is socially learned without assuming that the resulting feeling is insincere.

A difficulty to keep in view

If conscience has a history, what follows for its authority now?

A history of obedience can expose a gap in justification. If our only defense of a demand is that disobeying feels wrong, discovering how that feeling was trained gives us reason to investigate further. The feeling cannot simply certify itself.

Yet an inherited practice might also have defensible purposes. Reliability can matter to people who depend on one another. A community’s role in teaching it does not make that dependence imaginary, or settle whether a particular commitment should be kept.

We therefore need to distinguish the formation of a judgment from the reasons that might support it. Nietzsche’s reconstruction challenges the authority of obedience for its own sake. It does not automatically refute every reason anyone could offer for conduct that custom once commanded.

A second difficulty concerns §38’s underlying impulse. How do we establish that an impulse remains the same across different valuations? Moral education may alter what people attend to, desire, and do, as well as how they name it. Nietzsche’s contrast needs a way to identify what persists through those changes.

Keep these objections close to the passages’ force. Sincere conscience need not be an independent witness untouched by history. A convincing alternative would still have to explain how inherited authority enters felt experience.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why can customary conduct count as immoral in §9?

If someone performs it from an independent reason rather than obedience, they have departed from the traditional authority that defines morality in Nietzsche’s reconstruction. The outward act can remain familiar while its ground changes.

How do frequent and difficult obedience differ?

Frequent obedience makes many small occasions subject to the rule. Difficult obedience displays the rule’s power against strong competing interests. Both can mark moral distinction, but they encourage different practices and measures of sacrifice.

Why does an individual violation implicate the community?

Nietzsche describes a belief in supernatural punishment of the group. The community can see the transgression as evidence of its own declining discipline, and the offender can share its guilty interpretation. The process reaches beyond external punishment.

What does §38 mean by an impulse’s second nature?

Moral associations give an impulse a name, standing, and accompanying good or bad conscience. These acquired features can become habitual and feel immediate. Nietzsche proposes that judgment helps shape emotional experience rather than merely reporting it.

Does a learned origin prove that a moral demand should be rejected?

No. It can challenge an appeal to the feeling’s self-evident authority. Whether the demand has other defensible grounds remains a further question, as does the evidence for the historical explanation itself.

What should I write in my notes?

Return to the rule and persistent feeling you identified in Reading 1. Write 180–250 words:

  1. Describe one occasion when the rule guided you, including how obedience or departure felt.
  2. Distinguish following it because it was expected from following it for a reason you could examine.
  3. Use §9 to suggest how its authority may have been reinforced. Separate remembered events from conjecture.
  4. Use §38 to identify an impulse whose emotional character the rule may have shaped.
  5. Give one reason to retain, revise, or reject the demand that goes beyond its familiar feel.

A successful response connects historical explanation with present judgment while keeping their tasks distinct.
