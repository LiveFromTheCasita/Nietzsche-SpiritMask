How Did We Become Able to Promise?

On the Genealogy of Morals — Essential Reading Path · Reading 5 of 12

Read: Essay II §§1–7. Stop before §8.

Before you read

You promise to return something next week. Between speaking and acting, you will encounter distractions, new wishes, and changing circumstances. Keeping the promise requires more than remembering the sentence. You must continue to treat it as something you will do.

This ordinary example is ours. It introduces Nietzsche’s distinction between retaining an impression and sustaining a will through time. He asks how an animal that needs to forget became capable of binding itself to a future action.

Reading 4 examined the wishes concealed within promises of ultimate justice. The Second Essay turns to the capacity to promise, then to the relations through which owing, obligation, and punishment become connected.

The sequence is important. II §§1–3 investigate responsibility and memory. Sections 4–7 introduce debt, cruelty, and suffering’s possible meanings. They begin the inquiry into bad conscience; they do not yet give its complete explanation.

The main question: What historical work makes a person capable of a promise, and what costs disappear when we treat responsibility as an obvious human possession?

Three things to notice

  1. Two active capacities. In II §1, forgetting protects the possibility of a present, while the memory of willing sustains selected commitments. Neither is simply a passive failure to remember or forget.
  2. A fruit and its conditions. II §§2–3 place the sovereign individual at the end of a long formation through custom and violence. Distinguish the capacity described from the history said to have produced it.
  3. The puzzling equivalence. II §§4–7 ask how another person’s pain could repay a loss. Follow the roles of pleasure, rank, public spectacle, and divine spectators.

A way to read the passages

II §1: forgetting makes room for living

Nietzsche begins with the paradox of producing an animal able to promise. Forgetting stands in the way of that achievement, yet he immediately praises it. Active forgetting closes consciousness against the constant return of what has been taken in, allowing repose, anticipation, and new activity. Return to II §1

The digestive imagery presents forgetting as an organizing capacity. A person who cannot put anything aside resembles someone unable to digest what they consume. The account concerns the use of a past without having every part of it continually occupying the present; it is not a claim that every lapse of memory is beneficial.

Promising requires a selective counterforce. A memory of the will actively continues something once willed. New experiences and wishes can intervene without breaking the connection between the undertaking and its eventual performance.

In our opening example, you might remember the borrowed object perfectly while deciding that returning it no longer matters. Conversely, keeping the commitment may involve arranging circumstances so it survives periods when it is not consciously recalled. The distinction concerns continued willing, not uninterrupted rehearsal.

Nietzsche lists the supporting capacities: anticipating events, distinguishing causes and accidents, relating means to ends, and calculating. The promiser must become sufficiently reliable to offer themselves as a guarantee about a future.

II §2: custom produces an unusual independence

The next section places this reliability within a long history of making people regular and predictable. The morality of custom imposes discipline through collective expectations. Nietzsche provocatively calls its eventual achievement a justification of the process despite its coercion and stupidity. That evaluative claim should not disappear inside a neutral account of development. Return to II §2

The ripe fruit is the sovereign individual, able to make promises through a long, independent will. This person has become free of the customary morality that helped make the capacity possible. The means produce something that exceeds their own rule.

When Nietzsche opposes autonomy to morality here, morality concerns obedience to inherited custom. Independence means no longer depending on that form of regulation. It does not mean simply having no commitments or standards.

The passage describes rare, measured promising: the person knows what their word can sustain. Responsibility becomes a felt power and privilege, eventually an instinct called conscience. This confident conscience must be distinguished from the guilty, self-punishing bad conscience investigated later.

The portrait is hierarchical. The sovereign honors reliable equals and despises unreliable people, with images of chastisement that retain the violence of the surrounding history. It should not be converted into an ordinary personality category for organized people, or an uncomplicated picture of equal citizens following universal moral law.

Its precise status remains interpretively difficult. Nietzsche’s language is admiring, but the exaggerated mastery over circumstances and the figure’s place within this troubled history deserve scrutiny. We can identify the capacity he describes without deciding that every feature supplies his final ideal or that every claim of free will elsewhere must mean the same thing.

II §3: the price of making something unforgettable

Nietzsche now moves backward from the fruit to the long formation of memory. He proposes that pain fixed obligations in creatures otherwise governed by immediate impulses. Sacrifice, punishment, and ascetic practices appear as techniques that made certain prohibitions impossible to ignore. Return to II §3

The catalogue of historical punishments is meant to prevent an innocent picture of reason’s emergence. Seriousness and reliability carry a past that their respectable present appearance can conceal. His examples of German penal practices also turn the inquiry against a culture proud of being thoughtful and dependable.

Ascetic discipline receives a related explanation: a limited set of ideas becomes dominant by excluding competing ideas and organizing a whole life around their retention. Nietzsche is tracing how an obligation might acquire force before someone can independently assess its justification.

The section makes sweeping historical claims, including the claim that religions are fundamentally systems of cruelty. The catalogue gives concrete examples of the coercive history Nietzsche proposes; their historical accuracy and explanatory significance still require assessment; it does not establish that every form of memory or every religious practice had exactly this origin.

His concluding emphasis on the cost of good things keeps explanation and assessment under pressure. Showing that a capacity emerged through violence does not automatically make the capacity worthless. Nor does admiring the capacity establish that the violence was necessary or justified. Nietzsche’s own language sometimes moves toward that justification, so readers must keep the inference visible.

II §4: guilt enters through owing

Nietzsche opens the next stage by asking about bad conscience, then accusing other genealogists of projecting their short modern experience into the past. His alternative locates the ancestry of moral guilt in material indebtedness. The German connection is between Schuld, guilt or debt, and Schulden, debts; the free translation presents an English contrast between ought and owe. Return to II §4

The linguistic connection helps formulate a historical hypothesis. It cannot alone establish that every form of guilt developed through one kind of transaction. The proposed history needs evidence about practices as well as words.

Nietzsche also separates punishment’s early development from the judgment that an offender deserves it because they freely could have acted otherwise. He presents distinctions among intention, accident, negligence, and responsibility as later achievements, not assumptions present from the beginning.

In his account, anger at injury is shaped by a belief that an equivalent payment can settle it. The decisive question is how pain enters the calculation. Why should hurting the person who caused a loss count as returning anything to the injured party? Calling it revenge merely gives that puzzle another name.

II §5: a claim on the debtor becomes a pleasure in power

The creditor–debtor relation supplies Nietzsche’s proposed answer. Promises of repayment require guarantees, and the debtor pledges things over which they claim power. The examples include freedom, bodily integrity, and even a wife. That last example exposes the patriarchal hierarchy within the arrangements he describes; another person is treated as something available to pledge. Return to II §5

The creditor’s compensation can take the form of pleasure in exercising power over someone powerless. It does not restore the missing object or money. Instead, a different satisfaction is accepted as an equivalent for the loss.

Rank intensifies the explanation. A creditor of low standing temporarily participates in privileges otherwise reserved for masters. Punishment offers the experience of looking down on another person. Even when an authority carries it out, the creditor can enjoy the spectacle.

This makes the history more specific than a general claim that people are angry when injured. It asks why a social arrangement would recognize an entitlement to enjoy domination as a payment.

Nietzsche’s praise of a Roman provision as comparatively progressive also shows how unsettled his standard of progress can be. Greater freedom from precise bodily accounting remains inside an appallingly violent arrangement. His assessment should not pass into ours simply because it is presented as a historical comparison.

II §6: cruelty can be socially approved pleasure

Nietzsche extends the contractual account to guilt, duty, and the sanctity of obligation. He even detects cruelty within Kant’s demanding moral language. The suggestion is that a respectable inward demand may preserve something from coercive relations while changing its expression. Return to II §6

He calls the reconstruction conjectural. That acknowledgment should qualify how confidently we repeat it, even though his surrounding rhetoric is emphatic. His central explanatory problem remains why inflicted suffering can be satisfying.

Public festivals, royal celebrations, and aristocratic households become evidence of cruelty enjoyed without the shame a modern observer might expect. Punishment could belong to a feast. The beneficiary’s pleasure was not necessarily a secret exception to the community’s values; the community could openly approve it.

The discussion of Don Quixote adds a history of reception. Nietzsche contrasts an audience laughing at the protagonist’s humiliation with later readers feeling pain at the scene. That contrast is part of his proposed change in sensibility, not a complete survey of what every reader in either period experienced.

He also suggests that cruelty can become more inward, imaginative, and respectable. The disappearance of one public practice would not by itself establish the disappearance of every pleasure it organized. That is a hypothesis to investigate without treating all contemporary enjoyment as disguised cruelty.

II §7: changing the meaning of suffering

Nietzsche refuses to make this violent history another argument that life is simply bad. He claims that people unashamed of cruelty could experience life more brightly than people disgusted by their own animal existence. The contrast challenges the assumption that moral refinement necessarily produces greater satisfaction with living. Return to II §7

This argument includes a serious failure of judgment. Nietzsche uses racist claims about Black people’s pain, treats them as representatives of prehistoric humanity, and adds gendered speculation about a cultivated woman’s suffering. He also dismisses animal suffering through a sweeping comparison. These claims supply no adequate evidence for the hierarchy of sensitivity he asserts. They should not support the historical argument by passing as physiological knowledge.

The further claim is that suffering becomes harder to accept when it lacks a meaning. Christianity interprets it within salvation; Nietzsche’s ancient spectators find meaning in the pleasure of watching or inflicting it. These are explanations of how suffering was made intelligible and tolerable within a worldview, not demonstrations that the suffering was justified.

Imagined divine spectators extend that logic to pain no human witnesses. Nothing need be pointless or unseen if gods watch even hidden suffering. Life thereby acquires a way to justify what happens within it.

Nietzsche carries the account through Homer’s gods, dramatic spectacle, and philosophers’ moral heroes performing under a divine gaze. He even conjectures that free will could make human action more interesting to gods by keeping events unpredictable. This is a speculative explanation of a doctrine’s appeal, not proof of its origin or falsity.

The section ends by returning to publicity and festivity. We have moved from a person who can answer for a future action to communities and imagined spectators for whom punishment carries pleasure and meaning. The history of responsibility now includes the unequal arrangements through which that responsibility was exacted.

A difficulty to keep in view

Does a disturbing history explain the whole value of a present capacity?

A dependable promise can make cooperation, care, and trust possible even if the history of producing dependable people included coercion. Conversely, those benefits cannot by themselves establish that coercion was indispensable or deserved approval. The origin of a capacity, its present uses, and the justification of its conditions are separate questions.

Nietzsche’s claim about pain and memory needs the same discipline. Describing punishments and identifying their intended effects does not establish all their actual effects. Violence might produce compliance, fear, concealment, resistance, or several together. A genealogy needs evidence about the connections it proposes.

There is also tension between the sovereign individual’s expansive mastery and the long history of forces that made such a person possible. Calling responsibility an achievement need not mean imagining an agent without a history. But the exceptional figure’s exact relation to Nietzsche’s wider criticism of free will cannot be settled by celebrating one paragraph.

Finally, meaning is not sufficient justification. A spectator can find an event absorbing while the person suffering has excellent grounds to oppose it. Ask whose satisfaction organizes the explanation. That question brings the hierarchy within Nietzsche’s historical scenes into view without assuming that historical understanding requires us to accept the hierarchy.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

How do active forgetting and a memory of the will differ?

Active forgetting makes room for present life by keeping absorbed experiences from continually occupying consciousness. A memory of the will selectively sustains an undertaking across intervening experiences and wishes. Promising requires this continued commitment, not merely remembering that certain words were spoken.

Why is the sovereign individual more than someone who follows custom reliably?

The figure can bind themselves through an independent, enduring will after becoming free of customary morality. Custom is presented as a historical condition of that capacity. The portrait includes hierarchy and contempt, and its precise status should not be reduced to a familiar personality type or a simple doctrine of universal moral autonomy.

What does the history of painful memory establish about responsibility’s value?

It proposes conditions through which people became answerable for future conduct and exposes their violence. The value of the resulting capacity and the justification of those conditions require further arguments. Nietzsche’s own tendency to justify the process through its fruit remains contestable.

How can suffering count as repayment in II §§4–6?

Nietzsche proposes that the creditor receives pleasure in exercising or witnessing power over the debtor. That satisfaction substitutes for direct restitution and can confer a temporary privilege of rank. The account is a historical conjecture; the linguistic link between guilt and debt alone cannot prove it.

What do divine spectators contribute to II §7’s explanation?

They give even hidden suffering a witness and a place within an intelligible spectacle. Nietzsche contrasts such meanings with suffering experienced as pointless. A meaning that satisfies a spectator does not establish justification for the sufferer, and the section’s racial and gendered assertions do not provide sound evidence.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one ordinary promise, real or invented. Write 200–300 words:

  1. Explain what must continue between making the promise and fulfilling it. Distinguish remembered words from a memory of willing.
  2. Identify circumstances or practices that help make the promiser dependable. State what you know and what you are inferring.
  3. Describe a possible response to failure. Distinguish repairing the loss from making the promiser suffer.
  4. Using II §§4–7, explain what satisfaction someone might seek through the second response, and whose standpoint gives it meaning.
  5. Give a reason to value dependable promising while identifying something about Nietzsche’s proposed history that still needs evidence.

A strong response connects the capacity to promise with the puzzle of repayment, while keeping historical explanation distinct from approval of coercion.
