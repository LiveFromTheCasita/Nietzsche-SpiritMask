How Does Cruelty Turn Against the Self?

On the Genealogy of Morals — Essential Reading Path · Reading 7 of 12

Read: Essay II §§16–25. Read to the end of the Second Essay, stopping before the Third Essay.
Allow: About 110–140 minutes, including the assigned text, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. You can divide the lesson across two sittings.

Before you read

Imagine a person whose accustomed opportunities for competition and confrontation suddenly disappear. Their desire to overcome resistance remains. Could that energy turn toward disciplining, judging, and punishing themselves?

This is a small analogy for Nietzsche's hypothesis, not evidence for his history or a diagnosis of any particular person. Essay II now traces bad conscience to a violent transformation of human life, then asks how that capacity for inward cruelty joins an expanding sense of religious debt.

Reading 6 distinguished the practical effects of punishment from its supposed creation of remorse. The present assignment offers a different account. Social constraints prevent certain actions; the drives that once produced them seek another outlet.

Keep three stages distinct: the inward turn of drives, indebtedness to ancestors and gods, and their later combination into guilt that seems impossible to discharge. Reading them as one event would erase the essay's most important transitions.

The main question: How can the same capacity for giving form become self-torment, create ideals, and acquire an apparently unpayable debt?

Three things to notice

  1. The location of the force. In §§16–18, what happens to drives whose outward expression is blocked? Distinguish the conquerors who impose an order from those forced to live within it.
  2. The correction between §§20 and 21. Why does the apparent promise of innocence through atheism require revision once debt has been moralized?
  3. The alternative use of an ideal. Follow the Greek gods, the proposed reversal of bad conscience, and the final appeal to Zarathustra. What is imagined, and what has actually been established?

A way to read the passages

II §16: blocked drives acquire an inward field

Nietzsche introduces his account as a provisional hypothesis. Human beings formerly adapted to roaming, conflict, and adventure find themselves confined within organized social peace. Their familiar instincts no longer provide an acceptable guide to action, but their demands continue. II §16

He calls the resulting inward movement internalization. Hostility, cruelty, and pleasure in overcoming resistance turn against their own bearer. The person becomes something they can pursue, shape, hurt, and oppose.

This process also enlarges the inner world. Nietzsche does not begin with a complete, timeless soul waiting behind behavior. He proposes that the depth later attributed to the soul develops through the obstruction of outward activity.

The account explains why punishment can matter without directly teaching remorse. In the previous reading, a punished person might simply learn caution. Here punishment belongs to the wider barriers that restrict action and redirect drives. The immediate lesson and the proposed long transformation are different mechanisms.

Nietzsche calls this development an illness while also describing humanity as a new possibility and promise. The inward turn damages the animal and makes it capable of something unprecedented. His evaluation is ambivalent from the beginning.

II §17: the enclosure is imposed by conquest

The next section specifies that the change is neither gradual accommodation nor voluntary agreement. Organized conquerors force a population into an enduring form. The earliest state appears as violent machinery, and Nietzsche dismisses the contractual origin of political order. II §17

His conquerors act like artists imposing form on material. They partition functions and organize a whole without experiencing guilt about their work. Bad conscience arises in those whose freedom they suppress, rather than in the conquerors themselves.

This distinction prevents a misleading picture in which guilty rulers invent institutions to express their remorse. The rulers' force creates conditions under which the subdued population's drives become inwardly active.

The language of natural masters and predatory conquerors carries an explicit hierarchy. Calling people formless material discounts their existing practices and purposes. The artistic comparison can make violent subjection appear admirable by measuring it through the organizer's achievement.

A speculative reconstruction is not enough to dispose of every account of political association. Nietzsche would need evidence for the populations, transformations, and psychological effects he describes. Recognizing the hypothesis's force does not require treating conquest as either a universal fact of origins or a justified method of creation.

II §18: cruelty becomes creative within the self

Nietzsche identifies the force operating inwardly with the active, formative force that organizes other people: the instinct of freedom, which he names will to power. What changes is its material. The person now works upon their own animal nature. II §18

This matters after Essay I's account of ressentiment. Internalization here is not simply another name for reactive revenge, and it is not yet the ascetic priest's later redirection of blame. Nietzsche describes an active power making the self its field of transformation.

This fuller mechanism also complicates §11's earlier attribution of bad conscience to the resentful person. Follow the account of active self-formation rather than assuming that a label has already explained its origin.

The process includes pleasure in imposing standards, enduring resistance, and making oneself suffer. It can also produce imagination, beauty, and affirmation. The capacity that makes self-torment possible can give rise to ideals that would not otherwise exist.

Consider an imagined artist who enjoys ruthlessly revising an unsatisfactory work and also begins treating their entire person as defective material. The example helps distinguish productive formation from a pleasure in self-condemnation. It does not prove that every artistic discipline originates in cruelty.

Nietzsche's own claim is stronger: he makes bad conscience a condition for valuing selflessness and interprets delight in self-sacrifice as a form of cruelty. Mixed or demanding motives need investigation, but identifying them does not automatically establish that an act's benefits are unreal.

II §19: the debt to ancestors keeps growing

Nietzsche compares the illness with pregnancy, emphasizing what it may bring forth. He then temporarily returns to a different strand of the essay: the creditor–debtor relation. Living generations interpret their existence and prosperity as benefits received from ancestors, requiring repayment through sacrifice, observance, and obedience. II §19

In his reconstruction, the debt is more than gratitude. Ancestors continue to provide benefits as powerful spirits, so the obligation renews itself. Greater success makes their supposed power and the amount owed seem greater.

The account reverses an easy expectation that fear increases only when a group weakens. Here victory can intensify fear of the creditor, because victory is taken as evidence of the creditor's extraordinary assistance. The most powerful ancestors eventually acquire divine stature.

This is Nietzsche's proposed route from ancestral obligation to gods, including violent sacrifices meant to settle the account. It needs comparative historical evidence. The fact that a society honors ancestors would not by itself establish this particular fear-driven mechanism.

II §20: religious debt survives changes of political order

The dissolution of a clan does not, in Nietzsche's account, erase its inherited obligations. Subject populations take over their rulers' religions; conquests and political combinations reshape divine genealogies. Expanding powers are accompanied by more encompassing gods. II §20

Christianity's supreme God then corresponds to the greatest consciousness of indebtedness. The conclusion appears to follow that declining belief will diminish the debt, and eventual atheism could restore a kind of innocence.

Pause at that apparent conclusion. If obligation depended only on belief in an external creditor, removing the belief might remove its basis. But the next section explains that something has deliberately been left out. We have not yet accounted for the entanglement of debt with bad conscience.

Nietzsche's large claims about political unification and monotheism should also remain hypotheses to test. They are components of his explanatory story, not historical laws demonstrated in the passage.

II §21: moralized debt no longer has a payable amount

Nietzsche now corrects the simplification. Once debt becomes intertwined with bad conscience, it can turn against the debtor's very existence. What was potentially repayable becomes guilt that no payment can discharge, and punishment becomes eternal. II §21

The accusation can also turn toward the source of life: an original ancestor, nature, or existence itself. The debtor's condition seems fundamentally wrong because the origin from which they came has become tainted.

Imagine the difference between owing a specified service and believing that one's basic nature makes adequate repayment impossible. The first obligation has a possible endpoint. The second can survive every attempt to meet it, because the person who tries remains condemned.

This explains why disbelief does not automatically remove the acquired form of self-judgment. The thought has been worked into bad conscience; eliminating an explicit religious premise may leave its pattern of accusation active.

Nietzsche then presents Christianity's paradoxical answer: the divine creditor sacrifices himself to pay the debtor's debt. He calls it an extraordinary invention offering temporary relief, while his incredulous tone keeps its promise of love under scrutiny. This is his hostile interpretation of salvation, not an uncontested account of every Christian's experience.

II §22: the ideal supplies an instrument of self-torture

The next section brings the strands together. The person whose cruelty has turned inward uses the religious hypothesis to intensify it. God becomes the perfect opposite of their ineradicable animal drives, which are reinterpreted as rebellion against divine holiness. II §22

Because ordinary human nature cannot cease being what it is, the contrast generates an apparently inexhaustible reason for condemnation. Punishment cannot clear the debt; the structure continually confirms the person's unworthiness.

Nietzsche depicts a will actively seeking conditions of inescapable guilt. This is more than discovering an external authority that happens to be demanding. The sufferer's own formative power participates in constructing the ideal through which suffering is renewed.

His account helps explain a possible persistence of self-accusation. It does not establish that religious love is always disguised cruelty. Nor does his language of illness supply a medical diagnosis. The claims concern his interpretation of a historical and evaluative configuration.

II §23: gods can also protect people from guilt

The Greek contrast prevents the argument from becoming a simple equation between belief in gods and self-condemnation. Nietzsche depicts Greek gods as affirming noble human qualities and absorbing responsibility for actions that might otherwise destroy aristocratic self-respect. II §23

In his reading, misconduct can be explained through folly or divine bewilderment rather than sin. Zeus may criticize mortals' imprudence, but the relation does not become the Christian opposition between animal humanity and absolute holiness.

The culminating distinction is between taking on punishment and taking on guilt. In Nietzsche's portrayal, the Greek gods help remove the imputation of guilt itself. Christianity's sacrificial solution operates within a debt whose extremity it acknowledges.

This is a selective, admiring picture of aristocratic Greeks. Their ability to excuse a peer's misconduct raises a question about the people injured by it. Freedom from self-torment does not by itself establish an adequate recognition of harm.

The methodological gain is nevertheless clear: an ideal's psychological use varies. The presence of gods alone cannot explain what a particular religious arrangement does.

II §§24–25: a counterideal is announced, then deferred

Nietzsche asks what it costs to erect an ideal. The destruction of previous sanctities, distortions, and injuries to conscience enter his account. He then imagines reversing the established direction of accusation: attaching bad conscience to ideals hostile to earthly life instead of to animal nature. II §24

This is not a proposal simply to stop judging oneself or follow every impulse. It redirects a severe capacity for judgment and demands a different kind of creator, trained by danger, conflict, difficult conditions, and what Nietzsche calls great health.

His hoped-for figure would free reality from the condemnation imposed by otherworldly ideals and overcome the will to nothingness. The language remains hierarchical and martial. Its admiration for conquest and exceptional strength needs assessment alongside the promise of liberation.

There is also a structural difficulty: if the new ideal redirects bad conscience, how will it avoid reproducing destructive self-division? The proposed reversal does not by itself explain how severity becomes something other than renewed torment.

In §25 Nietzsche interrupts himself and reserves the task for someone younger, stronger, and more future-oriented: Zarathustra. This gesture of modesty is also a literary presentation of his own created figure. It marks the limits of the essay's completed account while sustaining its prophetic expectation. II §25

The ending gives a direction and a demand, rather than a demonstrated remedy. Bad conscience has become intelligible through several proposed transformations; overcoming its most destructive form remains unfinished work.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can a history that finds creativity in violence adequately judge the suffering involved?

Nietzsche refuses an uncomplicated celebration of inwardness. He traces depth and ideal-making through constraint, division, and cruelty. Yet calling these forces creative can also make the people subjected to them appear as material for an achievement valued by someone else.

The account of conquest is a test case. Even if imposed order contributed to new psychological capacities, that would not prove the conquest justified or necessary. We would need evidence for the causal claim and a separate argument concerning the harms.

The religious comparisons require the same discipline. A severe ideal may intensify guilt, while another may lessen it. The Greek aristocrat's relief could still excuse conduct that deserves criticism, and a believer's concern for others need not be reduced to self-torture.

Finally, Nietzsche presents some connections as unavoidable after introducing the origin story as a hypothesis. A compelling sequence can conceal gaps: blocked drives need not always take the form described, and ancestral obligation need not develop into limitless guilt. Counterexamples would test the proposed necessity.

The reading's strongest distinction remains useful even where the history is contested. Acquiring an inward life, owing something, and condemning one's existence are different developments. Explaining their conjunction is more demanding than assigning every form of guilt one cause.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

How can punishment matter here without directly creating remorse?

It helps enforce the social barriers that block outward activity. Nietzsche proposes that the still-active drives turn inward under these broader conditions. That process differs from a punished person immediately learning that their deed was morally wrong.

In whom does bad conscience begin in §§17–18?

In those whose freedom is constrained, rather than in the conquerors, whom Nietzsche portrays as free of felt guilt. The inward force is nevertheless described as active and formative. It should not be identified without distinction with ressentiment or the priest's later redirection of it.

Why is bad conscience both illness and possibility?

It enables people to persecute themselves while also expanding their inward capacities and producing ideals, imagination, and beauty. Nietzsche's comparison with pregnancy emphasizes what may emerge from it. This ambivalence does not establish that the suffering was justified.

Why must §20's promise of innocence be read through §21?

Section 20 temporarily treats debt as depending on belief in a divine creditor. Section 21 adds its moralization and entanglement with bad conscience. Once guilt structures self-judgment, rejecting the creditor's existence does not automatically eliminate that structure.

What do the Greek gods and final counterideal add?

The Greek comparison shows that gods can be used to relieve guilt rather than intensify it. The counterideal imagines redirecting judgment against hostility to life. Nietzsche leaves its achievement to a future creator, invoking Zarathustra instead of supplying a completed remedy.

What should I write in my notes?

Write 200–300 words reconstructing the argument without collapsing its stages:

  1. Explain the inward turn in §§16–18, distinguishing the imposed social order from the drives redirected within it.
  2. Describe how debt to ancestors and gods becomes different from ordinary gratitude.
  3. Identify what changes when debt is moralized and becomes impossible to discharge.
  4. Use the Greek contrast to show why a religious ideal need not have one psychological effect.
  5. End with an evidential or ethical objection to the history, or with a difficulty facing the proposed counterideal.

Distinguish Nietzsche's explanation from your evaluation of it. A successful response can recognize creative consequences without treating violence as justified, and can explain guilt without assuming that all responsibility should disappear.
