Can a Teacher Make You Free?

Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Essential Reading Path · Reading 4 of 10

Read: Part I, “The Way of the Creating One” in full, stopping before “Old and Young Women”; then “The Bestowing Virtue,” internal §3 only, ending at the close of Part I. Its §§1–2 are outside this assignment.
Allow: About 55–75 minutes, including the passages, this guide, rereading, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann in The Portable Nietzsche. Free alternative: The Way of the Creating One · The Bestowing Virtue, §3 (Thomas Common, Thus Spake Zarathustra).

Chapter names follow Common’s edition; see the edition guide for alternate titles. Its Roman numerals I–LXXX number chapters across the book, while the assignment above specifies parts and internal sections. If highlighting fails, locate the stated chapter and section. Before you read

A teacher gives someone a language for questioning the expectations they inherited. The student becomes more confident, makes difficult changes, and learns to defend their judgments. Then a question arises: can they disagree with the teacher who made those changes possible?

If disagreement feels like betrayal, the relationship may reproduce a dependence it seemed to overcome. But leaving the teacher does not automatically supply a defensible purpose or the strength to pursue it. Rejection can become another way of organizing a life around the person rejected.

This situation is our illustration. Zarathustra’s speeches make the problem more severe by connecting independence with solitude, creating values, and becoming answerable to a law one gives oneself.

Reading 3 examined the bodily and communal conditions of valuing. Here Zarathustra addresses someone seeking a path apart, then tells his own disciples to leave him. Read the speeches as acts within relationships. Nietzsche has written a teacher whose demands can also put his own authority in question.

The main question: What would have to change for a disciple to become independent, and can the teacher command that change without retaining control over it?

Three things to notice

  1. The purpose of separation. In “The Way of the Creating One,” distinguish escaping an authority from becoming capable of a demanding task. Why does Zarathustra distrust the desire to feel elevated?
  2. The solitude within independence. Notice the continuing voice of communal conscience, anticipated despair, and danger of becoming one’s own enemy. Leaving other people does not remove these conflicts.
  3. Departure with a promised return. In “The Bestowing Virtue,” §3, attend to the narrator’s account of Zarathustra’s hesitation, his changed voice, and his demand that disciples deny him before a reunion.

A way to read the passages

A departing person still carries the community

“The Way of the Creating One” opens with an address to someone contemplating isolation. Zarathustra asks them to wait and listen. Before authorizing departure, he describes a difficulty: the community’s condemnation of solitude will continue to speak within the person leaving it. Return to “The Way of the Creating One”

The pain of abandoning a common conscience can itself be produced by that conscience. Feeling guilty does not therefore establish that departure is wrong. Nor does recognizing the feeling’s origin make it vanish. The separation must be lived through by someone already shaped by what they are leaving.

Imagine someone choosing work their family considers unworthy. They may judge the family’s standard mistaken while still experiencing shame when asked what they do. This example is ours. It illustrates an inherited judgment continuing within a person who no longer endorses it.

Zarathustra does not treat such discomfort as sufficient evidence of a creative vocation. He demands to hear what authority and strength the departing person possesses. The speech is a test, and its speaker has not stopped occupying the position of examiner.

Feeling above others is an inadequate purpose

The images of a new movement and a self-moving wheel suggest activity that can begin something. Zarathustra contrasts that possibility with ambition which merely inflates its possessor. Grand thoughts may function like bellows, producing an appearance of magnitude while leaving the person emptier.

This criticism reaches readers tempted to identify immediately with the exceptional individual. Feeling misunderstood or disdaining conventional people does not answer the question about what one can create. A flattering image of independence might be a particularly comfortable dependence on an audience.

The distinction between freedom from and freedom for sharpens the test. What ruling commitment can organize the person’s life after the old restraint is removed? Zarathustra even suggests that some people lose their last value when they discard service. His hierarchy is severe: he does not assume that every departure is an advance.

That severity should remain visible. The speech provides a challenge to empty superiority, but it also makes freedom something for which a person must show entitlement. We should ask who could fairly apply that test, including whether Zarathustra has justified his authority to administer it.

Giving oneself a law includes answering to it

Zarathustra asks whether the solitary person can determine good and bad for themselves and make their will a law over them. The next demand is to become the judge and avenger of that law. Self-legislation here creates an obligation; it is much more demanding than treating each present preference as permission.

For an ordinary illustration, someone might commit to making truthful work even when distortion would win approval. Acting independently would then include judging their own convenient evasions. This is our limited example of answering to a commitment, not an equivalent for Zarathustra’s whole ideal of creating values.

The star entering cold, empty space gives this responsibility an emotional setting. No surrounding agreement guarantees the law’s worth or supplies reassurance when confidence fails. Zarathustra anticipates the day when the person’s elevated aspiration appears false and their own inadequacy becomes painfully close.

The speech’s violent images of destroying feelings and burning into ashes intensify the cost of transformation. They dramatize the loss of ways of being the person has loved. They should neither become literal instructions nor be quietly reduced to cheerful advice about confidence. Zarathustra’s ideal carries a disturbing willingness to undergo profound damage and loss.

The enemies outside and the enemy within

Zarathustra anticipates resentment from others who feel surpassed. He warns against those convinced of their own goodness and against a simplicity that treats unfamiliar possibilities as unholy. His images invoke persecution, making conformity potentially coercive rather than merely reassuring.

But his response also demands something from the solitary person: they must continue to shine despite injustice. Creating cannot be exhausted by the wish to punish those who fail to recognize it. The speech’s love for the departing person exceeds a shared contempt for the crowd.

Another danger comes from the solitary person’s own need for contact. They may reach too readily for anyone who approaches. Solitude can make judgment vulnerable precisely when the person imagines themselves free of dependence.

The worst enemy, Zarathustra finally says, is oneself. The path goes beyond the present self and its familiar oppositions. Love of oneself becomes connected with dissatisfaction with what one is; creation requires being able to turn against something loved. His farewell contains tears as well as demands. The speaker does not present separation as emotionally costless, even while admiring the creator who may succumb in seeking something beyond themselves.

A changed voice at the end of Part I

In “The Bestowing Virtue,” §3, the narrator pauses the teaching to describe Zarathustra handling his staff doubtfully. When he resumes, his voice has changed. This brief staging matters: the command to depart emerges through hesitation rather than arriving as an untouched formula. Return to “The Bestowing Virtue,” §3

Zarathustra now tells the disciples that he will go alone and that they must also leave, individually. He urges them to guard against him and even consider that he has deceived them. The warning places the teacher among the authorities they must learn to question.

His language about loving enemies and hating friends disrupts the assumption that loyalty always serves knowledge. A teacher is poorly repaid by a student who remains only a pupil. The wreath and statue turn admiration into something physically oppressive: reverence can grow heavy enough to crush the admirer.

This is not the narrator certifying that Zarathustra has deceived them, nor Nietzsche announcing that every teacher is fraudulent. Zarathustra asks his disciples to admit a possibility that their reverence may prevent them from examining.

Finding oneself after finding a teacher

Zarathustra’s diagnosis is that the disciples found him before seeking themselves. Belief supplied an answer before they had developed their own question. Their allegiance can therefore conceal an unfinished task.

Imagine a student who can repeat a mentor’s judgment but cannot identify what would count against it. The borrowed conclusion may even be defensible, yet the student has not acquired an independent relation to it. This is our example of the difference between possessing a teaching and being able to examine it.

Zarathustra’s demand goes beyond encouraging polite questions. He asks to be lost and denied. Independence must threaten his place in the disciple’s life, not merely add an approved exercise to the relationship.

Yet he continues to speak imperatively. If disciples leave because their teacher orders it, their departure could still express obedience. The scene stages this paradox without supplying a procedure that guarantees a successful escape from it.

The reunion keeps a common future in view

After demanding denial, Zarathustra promises to return with changed eyes and another love. The desired relation is friendship, joined to a shared hope, rather than permanent isolation. Departure might transform a bond instead of simply severing it.

The closing image of the great noon places this hope within the passage from present humanity toward the overman. Humanity is pictured midway through a course whose decline prepares another morning. Zarathustra’s final wish combines the gods’ death with the desired life of that future possibility.

That ending preserves an unresolved question. Are the former disciples free to reject this hope too, or does Zarathustra imagine their independence culminating in a transformed agreement with him? He promises to love differently, but the future he announces still bears the direction of his teaching. The text gives us the promise and the tension together.

Optional extension: a friend who can resist you

Optional, beyond this session’s required assignment: Read Part I, “The Friend.” It adds a useful complication to the solitude of these speeches. A friend enters the solitary person’s conversation with themselves and can prevent it from sinking into its own depths.

Zarathustra values the friend who can resist us and awaken a longing beyond our present condition. Friendship need not mean constant agreement, complete exposure, or pity offered without considering whether it is wanted. He also imagines someone unable to free themselves nevertheless helping free a friend. Independence can depend on assistance.

The chapter then explicitly excludes women from present capacity for friendship, linking them with servitude and tyranny and using degrading animal comparisons. These are sweeping, unsupported judgments that restrict his ideal. The word “yet” leaves a possible future open but does not undo the exclusion. His subsequent challenge to men’s capacities likewise does not cancel it.

Ask both questions together: what could friendship make possible, and what justifies denying that possibility to a whole group? The chapter’s demand for resistance should apply to its own speaker’s claims.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can an invitation to independence become another test of loyalty?

A teacher might celebrate students’ disagreement only when it takes the form the teacher expects. Departure could then be treated as a stage in the teacher’s story, with reunion providing its approved conclusion. Zarathustra’s demand to deny him unsettles devotion, but his promised shared future keeps this possibility alive.

The first speech adds a related difficulty. If opposition from others is taken as proof that the creator has surpassed them, criticism can become impossible to hear. Yet a person’s loneliness does not establish the merit of their project. Others might recognize a flaw, and a supportive relationship might strengthen rather than diminish independence.

Zarathustra partly guards against easy self-exaltation by attacking ambition and making the creator answerable to a demanding law. Even so, the law’s self-given character cannot alone establish its worth. A person can bind themselves intensely to a destructive commitment.

Keep the achievements and the costs distinct. These speeches expose dependence hidden in admiration and rebellion. They also present a severe ideal of solitude and transformation whose authority remains contestable. The dramatic setting permits us to question the teacher without treating every demand he makes as empty.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why can leaving a community still produce guilt?

Zarathustra says the common conscience continues to speak within the departing person. The pain can therefore be an effect of the authority being questioned. Recognizing this history does not automatically remove the feeling or establish that the departure is justified.

What does the distinction between freedom from and freedom for require?

It asks what commitment and creative capacity will organize life after a restraint is removed. Zarathustra distrusts mere ambition or superiority. He demands a purpose and the ability to answer to a self-given law, while leaving his standard of entitlement open to criticism.

Why does solitude threaten the creator from within?

The person can lose confidence, feel overwhelmed by their inadequacy, or become indiscriminate in seeking company. The present self also resists its own transformation. External opposition is only part of the ordeal; Zarathustra identifies oneself as the most dangerous enemy.

What changes when Zarathustra asks the disciples to deny him?

The teacher becomes someone whose authority must be tested. Remaining a believer would leave the disciple’s own search unfinished. Yet the departure is still commanded by the teacher, so obeying it does not automatically establish independence.

Does the departure end every relationship with Zarathustra?

No. He imagines a return with another love, friendship, and a shared hope for the overman. That promise makes transformed relationship possible, while raising the question of whether he can accept former disciples who do not return to his hoped-for future.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose a teacher, thinker, or admired example, real or invented. Write 200–300 words about what becoming independent of them could mean:

  1. Identify something their influence has made possible. Explain why simple rejection would give an incomplete account of the relationship.
  2. Distinguish a restraint you might leave behind from a commitment you would need to sustain. Use a detail from “The Way of the Creating One.”
  3. Describe one question you would need to be able to ask about the teacher’s judgment.
  4. Using “The Bestowing Virtue,” §3, explain what would distinguish an independent departure from performing the teacher’s preferred version of independence.
  5. State whether a transformed relationship remains possible, and what would test it.

A strong response acknowledges what the relationship gave while making its authority available for examination. The optional friendship chapter is not required.
