What Does the Promise of Justice Conceal?

On the Genealogy of Morals — Essential Reading Path · Reading 4 of 12

Read: Essay I §§14–17, including the closing Note. Include Nietzsche’s unnumbered Note following §17, and stop before the Second Essay.
Allow: About 90–115 minutes, including the assigned text, this guide, rereading, and a short written response. You can divide the lesson across two sittings.

Before you read

Imagine someone saying that they want justice after being treated badly. They might want acknowledgment, protection against another injury, or a fair settlement. They might also picture the pleasure of watching the offender humiliated. Those possibilities can coexist, and the admirable word does not tell us how they are related.

This example is ours. It introduces Nietzsche’s question about the wishes carried by moral language. He asks whether declaring an outcome just can transform the apparent character of the desire for it.

Reading 3 followed the account of ressentiment and the conversion of powerlessness into a position of moral superiority. Here the First Essay stages that conversion in an imagined workshop, examines promises of heavenly judgment, and offers a sweeping interpretation of European history.

Read through the Note after I §17. Nietzsche ends by proposing further research into the history and value of morals. The Note helps us assess the relation between the inquiry he calls for and the confidence of the history he has just narrated.

The main question: When a demand calls itself justice, what must we examine about its desires, its historical interpretation, and the standard by which it values its hoped-for outcome?

Three things to notice

  1. Different speakers in the workshop. In I §14, Nietzsche stages an exchange with an imagined observer. Watch how commands to look and listen shape the report. This is a polemical scene, not an independently recorded interview.
  2. What makes the promised happiness happy. In I §15, ask what role the suffering of others plays. Distinguish Nietzsche’s commentary from the Christian authorities he quotes.
  3. History followed by a research program. I §§16–17 present a contest between valuations; the closing Note asks for historical and physiological investigation. What evidence and evaluative decisions would that program require?

A way to read the passages

I §14: entering a workshop designed by the critic

Nietzsche invites a curious observer into a place where ideals are manufactured. The underground setting, uncertain light, whispers, and bad smells establish suspicion before an argument is completed. The reader is being led into a scene whose atmosphere already gives a verdict. Return to I §14

The visitor initially hears more than he sees. He reports the transformations Nietzsche’s previous analysis has prepared us to recognize: weakness becomes merit, inability to retaliate becomes goodness, submission becomes obedience, and compulsory waiting becomes patience.

The crucial operation concerns the description of an action’s possibility. Being unable to avenge oneself is represented as choosing not to do so. Something constrained becomes evidence of freely exercised virtue. The account depends on the distinction examined in the previous reading between what an agent can do and what they are praised for declining to do.

For an ordinary illustration, someone excluded from a competition might describe their absence as refusal to seek vulgar recognition. The description could be sincere, dishonest, or partly both. To judge it, we would need to know whether refusal preceded exclusion, whether they still wanted to compete, and what they actually valued. This is our example, not historical evidence for Nietzsche’s whole account.

The workshop turns that possible reinterpretation into a coordinated practice. Its speakers supply fine names that make necessity appear commendable. The imagined observer agrees with his guide, and the guide repeatedly presses him onward. Their agreement should not be mistaken for two independent sources confirming the history.

From present suffering to a superior future

The next transformation gives misery a divine explanation. It becomes a distinction, a trial, or preparation for a compensation that will exceed what was suffered. The disadvantaged can then claim both present superiority and a better future.

Nietzsche’s sharpest question concerns revenge. The workshop does not describe itself as waiting for a chance to hurt enemies. It hopes for the triumph of righteousness over godlessness. Hatred takes an impersonal object, and fellow sufferers become a community of love.

This is more than accusing someone of using a polite euphemism. Nietzsche suggests that the interpretation organizes feeling and expectation. A person can inhabit a moral world in which the anticipated defeat of their enemy appears as the necessary vindication of goodness itself.

The final judgment completes the transformation. Present powerlessness becomes temporary; the future kingdom promises reversal. Faith, love, and hope are therefore followed into their objects: what precisely is believed, loved, and awaited?

The question does not establish that every hope for justice conceals the same desire. A demand to prevent repeated injury would need to be examined in its own terms. Nietzsche’s scene presses us to investigate what an admirable description may conceal, while its sweeping identification of the speakers with ressentiment remains a claim requiring support.

I §15: whose voice describes heavenly pleasure?

The next section asks what the promised reversal would satisfy. Nietzsche provocatively replaces a picture of eternal love with a picture of hatred receiving endless compensation. The afterlife becomes a setting in which earthly weakness can finally enjoy the defeat of its opponents. Return to I §15

He then calls witnesses. The brief passage attributed to Thomas Aquinas says that the blessed contemplate the punishment of the damned in a way that increases their enjoyment of blessedness. Nietzsche presents this as evidence that another person’s suffering belongs within the promised satisfaction.

The long quotation that follows is from Tertullian. Its speaker rejects worldly spectacles while anticipating the vastly greater spectacle of final judgment. Rulers, persecutors, philosophers, poets, and performers are imagined suffering, and the observer’s delight intensifies as the catalogue continues.

The contrast matters. A denunciation of cruel public entertainment may preserve the attraction of spectacle while relocating it to a divine setting. Nietzsche reads the religious promise as offering a more comprehensive version of what it appears to renounce.

In the free edition, much of Tertullian’s passage remains in Latin. Follow its dramatic structure: the coming judgment, the succession of condemned groups, and the anticipated pleasure of witnessing them. Nietzsche adds comments within the quotation, including an identification of its later targets with Jews accused of abusing Christ. These changes of voice must remain visible.

Nietzsche is exposing and ridiculing these expectations, not himself promising their heavenly fulfillment. But his selection and interpretation of the quotations also need assessment. They establish that such images can occur in authoritative Christian writing; alone, they do not establish the motives of every Christian or exhaust Christian accounts of justice.

I §16: two valuations become a historical drama

Nietzsche now summarizes a long conflict between the noble distinction of good and bad and the moral opposition of good and evil. He also locates the conflict within individuals: a complicated person can become a battleground for both orientations. The history does not merely sort every person into one stable camp. Return to I §16

Rome and Judea become the principal symbols of this struggle. Rome represents aristocratic self-affirmation; Judea represents the priestly achievement of reversing the valuation. Christianity’s success, including the reverence paid to its central figures in Rome itself, becomes evidence of Judea’s victory.

The symbolic organization does not excuse the passage’s claims about actual peoples. Nietzsche characterizes Jews collectively through ressentiment and conditionally endorses Rome’s accusation against them by appealing to the supremacy of aristocratic values. This is an anti-Jewish generalization embedded in his argument, not a neutral report of two ancient positions.

The narrative then moves through the Renaissance, Reformation, French Revolution, and Napoleon. The Renaissance appears as a revival of classical valuation. The Reformation restores the popular, resentful movement; the French Revolution defeats aristocracy more decisively through the claims of the many.

These events acquire their meaning through Nietzsche’s opposition of valuations. The account compresses different conflicts, institutions, and participants into one recurring drama. Its force as an interpretation cannot substitute for a historical demonstration that ressentiment adequately explains those changes.

Napoleon leaves an unresolved problem

Napoleon interrupts the apparent victory of equality. Nietzsche presents him as a startling return of the privilege of the few and as an incarnation of the aristocratic ideal’s problem. The description combines monstrosity with the figure of the overman.

The combination matters. Napoleon is neither an uncomplicated solution nor an incidental name in a neutral chronology. He concentrates Nietzsche’s attraction to exceptional power together with the difficulty of what that power can mean.

Likewise, the attack on equalization is not simply a criticism of hypocrisy. Nietzsche ranks possible human futures and associates the claims of the majority with decline. A reader who values equal standing must confront that disagreement directly, rather than assume the essay merely recommends more honest motives for familiar political commitments.

I §17: the battle remains open

The closing section asks whether the antagonism has ended or is only postponed. Nietzsche then changes the question from prediction to desire: should another confrontation be wished for and willed? The historian’s account becomes a challenge addressed to the reader’s own valuations. Return to I §17 and its closing Note

His final distinction clarifies the stakes of moving beyond good and evil. That movement does not mean going beyond every contrast between better and worse, noble and contemptible, or good and bad. Nietzsche rejects one organization of valuation while retaining the demand to rank.

The essay therefore does not end by freeing us from judgment altogether. It asks which standards should govern judgment, and what kind of human possibility those standards favor. That demand also exposes Nietzsche’s own commitments to criticism.

The Note: what inquiry still has to do

The Note calls for university research into the history of moral ideas. Philologists and historians should investigate what language, especially etymology, can disclose about their development. The request gives the essay’s verbal histories the status of a program needing further work, not a closed archive of established results.

Nietzsche also asks physiologists and physicians to investigate valuations, with philosophers facilitating the exchange. He gives physiological interpretation priority and wants moral tables assessed in relation to the forms of life they support.

His controlling question is “valuable for what?” A practice might favor endurance, adaptation, or preservation of large numbers without favoring the development of a stronger type. Benefit to the majority and benefit to a minority are presented as competing standards. He refuses to treat the first as intrinsically superior.

Those comparisons make the evaluative decision unavoidable. Evidence might show that a practice produces a certain outcome. It does not, by itself, establish that this outcome deserves priority over every alternative. Nietzsche assigns the philosopher the future task of determining a hierarchy of values.

Nor should his physiological vocabulary be treated as a settled account of health or human rank. The relevant categories, the evidence for effects, and the choice of what counts as improvement all need scrutiny. The Note invites inquiry while carrying the aristocratic preferences that inquiry must also examine.

A difficulty to keep in view

Can the suspicion of revenge become a way of refusing every claim made by an injured person?

If anger, patience, protest, and forgiveness can all be interpreted as expressions of ressentiment, the explanation risks becoming impossible to challenge. A critic might always claim to know the concealed motive better than the person making the demand.

Give a claim to justice a fair test. What happened? What remedy is requested? Would the claimant accept protection or repair without the offender’s humiliation? Their answers might expose vindictiveness, support a different interpretation, or reveal mixed motives. The example is an invitation to distinguish possibilities, not a guarantee of transparent self-knowledge.

Even discovering vindictiveness would not erase the original injury. A badly motivated demand can concern a real wrong, while a sincere demand can rest on a mistaken judgment. Motive, injury, and justification must remain distinguishable.

The same scrutiny belongs to Nietzsche’s history. His anti-Jewish characterization and political hierarchy help determine which outcomes appear as victories or defeats. Identifying those commitments does not dispose of every psychological insight, but it prevents his rankings from arriving disguised as historical necessity.

The research Note offers a productive discipline: specify the object, evidence, and standard of value. Apply it to the workshop’s accusations and to Nietzsche’s account of Europe as firmly as to the ideals under investigation.

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

What transformation occurs in the workshop of I §14?

Inability to retaliate and compulsory submission receive the names of freely exercised virtues. Anticipated revenge becomes the victory of divine righteousness. Nietzsche stages this through a guide and imagined observer; their dialogue is part of his argument, not independent historical testimony.

Why are Aquinas and Tertullian quoted in I §15?

Nietzsche uses them to expose the enjoyment of others’ punishment within images of heavenly happiness. Tertullian’s anticipated judgment relocates the spectacle he rejects in worldly entertainments. Their quoted expectations must be distinguished from Nietzsche’s critical commentary and from every possible Christian position.

What does the Rome–Judea narrative claim?

It interprets European history as a recurring struggle between aristocratic valuation and the moral reversal associated with ressentiment. Its account includes anti-Jewish generalizations and attacks on equality. The symbolic scheme does not establish that it adequately explains the historical events it organizes.

Does going beyond good and evil abolish valuation?

No. I §17 distinguishes it from going beyond good and bad. Nietzsche continues to rank human possibilities and invites a renewed conflict over the standards of valuation. His own preferred hierarchy therefore remains something readers must assess.

Can the Note’s proposed sciences determine what we ought to value?

They can contribute evidence about origins, development, and effects. Choosing which effects deserve priority requires a further evaluative judgment. Nietzsche explicitly distinguishes competing goals and assigns the philosopher the task of determining a hierarchy rather than treating majority benefit as automatically decisive.

What should I write in my notes?

Choose one demand for justice, real or invented, that you can discuss without disclosing private details. Write 200–300 words:

  1. State the alleged injury and the requested outcome separately.
  2. Using I §§14–15, explain how a desire for another person’s humiliation could enter the demand. Mark this as a possibility, not a proven motive.
  3. Give evidence that could favor a different interpretation, such as concern for protection or repair.
  4. Use the Note’s question about value to identify the outcome being treated as most important.
  5. Explain whether the demand could remain justified even if one of its motives were objectionable.

A strong response distinguishes psychological suspicion from a verdict on the claim, and applies the demand for evidence to the suspicious interpretation itself.
