The Aesthetic Hearer and the Mythless Culture

Optional full-text route · Station 16 of 19

Read: §22 and §23 of The Birth of Tragedy, both in full.
Allow: About 55–80 minutes, including the passages, this guide, and a short written response.

Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. Free alternative (Haussmann): §22 · §23.

Quotations on this page follow Haussmann’s free translation. Haussmann prints lines from Isolde’s closing song in §22 in German, with a translation in a footnote.

Before you read

§21 ended with the “fraternal union” in which Apollo finally speaks the language of Dionysus. §22 asks the “attentive friend” to test that account against his own experience of a true musical tragedy. It then turns on those who explain tragedy in moral or medical terms. §23 turns the hearer’s test into a test of a whole culture: can it still receive myth? It ends with a nationalist call.

These two sections move from aesthetics to cultural politics without a break. This guide follows that movement closely and states plainly what the end of §23 says.

The main question: Who, on Nietzsche’s account, is capable of experiencing tragedy, and why does that question turn in §23 into a question about the health of a whole culture?

Read §22 and §23 once. Mark where §22 describes an experience, where it dismisses rival explanations, and where §23 moves from the individual hearer to a people.

What to notice

  1. The divided spectator. §22 lists pairs of opposed responses: seeing and wishing to be blind, understanding and fleeing into the incomprehensible. What single explanation does Nietzsche give for them?
  2. The critic. Who sits in the theatre in place of the “æsthetic hearer,” and how did that figure come to be there?
  3. Myth and a people. In §23, what does a culture lose without myth, and what does Nietzsche hope will restore it?

The divided spectator

Nietzsche asks the reader to recall the effect of a true musical tragedy. Watching the myth, the hearer felt “exalted to a kind of omniscience,” as though music let him see into the play of motives and passions. Yet this does not give the calm of Apollonian contemplation, “the justification of the world of the individuatio” that sculpture and epic provide. Instead the hearer is divided: “He beholds the transfigured world of the stage and nevertheless denies it.” He understands the scene and loves to flee into the incomprehensible. He shudders at the hero’s suffering and anticipates a higher joy. “He sees more extensively and profoundly than ever, and yet wishes to be blind.” §22, the divided hearer

Nietzsche’s explanation is the Dionysian spell working through Apollonian means. Tragic myth is “a symbolisation of Dionysian wisdom by means of the expedients of Apollonian art.” It leads the world of appearances to its limit, where it denies itself, like Isolde in her final song. The tragic artist, on this account, creates his figures like “a luxuriously fertile divinity of individuation,” then absorbs them again into the primordial unity. §22, tragic myth

Reading 7, which follows this lesson on the route, returns to these contradictions through the example of musical dissonance in §24.

Against moral and medical explanations

Nietzsche now attacks the usual theories. Aestheticians speak of the hero’s struggle with fate, of “the triumph of the moral order of the world,” or of a discharge of emotions. Nietzsche suspects they are not aesthetically excitable at all, but hear tragedy only as moral beings. “Never since Aristotle,” he says, has anyone explained the tragic effect from an aesthetic activity of the hearer. He names “The pathological discharge, the catharsis of Aristotle,” as the model of such explanations. He then cites Goethe’s remark that for the ancients the deepest pathos may have been mere aesthetic play. His own conclusion is that “the deepest pathos can in reality be merely æsthetic play.” §22, catharsis

Notice what Nietzsche refuses. Tragedy is not moral instruction, and it is not therapy for pity and fear. He wants an explanation from the hearer’s artistic experience alone. Ask whether that refusal is compatible with the “metaphysical comfort” the book has offered since §7. Is comfort not a kind of relief? Nietzsche returns to Aristotle and discharge in a later work, which the theme page on tragedy and aesthetic justification compares (1888).

The critic in the theatre

“Thus with the re-birth of tragedy,” Nietzsche writes, “the æsthetic hearer is also born anew.” In his place in the theatre there used to sit the “critic,” with “half-moral and half-learned pretensions.” Education and the press have prepared the whole public to be such critics: “the student, the school-boy, yea, even the most harmless womanly creature.” Art has been used for moral education and for political “tendency,” and has become an object of gossip: “there has never been so much gossip about art and so little esteem for it.” Only now and then does someone feel the unexplained effect of a performance of Lohengrin, and, as Nietzsche puts it, “divined what the æsthetic hearer is.” §22, the critic

The phrase “the most harmless womanly creature” is a patronizing gendered example. It closes a list of readers whom, Nietzsche says, education and the press have prepared to receive art as critics. The contempt is aimed at the educated public as a whole; here it takes a gendered form, and that form should be noticed, not passed over.

A test for the reader

§23 turns the question to the reader. To learn whether you are “the true æsthetic hearer” or belong with “the Socrato-critical man,” ask how you receive the wonder shown on the stage. Does your “historical sense, which insists on strict psychological causality,” feel insulted? Or do you allow it only as something for children? Myth, Nietzsche says, is “the concentrated picture of the world,” an “abbreviature of phenomena,” and it “cannot dispense with wonder.” §23, the test

Myth as a culture’s horizon

From this test Nietzsche moves to a general thesis. “Without myth, however, every culture loses its healthy, creative natural power: it is only a horizon encompassed with myths which rounds off to unity a social movement.” Myth guides the young soul, gives a person’s life its meaning, and gives the state its “mythical foundation.” Against this he sets “the abstract man proceeding independently of myth,” with abstract education, right, and state. The result is the present: “the myth-less man remains eternally hungering among all the bygones,” digging through every past culture. Everything nourishing is turned into “history and criticism.” §23, myth and culture

This is the section’s philosophical claim, and it can be discussed on its own terms. Does a shared myth make a culture’s unity possible? What would count as a myth in a modern society, and who would decide?

France, Luther, and a German hope

The claim then becomes national. Nietzsche contrasts “civilised France,” where people and culture have become identical, with Germany. There, he says, present culture has nothing in common with the “noble kernel” of the people, beneath which lies “a glorious, intrinsically healthy, primeval power.” That power broke out in the Reformation. In “this chorale of Luther” Nietzsche hears “the first Dionysian-luring call.” To it responded “the solemnly wanton procession of Dionysian revellers.” To that procession he credits German music, and from it he expects “the re-birth of German myth.” §23, German myth

Nietzsche then gives a reason for the importance of myth. A people, he says, is worth only as much as its ability to stamp its experiences with “the seal of eternity.” Nietzsche associates historical self-understanding with secularization. When a people “begins to comprehend itself historically,” he says, “a marked secularisation” usually follows. Greek tragedy delayed the destruction of myth. Modern culture since the fifteenth-century revival of Alexandrian and Roman antiquity shows the same restless hunger, and “It seems hardly possible to transplant a foreign myth” without harm. §23, eternity and history

The call to remove “foreign elements”

The section ends with a call. Nietzsche says he expects of “the pure and vigorous kernel of the German being,” and of it alone, the “elimination of forcibly ingrafted foreign elements.” Some, he writes, may think the German spirit must begin its struggle with “the elimination of the Romanic element.” They might find an outward preparation and encouragement for this in “the victorious bravery and bloody glory of the late war.” The inner impulse must come from the wish to be worthy of Luther and the great German artists and poets. No German should think he can fight such battles “without his household gods, without his mythical home.” The section closes with “the Dionysian bird” that points the way home. §23, the call

Keep five claims distinct:

  • Nietzsche’s textual claim: it is a nationalist call for the German spirit to remove what he regards as foreign, especially Romanic, cultural elements. He connects it to praise of the recent war as outward encouragement. The elements he names are cultural: myth, art, and forms of civilization. The passage proposes no expulsion of people. That does not settle every question about its nationalism. Its language of a “pure and vigorous kernel of the German being,” set against “forcibly ingrafted foreign elements,” invites the question of how far that nationalism is ethnicized. This guide leaves that interpretive question open for the reader.
  • What it also is: a call for national cultural purification, with military victory praised as encouragement. That should not be played down either.
  • Historical anchoring: “the late war” is Nietzsche’s phrase. In the 1886 preface (§1), he dates the book’s composition to “the Franco-German war of 1870-71.” This guide adds no further historical claim.
  • The site’s interpretation: §23 turns an aesthetic thesis (myth unifies a culture) into a program for one nation. It gives the nation the task of cleansing its culture.
  • Evaluation: whether a culture’s health requires such cleansing, and what follows from treating a neighbouring culture as the foreign element to be removed, are questions for you. The passage does not argue for its answer; it assumes it.

A difficulty

Can the claim that myth unifies a culture be separated from the national program §23 attaches to it?

The general thesis concerns any culture. Myth gives a horizon, meaning, and a foundation for common life. The program concerns one people, and asks it to remove foreign elements and recover its own myth. Nietzsche moves from the first to the second in a few sentences.

Write two paragraphs:

  • In the first, state the general thesis in your own words and give it its strongest form.
  • In the second, identify the premises the national program adds. These include that a people has a “pure” kernel, that some cultural elements are foreign to it, and that removing them is the path to health. Ask whether any of these follows from the general thesis.

A second difficulty concerns §22’s test of the hearer. Anyone who explains tragedy in moral or medical terms is classed as a “critic,” not a true hearer. How could someone disagree with Nietzsche’s account without being disqualified by it?

Retrospect checkpoint: an 1886 comparison

If you are following the chronological alternate, skip this until after the 1886 preface lesson.

In self-criticism §6, Nietzsche says he spoiled the Greek problem “by the admixture of the most modern things,” and that he has since learned to regard “this ‘spirit of Teutonism’ as something to be despaired of.” He also calls German music a narcotic.

  • Compare these judgments with the end of §23.
  • Note that the later judgment records a change of mind. It does not cancel what the 1872 text says, and a reader should not use it to soften §23.
  • Does the 1886 preface address the call to remove “foreign elements” itself, or only the hope attached to German music?

Check your understanding

Try answering before opening each suggested response.

Why does the tragic hearer both see more and "wishes to be blind"?

Because, on Nietzsche's account, tragic myth uses Apollonian means (a clear, individual world onstage) to express Dionysian wisdom. The hearer is drawn into vivid seeing and understanding, and at the same time beyond the individual world toward its denial. The Dionysian spell pushes Apollonian power to its limit.

What does Nietzsche object to in moral and cathartic explanations of tragedy?

That they explain its effect by something outside art: the triumph of a moral order, or a relieving discharge of pity and fear. He thinks this shows that such interpreters hear tragedy as moral beings, not as aesthetic hearers. He wants an explanation from the hearer's artistic experience, and holds that the deepest pathos can be aesthetic play.

What exactly does §23 call on the German spirit to do?

To eliminate what Nietzsche regards as "forcibly ingrafted foreign elements" in its culture, perhaps beginning with "the Romanic element." It is to take encouragement from the recent war's "bloody glory," and inner direction from Luther and the German artists, and so recover its own myth. What the text calls for removing are cultural elements; it proposes no expulsion of people. It is a nationalist call for cultural purification, and it praises military victory. How far its nationalism is ethnicized remains a question the passage leaves open.

Writing prompt

Write 200–250 words answering:

How does Nietzsche move from the experience of a single hearer in §22 to the destiny of a nation in §23, and at which step would you stop following him?

  • Begin with the divided hearer of §22.
  • Explain the thesis that myth gives a culture its horizon, using one phrase from §23.
  • State the closing call precisely, including its connection to the recent war.
  • Mark the step where the argument’s premises change, and say why you would accept or refuse that step.

A successful response states the nationalist call accurately, neither softening nor exaggerating it. It distinguishes Nietzsche’s claim from the site’s interpretation and from the writer’s own evaluation, and it identifies the premises the national program adds to the general thesis.
