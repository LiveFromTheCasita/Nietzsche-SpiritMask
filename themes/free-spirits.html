"Free spirit" sounds like a compliment anyone would accept. Nietzsche defines it narrowly, uses it against most of the people who would claim it, and by 1886 is describing something he says did not yet exist when he first wrote the word.

The term is introduced in Human, All Too Human in 1878 and defined there in a single dry sentence: a free spirit is one who thinks otherwise than would have been expected on the basis of his origin, his surroundings, his position and office, or the ruling views of the age. That definition is relative by design. It says nothing about what the free spirit believes, only that the beliefs were not the ones his circumstances would have produced. Freedom, on this account, is measured against a particular inheritance rather than against constraint as such.

By 1886 Nietzsche is doing something different. In the prefaces he adds that year to Human, All Too Human and Daybreak, and in the part of Beyond Good and Evil that carries the phrase as its title, the free spirit becomes a stage on a path: a necessary separation from what one had revered, valuable because of what it prepares rather than for itself. And in Beyond Good and Evil §44 he explicitly refuses the term to the people his contemporaries would have applied it to. The Freidenker — the levelling, democratic, anti-religious free thinker of the nineteenth century — is named there as the opposite of what he means.

Two things keep this page from becoming a portrait of independence in general. The first is chronology: the retrospective prefaces are evidence of how Nietzsche in 1886 wanted the earlier books to be read, and they are not evidence about what those books were doing in 1878 and 1881. The section below sets that problem out. The second is that the free spirit is not, for Nietzsche, a person who has escaped commitments. It is the name for a stage on the way to a type that commands and legislates — and that destination is where most of the difficulty on this page lives.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves chronologically: the 1878 definition, the two prefaces added in 1886, the chapter that breaks with the free thinkers, and the late sections on the philosopher of the future. Continue through the fuller route to test the figure against its neighbours and its critics, or use the work survey for a particular book. This theme has no separate notebook route; the note below says why.

Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order. The sequence is deliberately chronological, because on this theme the chronology is the argument: 1878 first, then the two prefaces written eight and five years later, then the 1886 chapter and the 1887 aphorism that break with the free thinkers, then the late description of what the free spirit was supposed to be a stage toward.

  1. Core · 1878

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §225

    The definition, as it stood when the word was new

    Question: The definition is explicitly relative. Whose expectations is the free spirit free of, and what follows if those expectations change?

    Why it belongs: One short aphorism, and the only place in the book where the title phrase is defined. A free spirit is called one who thinks differently from what would have been expected given his descent, his environment, his class and occupation, or the ruling views of the time; the "fettered spirits" are those who hold their positions for reasons they have not examined. Two things are worth noticing immediately. The definition is entirely formal — it specifies a relation to inherited expectation, not a content — and it makes free-spiritedness a matter of degree and circumstance rather than a type of soul. Everything the late work adds is an addition to this, and some of it is in tension with it.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §44; Human, All Too Human §§637–638.

    Online edition: Volume I (trans. Helen Zimmern; opens nearby — use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core · Preface added 1886

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, Preface (1886), §§1–7

    A story told about a book eight years afterwards

    Question: Nietzsche says he invented the free spirits to whom the book is dedicated, and that there are none yet. What is being claimed about the 1878 book, and what is being claimed about 1886?

    Why it belongs: The preface is the fullest account Nietzsche ever gave of the figure, and it is a retrospective narrative: a great severance arriving suddenly, a first victory over what had been revered, the dangerous years of licence and wandering, and a convalescence that ends with the free spirit able to look back at what it had escaped. It also contains the admission that matters most for reading it — that the free spirits it addresses did not exist and were company invented for a sick man. Read it as the best statement of the ideal and as evidence about 1886, not as a report of what the 1878 aphorisms were doing. See the section below.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §225; Daybreak, Preface (1886).

    Online edition: Preface (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  3. Development · Preface added 1886

    Daybreak Preface (1886), §§1–5; §575

    The subterranean worker, and the aeronauts at the end

    The free edition is titled The Dawn of Day (trans. J. M. Kennedy).

    Question: The preface describes the work as digging and undermining. What kind of freedom is available to someone who describes themselves that way?

    Why it belongs: The second 1886 preface presents the author as someone working underground, in the dark, at the foundations of a morality he had once trusted, and says that the book begins the campaign against morality by asking what morality's own trust in itself rests on. §575, the last section of the book, gives the other image: the birds that fly further than any bird before, and the question of where the ones who go furthest still are heading. The pair is the theme in miniature — patient destructive work at one end, an unspecified and slightly ominous open horizon at the other. Written five years after the book itself, the preface is again retrospective, and the same caution applies.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, Preface (1886); Daybreak §501.

    Online edition: Preface (trans. J. M. Kennedy; for §575 use the contents at the end of Book V) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil Part II, §§24–44

    The chapter that takes the name back

    Part II: "The Free Spirit." Section numbers run continuously across the book's nine parts.

    Question: §44 ends by saying "we free spirits" and then lists what they are not. What is left in the description that is positive rather than negative?

    Why it belongs: The mature treatment, and the place where the term stops being available to Nietzsche's contemporaries. Read the whole part, and read §44 twice. It calls the levellers who call themselves free spirits by that name in quotation marks, ties them to democratic taste and to the wish for universal green pasture happiness, and sets against them a type defined by the opposite preferences — severity, distance, and the conviction that danger and hardness are what produce anything worth having. §41 is the practical counterpart: a series of tests one sets oneself, including not remaining attached to a person, a country, a pity or a science. Note where the difficulty is. Almost everything positive in the chapter is stated as a preference for one kind of person over another, and the argument for that preference is not given here.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §347; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: Part II (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  5. Complication · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §347

    The need to believe, and where it goes when religion fails

    Kaufmann: Believers and their need to believe. Common: Believers and their Need of Belief.

    Question: If the need for certainty can attach itself to any content, what would show that someone had actually stopped needing it?

    Why it belongs: The aphorism argues that faith is wanted most where will is weakest, that what such people need is not a particular doctrine but something unconditional to lean on, and that this need survives the loss of religion and reappears attached to science, to nationalism, to political certainties. It is the published, argued version of the distinction that §44 makes polemically: what separates the free spirit from the free thinker is not the content of their opinions but whether the demand for something to hold onto has been given up. It also sets the hardest possible test for this theme, since nothing in the section exempts Nietzsche's own convictions from the diagnosis.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §24; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §347 (trans. Thomas Common; the free edition is titled The Joyful Wisdom) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §§203, 211

    What the free spirit was a stage toward

    §203 is in Part V, "On the Natural History of Morals"; §211 is in Part VI, "We Scholars."

    Question: §211 says genuine philosophers create values while philosophical labourers only formalize them. Which of the two does the free spirit of §225 resemble?

    Why it belongs: The destination. §203 states the hope for new philosophers strong enough to reverse eternal valuations, and admits in the same breath how unlikely it is and how heavy the thought of what might become of the human being has grown. §211 distinguishes the philosophical labourer, who takes established valuations and makes them manageable, from the philosopher proper, whose knowing is creating and whose will to truth is will to power. The pair is where the theme stops being about independence: the free spirit is valued as a stage because commanding is supposed to follow detachment. Whether Nietzsche gives any account of what would entitle such commanding is the open question this page ends on.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§212–213; Revaluation.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; §211 is in Part VI — use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Five investigations to take after the first encounter. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below. Read the assignments within a group in order; the question above them says what the comparison is meant to test.

1. Before the word existed

The figure has a prehistory in the early essays, where Nietzsche is describing the philosopher as a cultural type rather than naming a kind of reader. Ask how much of the later figure is already there and how much is read back into it.

  1. Untimely Meditations — III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §1
    The obstacles to becoming oneself, and the fear of one's neighbours.
  2. Untimely Meditations — III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §3
    The educator as one who reveals rather than instructs.
  3. Untimely Meditations — III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §6
    The public claim: what a culture owes to the production of a kind of person.
  4. Homer's Contest
    Excellence as requiring rivals — a check on any purely solitary reading of the figure.

2. Free spirit against free thinker

The distinction Nietzsche cares most about. Follow it from the polemical version through the argued one to its genealogical ground.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §44
    The levellers named, and the term reclaimed against them.
  2. The Gay Science — §347
    The need to believe, and its migration out of religion.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §24
    Unbelievers described as the ascetic ideal's most recent and most honest believers.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27
    Where that argument leads, and what it asks of anyone who accepts it.

3. What the freedom costs

Nietzsche is consistent that this is not a comfortable condition. Read the passages where the cost is stated without the compensations.

  1. Human, All Too Human — §§637–638
    The wanderer who has left one house and not found another.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Way of the Creator"
    Solitude, and the question of whether one may give oneself one's own law.
  3. Daybreak — §501
    The discipline the work requires, stated without heroics.
  4. The Gay Science — §283
    Preparatory human beings, described as living dangerously and for the sake of others later.

4. Neighbouring figures, kept apart

The free spirit is not the overman, not the noble type, and not simply a scholar. Test the boundaries where the corpus draws them itself.

  1. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Three Metamorphoses"
    Three stages, of which the lion — the destroyer of the "thou shalt" — is not the last.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue"
    The turn from taking one's independence to giving something away.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §§212–213
    The philosopher as the bad conscience of his age, and what cannot be learned.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §10
    The philosopher's historical need for a disguise, and the type's late arrival.

5. The retrospect as evidence

The last group is methodological. Compare what the 1886 and 1888 self-descriptions claim with what the books they describe actually did.

  1. Human, All Too Human — Preface (1886)
    The great severance, the dangerous years, the convalescence.
  2. Human, All Too Human — §225
    The definition the preface is describing, which contains none of that narrative.
  3. Daybreak — Preface (1886)
    The book recast as the opening of a campaign against morality.
  4. Ecce Homo — on Human, All Too Human, §1
    The final version of the story, two years later still.

Keep these distinctions in view

Free spirit and free thinker
The Freidenker of Nietzsche's century was an anti-clerical, progressive, usually democratic type. Beyond Good and Evil §44 uses the word for exactly that figure and refuses it. The difference Nietzsche draws is not about religion at all: it is about whether the demand for something unconditional to rest on has been given up, and The Gay Science §347 is where he argues for it.
Freedom from and freedom for
The 1878 definition is entirely negative: thinking otherwise than expected. The late work treats that as a stage and asks what the freedom is for. Readings that stop at the first make Nietzsche an advocate of independence as such, which is the one thing Zarathustra's lion is explicitly not enough to be.
The book and its preface
Human, All Too Human was published in 1878; its preface was written in 1886. Quoting the preface as though it stated the book's own view telescopes eight years of development. The same applies to Daybreak and its 1886 preface. See the section below.
Free spirit and higher type
The free spirit is an intellectual achievement described in terms of what someone has detached themselves from. The higher type, in the late books, is described in terms of constitution and rank. They overlap in Nietzsche's examples and they are not the same concept, and the slide from one to the other is where a claim about thinking turns into a claim about people.
Solitude and isolation
Nietzsche's free spirit is solitary but not self-sufficient: "Homer's Contest" makes excellence depend on worthy rivals, and Zarathustra makes the friend the one before whom one must not go soft. Loneliness is described as a condition of the work, not as its reward.
Scepticism and the absence of convictions
What §347 attacks is the need for certainty, not holding views. Nietzsche holds a great many, forcefully. Whether his own position survives the test he sets there is a genuine question, and it is more interesting than either the charge of self-refutation or the claim that he is exempt.
Nietzsche's ideal and modern individualism
The free spirit is not a defence of individual autonomy, of doing as one likes, or of freedom of opinion as a political value. Beyond Good and Evil Part II is hostile to most of those, and §44 explicitly counts the taste for them as a symptom. Naming that hostility is not endorsing it; it is the condition of arguing with the text rather than with a friendlier substitute.

The 1886 prefaces and what they show

In 1886, preparing new editions, Nietzsche wrote fresh prefaces for The Birth of Tragedy, the two volumes of Human, All Too Human, Daybreak and — in 1887 — The Gay Science. Most of what this theme is built from comes from those prefaces, so it is worth being clear about what kind of document they are.

They are retrospective and they are constructive. The Human, All Too Human preface tells a story with stages — attachment, severance, wandering, convalescence, mastery — and presents the book as the record of a passage through them. That story is not in the 1878 book. What is in the 1878 book is §225, a short formal definition, a great many aphorisms about art, religion, women, and the state, and a dedication to free spirits who, the 1886 preface says plainly, did not exist and had to be invented. The preface is not describing what the earlier Nietzsche thought he was doing; it is telling the reader how the later Nietzsche wants the sequence of his books to be seen.

This is not a charge of dishonesty. A preface is entitled to interpret. The point is that it makes weaker evidence than it looks like about the middle period, and stronger evidence than it looks like about 1886. Two practical consequences follow. First, when this page cites a preface, the date is given, because a claim about a book's purpose written eight years later is a different kind of claim from one made in the book. Second, where the preface and the book disagree in emphasis, the disagreement is worth keeping rather than resolving: the drier, more relative free spirit of §225 and the dramatic convalescent of the preface are both Nietzsche's, five and eight years apart.

Ecce Homo takes the same procedure further, since the whole book is an account of what each of the earlier works was for. The chapter on Human, All Too Human gives the most explicit definition of the free spirit anywhere in the corpus — a spirit that has become free and has taken possession of itself again — and it is also the least independent source available, written in 1888 by a man reconstructing his own development. Use it for the formulation; do not use it for the chronology.

The early essays and Untimely Meditations4 reading assignments

The prehistory. "Schopenhauer as Educator" (1874) is the third of the Untimely Meditations; the free editions divide the four essays across two volumes under the title Thoughts Out of Season, and this one is in the volume translated by Adrian Collins. "Homer's Contest" is a short piece from 1872 that Nietzsche did not publish, and it is in a different free volume.

  • Earlier formulation

    Untimely Meditations III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §1

    What makes becoming oneself difficult

    Collins: "Schopenhauer as Educator," in Thoughts Out of Season, Part II.

    Question: Nietzsche says people hide behind customs and opinions out of laziness and fear. Is that a moral criticism or a description of an incentive?

    Why it belongs: The essay opens by observing that everyone knows themselves to be unique and yet conceals it, and that what stands in the way of becoming oneself is comfort and the fear of one's neighbours. The structure of the later figure is here without the name: a specific inheritance, and a small number of people who do not simply reproduce it. Written in 1874, well before "free spirit" is a term of art, and in a much warmer register than the 1886 material.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §225; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: "Schopenhauer as Educator" (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Earlier formulation

    Untimely Meditations III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §3

    The educator who removes rather than adds

    Question: If educators are liberators who clear away what is foreign, what is supposed to be underneath?

    Why it belongs: The section describes true educators as those who reveal what one's nature actually is, and describes Schopenhauer as having performed that office for Nietzsche. It is the source of a formulation that runs through to the subtitle of Ecce Homo, "How One Becomes What One Is." Two cautions are worth carrying from it. The essay assumes a self to be uncovered, which the later critique of the subject makes harder to sustain; and it is an essay about Schopenhauer written by someone still under his influence, which the 1886 prefaces will describe as exactly the attachment that had to be broken.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, Preface (1886); Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: "Schopenhauer as Educator" §3 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Untimely Meditations III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §6

    Philosophy as a public office

    Question: What does a culture get from a philosopher, on this account, that it could not get from a scholar?

    Why it belongs: The section argues that the philosopher's value to a culture lies in the example of a life rather than in a body of results, and that the state's interest in philosophy is not the same as philosophy's interest in itself. It is the first statement of a claim the late books repeat: what philosophy produces is a kind of human being. That claim does a great deal of work later on this page, and it is asserted here rather than argued.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§212–213; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: "Schopenhauer as Educator" §6 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Homer's Contest 1872, unpublished

    Excellence as needing a rival

    Mügge: Homer's Contest, in Early Greek Philosophy & Other Essays.

    Question: If achievement depends on having someone to measure against, what happens to a figure defined by detachment?

    Why it belongs: The essay describes the Greek agon as an institution that kept ambition productive by ensuring that no one stood alone, and treats the removal of rivals as dangerous rather than as victory. It is the strongest internal check on reading the free spirit as a solitary ideal, and it comes from Nietzsche himself rather than from a critic. Unpublished, early, and about Greek institutions rather than about modern intellectual life — so it complicates the later figure without settling anything about it.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Way of the Creator"; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: Homer's Contest (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

  • Development · 1878

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§637–638

    The wanderer, at the end of the book

    Zimmern: §638, The Wanderer.

    Question: The wanderer is described as without a goal and taking pleasure in change. Is that freedom, or the absence of anything to be free for?

    Why it belongs: The closing aphorisms give the figure its image: someone who has left a final goal behind, who takes pleasure in what is transient, and for whom there are mornings after difficult nights. It is the middle period's own statement of the condition, made without the drama of the later preface and without promising that the wandering leads anywhere. Read beside the preface's convalescence narrative and note how much more provisional the 1878 version is.

    Read alongside: Preface (1886); Genealogy, Preface §§7–8.

    Online edition: §§637–638 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · Preface added 1886

    Human, All Too Human Volume II, Preface (1886)

    The second-volume retrospect

    Free edition: Human, All-Too-Human, Part II (trans. Paul V. Cohn), containing Miscellaneous Maxims and Opinions and The Wanderer and His Shadow.

    Question: Nietzsche describes the second volume as a continuation and a cure. What would count as evidence that the writing did the work he says it did?

    Why it belongs: The companion to the Volume I preface, and useful because it covers the material of 1879–80 that the first preface passes over. It extends the same retrospective narrative to the books written during the worst years of Nietzsche's illness and while he was giving up his professorship. Included as an extension rather than in the first encounter: it adds detail to the story rather than a new position, and the caution about retrospective evidence applies here with full force.

    Read alongside: Volume I, Preface (1886); Body and physiology.

Daybreak3 reading assignments

The 1881 book and its 1886 preface. Daybreak is the most patient of the middle-period books, and its treatment of the free-spirit task is correspondingly undramatic. Preferred translation: Hollingdale; the free edition is The Dawn of Day, translated by J. M. Kennedy.

  • Development · Preface added 1886

    Daybreak Preface (1886), §§1–5; §575

    The subterranean worker, and the aeronauts at the end

    Question: What kind of freedom belongs to someone who describes their own work as undermining?

    Why it belongs: The second of the 1886 prefaces, and the book's closing image. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, Preface (1886); Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Preface (trans. J. M. Kennedy; for §575 use the contents at the end of Book V) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1881

    Daybreak §454

    The long interruption

    Question: What does it change to treat the work of becoming oneself as something that can be interrupted rather than completed?

    Why it belongs: A short middle-period aphorism on the kind of task that cannot be finished and has to be taken up again. Its usefulness on this page is its tone: the middle period describes free-spiritedness as ordinary labour with setbacks, which is a long way from the prefaces' narrative of severance and convalescence. Holding the two registers together is most of what reading this theme chronologically achieves.

    Read alongside: §501; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Book V (trans. J. M. Kennedy; opens nearby — use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1881

    Daybreak §501

    Mortal souls and the discipline that follows from them

    Question: If one has only this life for knowing, does that make the work more urgent or less ambitious?

    Why it belongs: The section connects the loss of immortality to the conditions of inquiry: what one can learn is bounded by a single life, and that bound changes what it is reasonable to attempt. It supplies the middle period's version of the free spirit's discipline, grounded in a limitation rather than in a calling. Read it against Beyond Good and Evil §41, where the same discipline is described as a series of tests one imposes on oneself.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §41; Death of God.

    Online edition: Book V (trans. J. M. Kennedy; opens nearby — use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

The transitional book. Book IV (1882) supplies the description of the preparatory type; Book V (1887) supplies the argued break with the free thinkers and is contemporaneous with the Genealogy. The year is marked on each entry.

  • Development · Book IV (1882)

    The Gay Science §283

    Preparatory human beings

    Kaufmann: Preparatory human beings.

    Question: The section says these people live for the sake of others who come later. How is that different from the self-sacrifice Nietzsche attacks elsewhere?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche calls for people who know how to be silent, solitary, resolute and patient in invisible activity, who seek in all things what must be overcome in them, and who will live dangerously and build their cities on the slopes of Vesuvius. It is the most concrete published description of what the free spirit is supposed to do rather than avoid. The strain worth noticing is the one in the question above: a type defined by hardness and distance is here asked to work for a future it will not see, which is close to a demand for sacrifice.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§24–44; Overman.

    Online edition: §283 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · Book IV (1882)

    The Gay Science §307

    What is owed to a conviction one has left

    Question: Nietzsche says a discarded opinion was not an error but a necessary stage. Does that make abandoning it easier or harder to justify?

    Why it belongs: The aphorism argues that what now looks like an error was once required by the person one was, and that criticism is a moulting rather than a correction. It is the most generous thing Nietzsche says about giving up a position, and it gives the free spirit's relation to its own past a shape that is neither triumph nor regret. It also anticipates, in a calmer voice, the story the 1886 prefaces will dramatize.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, Preface (1886); Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: §307 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

    Why it belongs: The argued version of the break with the free thinkers. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra4 reading assignments

The dramatic middle. Zarathustra does not use the term, and its usefulness here is that it refuses to let detachment be the end of the story. All four chapters are from Parts I and II.

  • Core of the developmental picture

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Three Metamorphoses"

    Camel, lion, child

    Common: The Three Metamorphoses.

    Question: The lion can destroy the "thou shalt" but cannot create new values. Why is a third stage needed rather than a stronger lion?

    Why it belongs: The speech sets out three transformations: the camel that takes the heaviest burdens on itself, the lion that wins freedom in its own desert by saying no to the dragon whose scales read "thou shalt," and the child that is innocence, forgetting and a new beginning. It is the clearest statement anywhere in Nietzsche that negative freedom is insufficient, and it is the reason this page treats the free spirit as a stage. Note that the speech tells you a third stage is necessary and tells you very little about how the second becomes the third.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §44; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Three Metamorphoses" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Way of the Creator"

    Free from what, and free for what

    Common: The Way of the Creating One.

    Question: Zarathustra asks whether you can give yourself your own good and evil and hang your own will above yourself as a law. What would a negative answer imply?

    Why it belongs: The speech puts the question this theme most needs asked — free from what, and free for what — and then describes the dangers: the isolation, the hatred of the herd for the one who separates, and the risk of becoming one's own worst enemy in the woods. It is the least encouraging chapter in Zarathustra about the free spirit's situation, and it is the best counterweight to reading independence as achievement.

    Read alongside: Homer's Contest; The Gay Science §283.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Way of the Creating One" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue" §§1–3

    Independence turning into something given away

    Common: The Bestowing Virtue.

    Question: The highest virtue is described as a squandering. How does that sit with the hardness demanded elsewhere?

    Why it belongs: The closing speech of Part I describes a virtue that gives itself away out of abundance, and ends by telling the disciples to lose Zarathustra and find themselves. It is where the figure of the detached thinker turns into something else, and it supplies the positive content that "On the Three Metamorphoses" leaves as a promise. It is also the chapter most easily sentimentalized, so read §2 as well, where the same abundance is described in terms of power and of a selfishness Nietzsche is not apologizing for.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211; Overman.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Bestowing Virtue" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part II, "On the Famous Wise Men"

    Serving the people and calling it truthfulness

    Question: The charge is that the celebrated wise have served the people's reverence. What would a thinker who did not look like?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra accuses the famous wise men of having served the people and the people's superstition, and says that the spirit of the free is something else — a will that wants truth even where it is unwelcome. It is the dramatic predecessor of Beyond Good and Evil Part VI, and it shows the theme's polemical edge in its earliest strong form. The speech also makes no distinction between serving the public and being wrong, which is worth pressing on.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§212–213; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: Open the free edition (trans. Thomas Common; use the contents for the Part II chapter on the famous wise) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil4 reading assignments

The major late site. Part II carries the theme's name, and Part VI describes what the free spirit is supposed to become. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296 across the nine parts.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §6

    Philosophy as a confession

    Part I, "On the Prejudices of Philosophers."

    Question: If every philosophy is its author's involuntary memoir, what does the free spirit know about their own positions that others do not?

    Why it belongs: The section proposes that every great philosophy has been the personal confession of its author and a kind of unintended memoir, and that the moral or immoral intentions in it are the real seed from which the rest grew. On this page it supplies the free spirit's distinguishing capacity: not holding better opinions but being able to ask what in oneself is producing them. It is also the claim that makes the type hard to verify from outside, since the difference it names is one of self-knowledge.

    Read alongside: The psychology of morality; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil Part II, §§24–44

    The chapter that takes the name back

    Question: What in §44 is positive rather than a refusal?

    Why it belongs: The mature treatment and the break with the Freidenker. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §347; Human, All Too Human §225.

    Online edition: Part II (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§203, 211

    What the free spirit was a stage toward

    Question: Which of the two types in §211 does the 1878 free spirit resemble?

    Why it belongs: The destination, and the distinction between labourers and legislators. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Revaluation; Zarathustra I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue".

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; §211 is in Part VI — use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§212–213

    The bad conscience of an age, and what cannot be taught

    Part VI, "We Scholars."

    Question: §213 says one must be born to philosophy and that the rest is inherited. What happens to the free-spirit project if that is right?

    Why it belongs: §212 describes the philosopher as necessarily the bad conscience of his time, measuring greatness by how much a person can bear rather than by virtue; §213 says that it is hard to learn what a philosopher is, because the thing cannot be taught, and that everything essential in the matter is inherited. The pair states the theme's least comfortable possibility: that the type Nietzsche is describing is a given rather than an achievement. That reading sits badly with the 1878 definition, which made free-spiritedness circumstantial, and the tension between them is not resolved anywhere in the published work.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §225; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: Part VI (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals5 reading assignments

The Genealogy puts the free spirit inside a history rather than outside one, and the third essay is where the theme meets its hardest objection. The free edition numbers the Preface and each essay separately.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface, §§7–8

    The reader the book requires

    Question: Nietzsche says his book needs a reader who chews and ruminates. Is that a demand on the reader or a defence of the writing?

    Why it belongs: The Preface sets out the new question — the value of the values themselves — and then describes the kind of attention it will take to follow it: slow reading, and a willingness to be made uncomfortable by one's own answers. It gives the free spirit a job description that is intellectual rather than social, and it is the least polemical statement of the theme in the late work.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §§637–638; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Preface (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each essay are numbered separately) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§6–10

    The conditions under which philosophers have survived

    Question: If chastity, poverty and humility were the philosopher's working conditions rather than virtues, what are the equivalent conditions now?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reads the ascetic ideal in philosophers not as conviction but as the arrangement under which the type could work at all, and argues that philosophy had to disguise itself as something socially permitted — as the ascetic priest — in order to exist. The sections put the free spirit inside a history of institutional cover rather than outside institutions, and they supply the best reason to doubt that detachment is ever as complete as it appears.

    Read alongside: Body and physiology; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Essay III §§6–10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §10

    The disguise, and how recently it was dropped

    Question: If the contemplative type was long regarded with suspicion, what does its present respectability indicate?

    Why it belongs: The section traces how the contemplative life was first tolerated only when it looked frightening or priestly, and how long it took before a philosopher could be anything other than a disguised ascetic. On this page it is the corrective to any timeless picture of the free spirit: the social possibility of the type is dated, recent and conditional. Nietzsche's history here is a sketch rather than a demonstration, and should be read as the shape of an argument.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations III §6; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Essay III §10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §24

    The unbelievers named as the ideal's last believers

    Question: Nietzsche includes himself among those the argument implicates. Does that rescue the argument or make it worse?

    Why it belongs: The sharpest objection this theme faces, and Nietzsche makes it himself. The section denies that the free spirits and scientific unbelievers of the age are the ascetic ideal's opponents and calls them its most recent and most spiritual form, because they still hold truth to be unconditionally valuable. It is where the break with the Freidenker stops being a matter of taste and becomes an argument — and where the same argument is turned on the free spirits Nietzsche approves of.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §347; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Essay III §24 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §27

    Truthfulness destroying what produced it

    Question: If Christian truthfulness dissolved Christian dogma, what should it dissolve next — and who is in a position to let it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that Christianity as a dogma perished at the hands of its own morality, and that Christianity as a morality must now perish in the same way, by the law of self-overcoming. For this theme it is the place where the free spirit's task stops being a matter of temperament and becomes a position in a sequence: the one who carries the will to truth to the point where it turns on itself. It is also the strongest reason for reading §24's indictment as a task rather than as a dismissal.

    Read alongside: Essay III §24; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Essay III §27 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo2 reading assignments

Written in 1888 and published in 1908. It contains the most explicit definition of the free spirit in the corpus and is the least independent source for the history it narrates. The free edition titles the chapter "Why I Write Such Excellent Books."

  • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Write Such Good Books" — on Human, All Too Human, §1

    The definition, given last

    Question: "A spirit that has become free" is a completed process. When is it supposed to have completed?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche states that "free spirit" is to be understood in no other sense than as a spirit that has become free and has taken possession of itself again, and describes the 1878 book as a monument to a crisis. It is the sharpest formulation available and the last one written. Read it against Human, All Too Human §225, and note what has changed: a relative, circumstantial description has become an achieved condition with a narrative behind it. See the section on the prefaces for why that matters.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §225; its 1886 Preface.

    Online edition: "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use the contents for the section on Human, All-Too-Human) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §3

    Solitude described from inside

    Question: Is the loneliness reported here a philosophical condition or a biographical fact? Does Nietzsche distinguish them?

    Why it belongs: The section gives the personal version of what the free-spirit chapters describe structurally: distance from his own time, an instinct for cleanliness, and a solitude presented as constitutional rather than chosen. It is useful precisely because it does not argue. Readers can see what the ideal looks like when it is lived and reported by the person living it, and can weigh how much of the theme rests on that testimony.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Way of the Creator"; Amor fati.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

A note on the notebooks

This page carries no separate notebook route. There is late notebook material on the philosophers of the future, much of it from 1885–87 and overlapping with the drafting of Beyond Good and Evil Parts II and VI, but on this theme the published books are unusually complete: the definition, the break with the free thinkers, the developmental picture and the destination are all in print, and the notes mostly work toward what was printed. Adding a notebook route here would make the page longer without making the argument better.

If you do follow the material, the standard cautions apply. The Will to Power is a posthumous arrangement of notebook entries and not a book Nietzsche completed; its numbers are editorial entry numbers rather than original notebook references, and it gathers notes written at different times. For the original arrangement and manuscript references, consult Nietzsche Source's critical edition. The related notebook routes on revaluation and perspectivism cover the entries most often quoted in this vicinity.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • Eight years in one sitting. Read Human, All Too Human §225, then its 1886 Preface, then Ecce Homo on the same book. Three definitions, ten years apart. Which is the odd one out, and why?
  • The lion problem. Take Zarathustra I, "On the Three Metamorphoses" into "On the Gift-Giving Virtue" and then Beyond Good and Evil §211. Is there an account anywhere of how one gets from saying no to legislating?
  • The objection Nietzsche made first. Read Genealogy III §24 beside The Gay Science §347, then go back to Beyond Good and Evil §44. Does §44's free spirit survive §24's test any better than the free thinkers do?
  • Born or made. Compare Human, All Too Human §225 with Beyond Good and Evil §213. One makes the type circumstantial and the other makes it inherited. What in the corpus decides between them?

A question to carry forward

If the mark of a free spirit is not what they believe but whether they still need something unconditional to hold onto, what would you look for in someone's writing — including Nietzsche's — to tell the difference?
