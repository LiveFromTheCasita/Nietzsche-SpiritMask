A young philologist argues that the Greeks kept envy and the wish to destroy from wrecking their culture by building institutions in which those impulses produced poets, athletes and lawgivers instead. The claim is specific, it is historical, and almost everything on this page depends on whether it can bear the weight later placed on it.

The agon is the formalized contest: games, dramatic competitions, ostracism, the rivalry of one poet with another. In "Homer's Contest" — an unpublished 1872 essay — Nietzsche reads it as a solution to a problem. Greek culture, on his account, was not serene: it contained cruelty, the appetite for destruction, and an envy the Greeks themselves named and treated as ordinary. The contest does not remove those impulses. It gives them an object, a rule and an audience, and in doing so makes them productive. The mechanism is competitive and the outcome is cultural.

Two features of the argument are worth holding onto from the start. The contest requires a rough equality of powers: it works because the rivals can plausibly threaten each other, which is why the essay treats the removal of a rival — by ostracism, or by there being no one left to compete with — as a danger rather than a victory. And it is an argument about institutions, not about character. What makes envy productive is the frame it is put in, and nothing in the essay suggests that a person who competes is thereby improved.

That second point is what keeps this page from becoming a page about ambition. Nietzsche's later writing does have a good deal to say about greatness, rank and the noble type, and some of it grows out of the early essay. But "greatness" in Beyond Good and Evil is measured by what a person can bear rather than by whom they have beaten, and the Genealogy's noble is described by a relation to enemies rather than by success in contests. Whether the late material is the agon's descendant or a different idea that has absorbed its vocabulary is a real question, and this page keeps it open.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter runs chronologically from the 1872 essay to the last year of Nietzsche's working life, so that the distance between the Greek argument and the late one stays visible. Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order and in date order: 1872, 1874, 1883, 1886, 1887, 1888. The first two are the Greek argument and an early warning about how it can be misused. The last four are the places where a later vocabulary of nobility and rank takes over, and the question to carry through them is whether the contest is still doing any work.

  1. Core · 1872, unpublished

    Homer's Contest 1872, unpublished

    Envy given an institution

    Free edition: Early Greek Philosophy & Other Essays, trans. Maximilian A. Mügge. This collection has no single matching Kaufmann or Hollingdale edition; check the individual essay title in any modern anthology.

    Question: The essay says the Greeks had two goddesses of strife and thought one of them good. What has to be true of a society for that distinction to hold?

    Why it belongs: The theme's founding text and its most concentrated statement. Nietzsche begins from the cruelty in Greek life rather than from Greek serenity, distinguishes a strife that destroys from a strife that drives people to work, and reads the contest as the institution that keeps the second alive. The argument about ostracism is the sharpest part: a city removes its outstanding man not out of resentment but to keep the contest going, because a rivalry with no possible rival has stopped being a contest. Note three things about its status. Nietzsche did not publish it. It is a classicist's essay written for a particular scholarly quarrel. And its account of what the Greeks were like is an interpretation, not a report — see the section below.

    Read alongside: The Greek State; Untimely Meditations II §2.

    Online edition: Homer's Contest (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

  2. Complication · 1874

    Untimely Meditations II, "On the Use and Disadvantage of History for Life" §2

    The past as a gallery of rivals, and what that does to the living

    Free edition: Thoughts Out of Season, Part II, trans. Adrian Collins, where the essay appears as "The Use and Abuse of History." Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: Monumental history is supposed to encourage. Nietzsche says it also deceives. Which of the two effects can a reader detect in themselves?

    Why it belongs: The section describes the person who looks to past greatness for the assurance that greatness is possible, and treats that as the agonistic relation extended backwards in time — the dead as competitors. It is assigned here because Nietzsche immediately turns on it. Monumental history, he argues, has to smooth away the particular causes that made a past achievement possible in order to present it as repeatable; it can inspire the wrong people; and it can be used to prevent the present from producing anything by insisting that everything worth doing is already done. This is the earliest and most useful check on the whole theme, and it is Nietzsche's own.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations III §§3, 6; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: "The Use and Abuse of History" §2 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  3. Development · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Friend"

    The contest brought inside a relationship

    Common: "The Friend." The free edition is Thus Spake Zarathustra.

    Question: Zarathustra wants the friend to be the best enemy. Is that a description of friendship or a demand that most friendships could not meet?

    Why it belongs: The chapter transfers the agonistic structure from an institution to a private relation: the friend is the one before whom one may not relax, who should be approached in one's best condition rather than one's most comfortable, and who serves as an opponent worth having. It is the most attractive thing on this page and the place to be most careful. The chapter also contains dismissive remarks about what women are supposedly capable of in friendship, which are part of the text and are examined on the women, marriage, and gender page. Read the whole speech rather than the memorable lines.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Way of the Creator"; Genealogy I §§10–11.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Friend" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §§259, 260, 265, 287

    Rank, and a kind of greatness that is not a contest

    Part IX, "What Is Noble?" Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296 across the book's nine parts.

    Question: None of these four sections mentions a contest. What, if anything, has replaced it?

    Why it belongs: The late account of nobility, and the point at which this theme becomes genuinely difficult. §259 generalizes exploitation into a claim about life itself — the most contestable move in the book, and one this site does not endorse. §260 sets out the two types of morality. §265 describes the noble's assumption of its own priority, in a form most readers will find repellent. §287 asks what makes a person noble and answers with an inner relation to oneself rather than with any achievement or victory. Read the four in sequence and notice what has changed since 1872: rivalry has been replaced by rank, the institutional argument by a typology of persons, and the claim about what produces culture by a claim about who is entitled to what. That is a large change, and whether it is a development or a substitution is the question this page leaves open.

    Read alongside: §257; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Part IX (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core · 1887

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§10–11

    Two ways of having an enemy

    Free edition trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each of the three essays are numbered separately.

    Question: Nietzsche says the noble seeks out an enemy worth having. How would that look different, from outside, from ordinary aggression?

    Why it belongs: The closest the late work comes to an analysis of the agonistic relation. §10 contrasts a valuation that begins from itself and treats its opponent as an occasion with one that begins from an enemy and needs him to remain hateful; the first is described as able to let go of an injury, the second as storing it. §11 is where respect for an opponent is presented as characteristic of the first type. It is also the passage in which Nietzsche writes about the predatory violence of noble societies in terms that are deliberately shocking and are examined on the master and slave morality page. Assigned here for the distinction between two relations to an opponent, not as an endorsement of the type described.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §17; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§10–11 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  6. Development · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §7

    Four rules for picking a fight

    Free edition trans. Anthony M. Ludovici. Written in 1888; first published in 1908.

    Question: The rules are restrictive — attack only what is strong, only when one stands alone, never for personal reasons. Does Nietzsche's own late polemic obey them?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche sets out what he calls his practice in war as a small number of explicit rules: to attack only causes that are winning, only where he would find no allies, never with a personal motive behind the public quarrel, and to take on only what is strong enough to make the attack worth making. It is the agonistic structure restated as a personal discipline, and it is the best evidence that he still held the early insight in 1888 — the value of an opponent depends on the opponent being formidable. It is also, written in the same months as The Case of Wagner and The Antichrist, a claim about his own conduct that a reader can test against the books.

    Read alongside: "Why I Am a Destiny" §1; Wagner and decadence.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use Find for §7) · Preferred edition.

What the agon is not

The early essay is unusually easy to convert into advice, and the conversion loses the argument. Five things it does not say:

It is not a claim that competition is good
The argument is about a particular institutional arrangement in a particular society: rules, an audience, a recognized field, roughly matched rivals, and a culture that treated the outcome as a public good. Remove any of those and the mechanism is not present. Nothing in "Homer's Contest" suggests that competition as such produces anything worth having.
It is not a psychology of motivation
Nietzsche is not saying that rivalry makes individuals better people or that ambition is healthy. He is saying that a society which has to deal with envy and destructiveness can build a frame in which those impulses generate poems and statues rather than feuds. The claim is about the frame.
It is not a defence of winning
The ostracism argument runs the other way. A contest that has produced a permanent victor has destroyed itself, because the point was the continued striving of several and not the elevation of one. Read that way, the essay is closer to an argument for plurality than for supremacy.
It is not the late doctrine of rank
Beyond Good and Evil's order of rank is a claim about kinds of person and what they may demand of each other. The agon is a claim about how a society channels an impulse. The second can be true while the first is false, and treating the early essay as an early version of the later politics reads a continuity into the corpus rather than out of it.
It is not an account of modern institutions
Nietzsche is describing games, drama festivals and civic practices in an ancient slave society, and in "The Greek State" he says explicitly that he thinks the culture rested on that slavery. Applying the model to a workplace, a market or a school is an extension a reader may want to make, and it is theirs to defend, not his.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Four investigations to take after the first encounter. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below.

1. The Greek argument, and what it costs

The early essays belong together, and the least comfortable of them is the one that says what the contest was supposed to rest on.

  1. Homer's Contest
    Two kinds of strife, and the ostracism argument.
  2. The Greek State
    The same culture, with its material conditions stated without euphemism.
  3. The Birth of Tragedy — §§7–9
    Greek art treated as an achievement over terror rather than an expression of calm.
  4. Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks
    The pre-Socratics read as rivals rather than as a sequence of approximations.

2. Nietzsche's own warnings

Before extending the model, read the places where he limits it.

  1. Untimely Meditations — II §2
    Monumental history as encouragement and as deception.
  2. Untimely Meditations — III §§3, 6
    What an exemplary figure is for, and what it is not.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Way of the Creator"
    Solitude and self-opposition, where there is no rival to measure against.
  4. Ecce Homo — "Why I Am So Wise" §7
    Restrictions on whom one may attack, stated as rules.

3. Having an enemy

The late analysis of what a relation to an opponent can be, and of what it becomes when the opponent is needed rather than chosen.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §§10–11
    Two valuations, distinguished by their relation to an enemy.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Friend"
    The friend as the opponent one does not want to defeat.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §17
    Founders of states described without any contest at all.
  4. Twilight of the Idols — "Skirmishes" §38
    Freedom measured by resistance overcome, in the late polemical register.

4. What "greatness" comes to mean

Follow the word away from the contest and ask, at each step, what would count as evidence for the claim being made.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §257
    Distance as the precondition of rank, stated as a historical thesis.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §§259, 260, 265, 287
    Nobility described from inside, and greatness detached from victory.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "Skirmishes" §49
    One person offered as the whole argument, in a single portrait.
  4. Ecce Homo — "Why I Am a Destiny" §1
    The agonistic vocabulary turned on an entire civilization.

Keep these distinctions in view

Contest and rivalry
A rivalry is a relation between two people. A contest is an institution with rules, a field and an audience that makes the rivalry legible and repeatable. "Homer's Contest" is about the second, and the whole causal claim depends on the frame rather than on the feelings of the competitors.
Envy and ressentiment
The essay's envy is an open impulse with a named object: one wants to do what the other has done. What the Genealogy calls ressentiment is a blocked, inward and concealed relation to an enemy who is needed for one's own self-description. Nietzsche keeps them apart, and collapsing them makes both arguments unintelligible.
Excellence and victory
What the contest is supposed to produce is achievement — a poem, a statue, a law. Winning is the occasion, not the product, and the ostracism argument exists to say that a permanent winner has broken the mechanism. Readings that make Nietzsche an advocate of dominance have inverted the essay.
Rank and hierarchy
The late order of rank is a claim about kinds of person and their entitlements. The agon is compatible with a rough equality among competitors and in fact requires one. These are different structures, and the late work does not derive the second from the first.
Cultural production and personal improvement
The early argument is that a society gets art and law out of an arrangement. It says nothing about whether the individuals inside it flourish, and the material conditions Nietzsche names in "The Greek State" suggest he thought most of them did not.
The early essay and the late greatness
By 1886 greatness is measured by how much a person can bear, by the distance they can sustain from others, and by an inner relation to themselves. The contest has dropped out. Whether that is the agon internalized or a different idea using the same words is genuinely contested, and this page does not resolve it.
Nietzsche's Greeks and Greek history
Every historical claim on this page is Nietzsche's. The reconstruction of an agonistic culture is an interpretation built from selected sources by a young classicist arguing with the scholarship of his day. See the section below.

Nietzsche's Greeks

This page assigns a great deal of material in which Nietzsche makes claims about what the Greeks were like: that their culture was built on cruelty as much as on measure, that envy was openly acknowledged among them, that the contest was a deliberate cultural instrument, that ostracism was designed to preserve rivalry. These are claims about the past, and they are his.

Three things follow for reading. They were arguments, made against a prevailing nineteenth-century image of Greek serenity that Nietzsche thought sentimental, and they were contested by other classicists at the time — his first book provoked exactly that response. They are built on a selection of sources: Hesiod, Homer, a handful of anecdotes about ostracism and the contests, read with a strong thesis already in view. And they are inseparable from a polemical purpose, since the Greeks are being used throughout as a measure against which Nietzsche's own century is found wanting.

The site's practice is to present these as Nietzsche's claims and to say so where it matters, rather than to certify or refute them. A reader who wants to know what classical scholarship now holds about archaic Greek competition, ostracism or the social place of the games will need to go to that scholarship; this page does not stand in for it, and the passages assigned here are not evidence about ancient Greece independent of Nietzsche's reading of it.

One consequence is practical. Where the argument on this page is strongest — the analysis of two relations to an opponent in Genealogy I §§10–11, the rules in Ecce Homo, the warning about monumental history — it does not depend on the historical reconstruction being correct. Where it depends most heavily on the Greeks, it is most exposed. Keeping track of which is which is the most useful discipline the theme offers.

  • Core · 1872, unpublished

    Homer's Contest 1872, unpublished

    Envy given an institution

    Question: What has to be true of a society for a "good" strife to be distinguishable from a destructive one?

    Why it belongs: The theme's founding text. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Greek State; What the agon is not.

    Online edition: Homer's Contest (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

  • Complication · 1872, unpublished

    The Greek State 1872, unpublished

    What the culture was said to rest on

    In the same Mügge volume as "Homer's Contest"; use the contents of the free edition, which collects several short pieces.

    Question: If a culture's achievements require a class excluded from them, what has been claimed about culture?

    Why it belongs: The companion essay, and the one that makes the early position hard to admire. Nietzsche argues that the leisure required for Greek art and philosophy depended on the labour of slaves, and treats that dependence not as a regrettable historical fact but as a condition of culture as such. It belongs on this page because it is where the institutional argument shows its underside: the frame that made envy productive was built inside a society organized on terms Nietzsche states without apology. Assigned as a complication, unpublished, and not endorsed — but omitting it would leave the agon looking more attractive than the early essays permit.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §257; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Open the volume (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge; use the contents for "The Greek State") · Edition guidance.

  • Extension · 1873, unpublished

    Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks 1873, unpublished

    Philosophers read as competitors rather than as a sequence

    Mügge: Philosophy during the Tragic Age of the Greeks. Left unfinished.

    Question: What changes when early philosophy is described as a contest of personalities rather than as progress toward a correct account?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche treats the pre-Socratics as a succession of distinct types, each imposing a shape on the world, rather than as a series of attempts at one physics. The relevance here is that it applies the agonistic model to intellectual life — the value of each thinker partly a function of the others they were working against. Unpublished and unfinished; the perspectivism page treats its epistemological side.

    Read alongside: Homer's Contest; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Philosophy during the Tragic Age of the Greeks (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

  • Development · 1872

    The Birth of Tragedy §§7–9

    A culture that built its serenity over something else

    Free edition trans. William A. Haussmann; preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: The chapters treat Greek art as a response to terror. How does that bear on the claim about the contest?

    Why it belongs: The published book of the same year, arguing on the same premise: that Greek achievement was not the expression of a naturally balanced people but something made under pressure. It gives the agon essay its context — both texts are refusing the same picture of Greek calm — without repeating its argument. The account of the satyr chorus and of the god behind the tragic mask belongs to Apollonian and Dionysian; take from it here only the premise about what the Greeks were working against.

    Read alongside: Apollonian and Dionysian; Tragedy and aesthetic justification.

    Online edition: §7 (trans. William A. Haussmann; §§8–9 follow) · Preferred edition.

    Untimely Meditations II, "On the Use and Disadvantage of History for Life" §2

    The past as a gallery of rivals, and what that does to the living

    Question: Which of monumental history's two effects can a reader detect in themselves?

    Read alongside: III, §§3, 6; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: "The Use and Abuse of History" §2 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1874

    Untimely Meditations III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §§3, 6

    An exemplar who is not a standard to be matched

    Question: If an educator reveals what one already is, in what sense is the relation competitive at all?

    Why it belongs: §3 describes the educator as someone who liberates rather than instructs, clearing away what is not one's own; §6 argues that what a culture gets from a philosopher is the example of a life rather than a set of results. Together they complicate the agonistic picture in a useful direction: here the relation to a superior figure is not rivalry but release, and the aim is not to surpass the exemplar but to stop imitating anyone. The free spirits page takes the essay's main argument.

    Read alongside: II §2; Free spirits.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On War and Warriors"

    Martial language, and what it is being used for

    Common: "War and Warriors." The chapter falls in Part I between "On the Flies of the Marketplace" and "On the New Idol"; use the contents.

    Question: Zarathustra addresses warriors and then says he does not mean soldiers. Does the disclaimer do the work it needs to?

    Why it belongs: The speech praises enmity, short peace and the search for opponents, and distinguishes the warriors it addresses from men of the army — a distinction on which a great deal depends and which the chapter states rather than establishes. It belongs here because it is the most quoted and most abused agonistic passage in the corpus, and because the abuse is made possible by the text itself: the vocabulary is military, the disclaimer is brief, and the surrounding chapters are hostile to the state rather than to violence. Read it with the disclaimer in view and without assuming it settles the matter.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Wise" §7; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: Part I, "The New Idol" (trans. Thomas Common; "War and Warriors" immediately precedes it — use the contents) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Way of the Creator"

    What is left when there is no rival

    Common: "The Way of the Creating One."

    Question: If one must become one's own opponent, has the contest been internalized or abandoned?

    Why it belongs: The speech describes the person who has separated from the herd and has no one left to measure against: the danger is now oneself, the loneliness is the condition, and the demand is to be able to give oneself one's own law. It is the strongest internal pressure on the agonistic model, because it describes exactly the situation the ostracism argument said was dangerous — and treats it as the creator's ordinary lot rather than as a failure.

    Read alongside: Homer's Contest; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Way of the Creating One" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Much of this material is anti-egalitarian in a way the site states rather than softens; the master and slave morality page carries the main analysis.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §257

    Distance offered as the condition of every elevation

    Part IX, "What Is Noble?"

    Question: The claim is that every raising of the type has come from societies with fixed differences of rank. What evidence could bear on it?

    Why it belongs: The framing section of Part IX, and a historical thesis stated with great confidence: that aristocratic societies, and the distance between orders within them, have been the precondition of anything higher. It belongs on this page as the clearest case of the theme's central risk — an institutional argument about culture, now attached to a claim about social hierarchy rather than to a claim about contest. Read it as an assertion about history that the book does not argue for, and note that the agon required competitors of comparable standing while this requires the opposite.

    Read alongside: The Greek State; The herd and the last man.

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§10–11

    Two ways of having an enemy

    Question: How would the noble's relation to an opponent look different from outside?

    Why it belongs: The late analysis of the agonistic relation, and the distinction between choosing an opponent and needing one. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Friend"; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§10–11 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §17

    A founding with no contest in it

    Question: If states were founded by conquest rather than contract, what follows for the claim that rivalry is culturally productive?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes the earliest state as imposed by an organized band on a formless population, without contract and without resistance that could count as a contest. The passage is frequently quoted for its imagery and frequently misread as praise; in context it is part of the account of how bad conscience became possible. It belongs here as the counter-case: the most consequential cultural formation in the Genealogy is produced by domination, not by the agon, and the two mechanisms are not reconciled anywhere in the published work.

    Read alongside: Suffering and cruelty; Beyond Good and Evil §257.

    Online edition: Essay II §§16–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols3 reading assignments

Section numbers restart in each chapter, and the free edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) uses different chapter titles, given below.

  • Development · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §38

    Freedom measured against resistance

    Ludovici: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age."

    Question: If freedom is measured by the resistance it overcomes, what happens to it when the resistance is removed?

    Why it belongs: The late statement closest to the early argument: Nietzsche defines freedom by what it costs and by the opposition it has to sustain, and attacks the institutions of his century for removing exactly that. It is the agonistic insight in its most portable form — and its most portable form is also its most dangerous one, since stated this abstractly it can be used to defend any hardship whatever. The herd and the last man page examines the political claim.

    Read alongside: Homer's Contest; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes" §38 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §49

    One person offered in place of an argument

    Ludovici: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age."

    Question: Goethe is described as having disciplined himself to wholeness. Is that a contest, and if so with whom?

    Why it belongs: The late portrait of an individual who is said to have made something whole of himself. It shows exactly where greatness has ended up by 1888: not the outcome of a rivalry but an achieved condition, described from outside and offered as a standard. Read it as Nietzsche's construction. It tells you what he took greatness to include, and very little about Goethe. The amor fati page treats the same portrait for what it says about affirmation.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §287; Amor fati.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes" §49 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

    Twilight of the Idols "What I Owe to the Ancients" §§2–3

    The Greeks kept as a measure, sixteen years later

    Ludovici: "Things I Owe to the Ancients."

    Question: Nietzsche credits Thucydides and refuses Plato. What does that tell you about which Greeks the early essay was about?

    Why it belongs: The late return to the material this page begins from, now compressed and openly partisan: a preference for the historians and the Sophists over the philosophers, and the Greeks used as the standard against which his own century is measured. Useful for seeing what survived sixteen years — the premise that Greek culture was hard rather than serene — and what did not: the institutional analysis of the contest is gone, and what remains is an allegiance.

  • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am a Destiny" §1

    An opponent the size of a civilization

    Ludovici: "Why I Am a Fatality." Nietzsche's declaration that he is not a man but dynamite appears in this section; renderings differ between translations.

    Question: The rules in "Why I Am So Wise" §7 require an opponent that can be faced. Is two thousand years of morality such an opponent?

    Why it belongs: The chapter opens by predicting that his name will be attached to a crisis unlike anything in history and describes himself in terms of explosive force. Placed against §7 it is the theme's sharpest internal tension: the discipline of attacking only what is strong has been scaled up until the adversary is an entire moral inheritance, at which point it is no longer clear what contest is being entered or who could referee it. Read the two sections together; neither settles the question.

A note on the notebooks

This page carries no notebook route. The 1872–73 essays at the centre of the theme are unpublished but they are finished pieces of writing that Nietzsche completed and set aside, which is a different thing from a notebook entry; they are labelled as unpublished wherever they are assigned.

The late notebooks do contain material on rank and on the cultivation of a higher type, and it is treated where the published arguments it bears on are set out — chiefly on master and slave morality, which carries the relevant entries with the standard caution. Repeating them here would add length without adding evidence, and on this theme the published books are where the argument actually happens.

The sitewide caution applies if you follow that material: The Will to Power is a posthumous arrangement of notebook entries, its numbering is editorial rather than Nietzsche's, and it brings together notes written at different dates. See the edition guidance, and for the notebooks as written rather than as arranged, Nietzsche Source's critical edition.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The two 1872 essays together. Read Homer's Contest and then The Greek State. The second states what the first leaves out. Does the argument about the contest survive it?
  • Sixteen years of one word. Put Homer's Contest beside Beyond Good and Evil §§259, 260, 265, 287. List what each needs "greatness" to mean. Then ask which one the Goethe portrait belongs to.
  • Two mechanisms, unreconciled. Read Genealogy I §§10–11 and then Essay II §17. One culture-forming process runs on contest, the other on conquest. Does the book choose?
  • Nietzsche against the extension. Take Untimely Meditations II §2 and Zarathustra I, "On the Way of the Creator" as the two internal objections, and see how much of the theme they leave standing.

A question to carry forward

The ostracism argument says a contest is damaged when one competitor becomes unbeatable. Take any field you know well and ask whether that is true of it — and what, if anything, plays the part the Greek assembly is said to have played.
