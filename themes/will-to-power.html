The phrase does three different jobs across the corpus, and they are not equally well supported. Two of them are worked out at length in books Nietzsche published. The third — the one that says the world is will to power — belongs mostly to notebooks he did not publish, and this page keeps that difference visible throughout.

The order matters more than it might seem. A reader who starts from the posthumous compilation called The Will to Power meets a cosmology arranged by editors and numbered by editors, and then reads the published books as though they were applications of it. A reader who starts from Daybreak and Zarathustra meets something else: an account of what drives people, extended into a way of asking historical questions, and only then pushed — tentatively, in the form of a supposition — towards a claim about everything. The second order is the one the published texts support, and it is the order this page uses.

Two cautions before the reading begins. Power here does not mean political domination, and a page that read it that way would get most of the corpus wrong: the artist composing, the ascetic priest governing by weakness, the scholar mastering a problem, and the person who simply wants to be felt by someone are all doing what Nietzsche means by it. Domination of other people is one case, and Nietzsche does discuss it, but it is a case rather than the definition. And the compilation called The Will to Power is not a book Nietzsche wrote. It is an arrangement of notebook entries made after his collapse, numbered editorially. Its entries can show what he was working out in private; they cannot settle what the published books leave open.

Three registers sets out the distinction the rest of the page runs on. The cosmological question takes up the contested one on its own, after the published spine, and notebook material is a short, explicitly secondary appendix.

Begin with six readings, all from books Nietzsche published. The first encounter runs in date order and builds the idea in the order the published corpus builds it: a feeling, a principle of life, a hypothesis, a method of historical reading, a standard of evaluation. No notebook cosmology appears in it. Continue through the fuller route, or use the work survey for a particular book.

Use the preferred translation where possible. Each assignment gives a precise section or chapter and links to a named online translation. Some links open a nearby printed page or a whole part; use the contents or Find for the number or alternative title shown. The headings and explanations are editorial. Translation guidance.

Essential first encounter

Six sections, read in order: 1881, 1883, 1886, 1886, 1887, 1888. The sequence is deliberately published-only. Ask at each step what kind of claim is being made — about a person, about living things, about how to read history, or about what is good — and whether the text supplies the evidence that kind of claim would need.

  1. Core · 1881

    Daybreak §262

    A desire that comfort does not quiet

    Kennedy's free edition heads the section "The Demon of Power." The free translation is J. M. Kennedy; preferred translation R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: The section claims that security and satisfaction do not remove the desire it describes. What would a counterexample look like?

    Why it belongs: Start five years before the phrase is famous. Daybreak works with a feeling of power — Machtgefühl — as an explanatory category in moral psychology, and this section puts the claim in its strongest early form: neither necessity nor desire for material goods accounts for what people pursue, because the wanting continues after both are satisfied. The closing turn to Luther places the same desire inside religious language, where it would seem least at home. Read it as what it is, a sweeping psychological hypothesis offered for testing against cases rather than a completed doctrine, and notice how much of the later idea is already here without the later vocabulary.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §189; Psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §262 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, "On Self-Overcoming"

    Commanding, obeying, and the cost of both

    Common: "Self-Surpassing"; the free edition is Thus Spake Zarathustra. The chapter's best-known sentences about finding will to power wherever there is life are rendered differently by Kaufmann and Common — compare the two rather than quoting one as the other.

    Question: Zarathustra says the one who commands also obeys, and risks more in commanding. What does that add to a claim that living things seek power?

    Why it belongs: The chapter where the phrase is named and given its content, and the reason the psychological register is not simply a theory of dominance. What Zarathustra describes is a structure inside the living thing: something in it commands, something obeys, and the commanding is itself an exposure, since a command that fails destroys the one who gave it. He then finds the same structure between living things — the weaker serving the stronger, and the stronger in turn serving something still weaker that it wants. The picture is of organization under strain rather than of one party subduing another, and it is the picture Beyond Good and Evil will restate in prose. Read the whole speech; the aphoristic lines that circulate from it lose the argument.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §13; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Part II, "Self-Surpassing" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §13 · Part I

    Self-preservation demoted to a consequence

    Part I, "Prejudices of Philosophers" (§§1–23). The free edition is divided by part, so the link opens Part I; use Find for the section number. Free translation Helen Zimmern; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: Nietzsche calls self-preservation one of the indirect and most frequent results of what a living thing does. What would count as evidence for that ordering rather than the reverse?

    Why it belongs: Four sentences that set the terms for everything after. A living thing, Nietzsche writes, seeks above all to discharge its strength; life itself is will to power, and self-preservation follows as a consequence rather than standing as the aim. The target is named: he calls the self-preservation principle a superfluous teleological principle, and takes it to be a piece of inherited philosophy rather than an observation. Note what the section does and does not do. It states a position with unusual compression and it identifies what it rejects. It does not argue for the position, and Nietzsche gives it as a thesis about method — economy of principles — as much as a thesis about organisms.

    Read alongside: §36; Twilight, "Skirmishes" §14.

    Online edition: Part I: locate §13 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §36 · Part II

    The experiment, stated as a supposition

    Part II, "The Free Spirit" (§§24–44) — a different part from §13, and a separate link below. Zimmern's free edition opens the section "Supposing that nothing else is 'given' as real but our world of desires and passions"; Kaufmann's English differs, and the two should not be run together.

    Question: The section is built entirely out of conditionals. What has been shown by the end of it, and what has only been proposed?

    Why it belongs: The most careful passage on this page, and the one most often quoted as though it were a conclusion. The whole section is a supposition: supposing nothing were given as real but our drives, supposing thinking were only a relation among them, then it would be a matter of method — the conscience of method, Nietzsche says — to try whether one kind of causality might explain the rest, rather than helping oneself to several. Only after that chain does the formula arrive, and it arrives as what the world would be called if the experiment succeeded. Nietzsche does not claim the experiment has succeeded. Read the grammar as carefully as the content; it is the difference between a hypothesis and a physics, and most of the dispute recorded on this page turns on it.

    Read alongside: §22; the cosmological question.

    Online edition: Part II: locate §36 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core · 1887

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II §12

    Why a practice outlives the purpose it was made for

    Free translation Horace B. Samuel; the link opens the second essay, so use Find for the section number. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: If a later power can take over an existing practice and give it a new purpose, what happens to explanations that read a practice's present function back into its origin?

    Why it belongs: The register that does the most work in the late books, and the one that can actually be tested. Nietzsche separates two questions that moral history habitually runs together: how a thing arose, and what it is now for. Whatever exists, he argues, is repeatedly reinterpreted by whatever gains power over it, so that the chain of purposes a practice has served is a succession of appropriations rather than a development of one intention. Punishment is the worked example, and it is chosen because its present meaning looks so obviously like its origin. Two things to hold apart while reading. The distinction between origin and function is a genuine methodological gain, usable by a reader who accepts nothing else here. The stronger interpretation of life as essentially appropriation and overpowering, and the ranking of sacrifices to exceptional types as progress, are separate claims that need independent assessment — and the second is where the register touches the politics.

    Read alongside: Essay III §27; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Second Essay: locate §12 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core · 1888

    The Antichrist §2

    The idea turned into a standard of value

    Free translation H. L. Mencken; the link opens the work, so use Find for the section number. The section's famous answers to "what is good?" are worded quite differently by Kaufmann and by Mencken, whose English speaks of what augments the feeling of power; this page paraphrases rather than presenting one translator's sentence under the other's link. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: A standard has to be able to rule things out. What does this one rule out, and who decides?

    Why it belongs: The last step because it is the least careful, and seeing that is the point of putting it here. Nietzsche answers three questions in a few lines: what is good is what raises the feeling of power and the will to power in a human being; what is bad is what comes from weakness; happiness is the feeling that power is growing and resistance is being overcome. Everything the earlier steps built — a hypothesis about drives, a supposition about method, a way of separating origin from function — is here compressed into a criterion and used to open a polemic. Read it against Beyond Good and Evil §36 and ask what has happened to the conditionals. The slogan is memorable and it is doing far less argumentative work than the sections behind it; treat it as the late evaluative use of the idea rather than as its summary.

    Read alongside: §6; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: The Antichrist: locate §2 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Three registers

Three different claims travel under one phrase. They are related, and Nietzsche moves between them without always marking the move, but they are not equally established and they would not be refuted by the same evidence. The first two are worked out in books he published. The third is mostly notebook material, and the published texts that approach it do so as a supposition.

1. Psychological — what moves a person
The claim that what people pursue is best explained by an expansion of capacity and a sense of their own effectiveness, rather than by pleasure, comfort or survival. It begins in Daybreak as a feeling of power, is dramatized in Zarathustra as commanding and obeying inside a single creature, and is applied in the late books to cases that seem to refute it — pity, benevolence, asceticism, self-denial. It is a hypothesis about motivation, and it can be argued with by producing motives it does not fit. This register is well represented in the published works.
2. Methodological — how to read a practice, a morality, a text
The claim that institutions and valuations should be read as successive interpretations imposed by whatever gained power over them, so that a thing's present purpose is no evidence of its origin. Genealogy II §12 states it; the Genealogy as a whole is it being used. This is the most defensible register on the page, and the one a reader can adopt as a working method without committing to any thesis about the universe. It is also where the idea turns on itself: Genealogy III §27 applies it to the will to truth, including Nietzsche's own.
3. Cosmological — what the world is
The claim that everything that is, is will to power, and that what looks like matter, causation or law is a relation among quanta of force. In the published works this appears twice and both times in the conditional: Beyond Good and Evil §22 offers it as a rival interpretation while conceding that it is one, and §36 reaches it only at the end of a chain of suppositions. The unhedged formulations are in notebooks Nietzsche did not publish. How much weight the register can bear is a live disagreement among readers, and this page does not settle it — see the cosmological question.

One consequence is worth stating plainly, because it shapes how the rest of the page is arranged. A reader who finds the third register unpersuasive has not thereby disposed of the first two, and a reader persuaded by the first two has not thereby acquired the third. They stand or fall separately, and Nietzsche's published prose is considerably more careful about that than the compilation's title suggests.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Four investigations to take after the first encounter. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below.

1. The psychological register, tested on hard cases

The hypothesis is only interesting if it can be applied where it looks least likely to hold.

  1. Daybreak — §113
    The early extended portrait of the drive to be felt by others.
  2. The Gay Science — §13
    Pity and benevolence read as ways of acting on another person.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, "On Self-Overcoming"
    Commanding and obeying inside one creature.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §15
    Weakness that governs, which is the case the hypothesis most needs.

2. The methodological register

The most careful material here, and the part a reader can use without accepting anything about the universe.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §36
    The experiment, and the economy of principles that motivates it.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §12
    Origin separated from function, with punishment as the example.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §186
    Moralities treated as evidence about their holders rather than as answers.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27
    The method turned on the will to truth, Nietzsche's included.

3. Life, organization, and the political edge

Where the account of living activity becomes a claim about how people may treat each other. Do not read past the difficulty.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §13
    Discharge of strength as primary, self-preservation as a result.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §259
    Life described through appropriation and injury, and used politically.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "Skirmishes" §14
    Against a picture of life organized by scarcity and survival.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, "On the Tarantulas"
    A demand for equality read as the thing it claims to oppose.

4. The late evaluative use

What happens to a hypothesis when it becomes a standard, and what the standard is then used to do.

  1. The Antichrist — §2
    The criterion, compressed to three answers.
  2. The Antichrist — §6
    Pity assessed by that criterion, in its harshest application.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "Morality as Anti-Nature" §6
    The same standard stated negatively, against morality that opposes life.
  4. Ecce Homo — "Why I Am a Destiny" §1
    The criterion restated as a personal vocation.

Keep these distinctions in view

Power and domination over other people
Domination is one form the idea covers, not its meaning. If it were the meaning, most of the late analysis would collapse: the ascetic priest, who governs by weakness, and the self-denying believer, whose renunciation Nietzsche reads as a way of exercising power on oneself, would be counterexamples rather than the hypothesis's best cases. Read "power" as effectiveness, capacity, and the extension of what a thing can do — and then notice separately, without softening it, that Nietzsche does sometimes draw political conclusions from it.
A hypothesis and a demonstration
Beyond Good and Evil §36 is built out of suppositions and calls its own procedure a matter of method. Quoting its last clause as a thesis removes the argument that produced it. Nothing in the published works claims the experiment has been carried through.
Life is will to power / the world is will to power
The first is a claim about organisms and appears without hedging at Beyond Good and Evil §13. The second is a claim about everything, and in the published works it is offered conditionally or as an avowed interpretation. The step from the first to the second is exactly the step the notebooks take and the books do not.
Feeling of power and will to power
Daybreak's Machtgefühl is something a person can have, notice and pursue. The later phrase names what is supposed to produce it. Nietzsche does not always keep them apart, and The Antichrist §2 in particular runs them together in a single sentence; a reader who keeps them apart will see more of the argument.
Published register and notebook register
Where the two diverge, the published text governs. The notebooks can sharpen a published claim, preview one, complicate one, or overstate one. They cannot settle a question the books leave open, and the strongest cosmological formulations are precisely where the divergence is widest.
Explanation and endorsement
Reading an institution as an expression of power is an analysis. Judging that it is therefore good is a further step that needs its own argument. The late books take that step, and the reader should be able to see exactly where — usually at the point where the vocabulary shifts from "is" to "raises" or "diminishes".
Nietzsche's compilation and Nietzsche's book
He planned a major work under a title of this kind and abandoned the plan; the published books of 1888 are what he did with the material instead. The Will to Power as it circulates was assembled after his collapse from notebooks, and its numbering was given by editors. See notebook material.

The cosmological question

The strong reading holds that will to power is Nietzsche's account of what there is: not a psychology extended by analogy, but a claim that the world consists of quanta of force in relations of command and resistance, with matter, substance and causal law as interpretations laid over that. Something like this is certainly present in the corpus. The question is what supports it.

In the published works it surfaces twice, and both times with the argument's provisional character on the surface. Beyond Good and Evil §22 attacks the physicists' "conformity of nature to law" as bad philology — a reading of the world produced by democratic instincts rather than by the evidence — and then proposes the counter-reading, while conceding in the same breath that someone will object that this too is only interpretation, and answering: so much the better. §36 arrives at the formula at the end of a chain of suppositions. Neither passage asserts the cosmology flatly, and a reading that treats them as doing so has to explain away their grammar.

The unhedged statements are in notebooks Nietzsche did not publish, and the best known of them is the entry usually cited as KSA 11, 38[12] / WTP §1067 — where §1067 is an editorial number in the expanded posthumous compilation rather than an original notebook number, and KSA 11, 38[12] is the securely known manuscript reference. It describes the world as a monster of energy, without beginning or end, eternally creating and destroying itself, named at the close as will to power. It is a remarkable piece of writing. It is also an unpublished note, and its position at the very end of the compilation, which makes it read like a conclusion, is an editorial decision rather than Nietzsche's.

Readers disagree about what follows, and the disagreement is worth preserving rather than resolving. One tradition, associated above all with Heidegger, treats the cosmological claim as the centre of Nietzsche's thought and the published books as its partial expression. Another, represented by readers such as Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter, holds that the notebooks record experiments Nietzsche declined to publish, and that a naturalistic psychology is what the books actually deliver. The texts underdetermine the choice. What can be said without taking a side is narrower and more useful: the psychological and methodological registers are established in works Nietzsche saw into print, the cosmological register is not, and any reading that leans on the third should say that it is leaning on notebooks. Compare interpretive approaches.

Daybreak3 reading assignments

1881, and the book most often skipped by readers who associate the idea only with the late polemics. Here it is moral psychology, the vocabulary is feeling of power rather than will to power, and the claims are offered as hypotheses about cases. Free edition trans. J. M. Kennedy; preferred translation R. J. Hollingdale.

  • Development · 1881

    Daybreak §113

    Wanting to be felt by someone

    Locate this one by number: the link opens at §109 and §113 follows a few pages later. Headings differ between Kennedy's free edition and Hollingdale's, so match by section number rather than by title.

    Question: The section describes a long ladder of ways to make an impression on others. Is the ladder a description of a drive, or of a society that rewards one?

    Why it belongs: The most extended early treatment of the striving to stand out and to be registered by other people, worked through a sequence of increasingly refined forms — from the crudest display to renunciations that impress precisely by refusing to impress. It is the passage that shows how much of the later idea was in place before the later vocabulary, and it is useful here for a specific reason: the ladder ends in ascetic and self-denying forms, which is where a simple theory of dominance would have nothing to say.

    Read alongside: §262; Genealogy III §15.

    Online edition: opens at §109; locate §113 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1881

    Daybreak §189

    The same desire, at the scale of a nation

    Kennedy heads the section "High Politics."

    Question: If a population can enjoy a feeling of power it has not exercised, what does that do to the hypothesis as an account of individuals?

    Why it belongs: The political case, and the reason this page insists that domination is one form of the idea rather than its definition — because here it really is domination that is in view. Nietzsche examines the desire to feel powerful in populations as well as rulers, and argues that national enthusiasm can sustain conquest while obscuring the injustice of it, with conquerors and conquered evaluating the same events quite differently. Read it as a check on the theme rather than as an illustration of it: it shows the analysis working on collective identification, and it shows how quickly an account of a drive becomes an account of what people will permit themselves to admire.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §259; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: §189 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

    Question: What would a counterexample to this claim look like?

    Why it belongs: The strongest early statement, and the origin of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

Books I–IV are from 1882; Book V was added in 1887. The middle-period statements are the sharpest test of the psychological register, because they are applied to affects that look like its refutation. Free edition: Thomas Common's The Joyful Wisdom; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann. Book V's §360, on distinguishing the cause of an action from the force that drives it, is the methodological companion to these and is worth finding by number.

  • Development · 1882

    The Gay Science §13 · Book I

    Doing good and doing harm as two ways of reaching someone

    Question: Does describing benefaction as an exercise of power impugn it, or only redescribe it?

    Why it belongs: The most economical middle-period statement, and the one that shows what the hypothesis costs. Nietzsche puts benefiting and harming side by side as ways of exercising power on other people, distinguished by whom they are available to: we benefit those already in some way dependent on us, and harm those we need to make feel us at all. The argument is uncomfortable by design, and it is also genuinely arguable — a reader can ask whether the redescription fits cases of help given anonymously, or at cost, or to people who will never know. It is assigned here as the test case, not as a verdict. This section is also central to suffering and cruelty.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §262; The Antichrist §6.

    Online edition: §13 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1887

    The Gay Science §349 · Book V

    Against explaining life by scarcity

    Locate by number: the link opens at §347 and §349 follows shortly after.

    Question: Nietzsche treats the struggle for existence as an exception rather than the rule. What observation would settle that?

    Why it belongs: The middle-period form of an objection the late books repeat: that a biology built on self-preservation describes distress rather than life, and that the naturalists who framed it were generalizing from the conditions of their own society. What Nietzsche puts in its place is abundance — expenditure, even wasteful expenditure — as the normal case. Read it as the bridge between Beyond Good and Evil §13 and the late "Skirmishes" §14, and read it sceptically: the claim about what nature is mostly like is an empirical one, and Nietzsche argues it by assertion and by attacking his opponents' motives rather than by evidence.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "Skirmishes" §14; Beyond Good and Evil §13.

    Online edition: opens at §347; locate §349 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1887

    The Gay Science §360 · Book V

    Two things that both get called the cause

    The link opens Book V; use Find for the section number.

    Question: If the quantity of force and the direction it takes have different explanations, which one does a person's stated reason usually explain?

    Why it belongs: The section that separates the driving force of an action from the steering of it — the reservoir of strength that has to be discharged, and the occasion that decides where it goes. Nietzsche's point is that people routinely mistake the second for the first, and give the occasion, the goal, the purpose as the explanation when it only selected a channel. This is the psychological register supplying the methodological one with its warrant, and it is the closest thing in the published work to a statement of why the hypothesis is supposed to explain better than a theory of aims.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §12; Beyond Good and Evil §36.

    Online edition: Book V: locate §360 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra4 reading assignments

1883–85, and where the phrase is named. The dramatic form is not decoration: what the speeches supply is a picture of the idea working inside a single creature and between creatures, which the later prose then states more flatly. Free edition trans. Thomas Common, titled Thus Spake Zarathustra, whose chapter titles differ; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

  • Development · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, "On the Thousand and One Goals"

    A people's table of goods, read as its history of overcomings

    Common: "On a Thousand and One Goals."

    Question: Zarathustra surveys four peoples and finds four incompatible tables. Does that support his explanation of where tables come from, or only show that they differ?

    Why it belongs: The first place where valuations themselves are attributed to will to power. Zarathustra looks at what different peoples have called good, finds that each table records what that people found hard and mastered, and concludes that a table of goods is the voice of a will to power. This is the dramatic form of what Genealogy II §12 will state as method, and it arrives with the same ambiguity: it is offered as an explanation of value, and it is also itself a valuation. The closing turn — that so far there have been a thousand goals and no single one — hands the problem to the rest of the book.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §12; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: Part I, "A Thousand and One Goals" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, "On the Tarantulas"

    A demand for equality read as the thing it opposes

    Common: "The Tarantulas."

    Question: Is this an analysis of a motive or a way of refusing to answer an argument?

    Why it belongs: The hypothesis used polemically, and the place to see what that costs. Zarathustra reads the preachers of equality as moved by revenge — their justice is the wish that those who stand higher should be brought down — and so treats a political claim as a symptom of the very drive it denies. The move is instructive twice over. It shows the analysis reaching valuations that present themselves as impartial, which is its real ambition. And it shows the analysis being used to avoid an argument: that someone demands equality out of resentment, even if true, would not tell you whether they are owed it. Nietzsche's anti-egalitarian claims are examined at length on the herd and the last man.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §15; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Part II, "The Tarantulas" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, "On Self-Overcoming"

    Commanding, obeying, and the cost of both

    Common: "Self-Surpassing." The chapter's best-known sentences are rendered differently by Kaufmann and Common; compare rather than quoting one as the other.

    Question: What does the claim that commanding is harder than obeying add to the theme?

    Why it belongs: The chapter where the phrase is named and given its content. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §13; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Part II, "Self-Surpassing" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1884

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, "On Old and New Tables"

    The creator described as someone who must also destroy

    Common: "Old and New Tables." A long chapter in numbered parts; use the contents.

    Question: If making new values requires breaking old ones, who is entitled to decide which are broken?

    Why it belongs: The late Zarathustra chapter that gathers what the theme is for, and the point at which the analysis becomes a programme. The claim that a creator of values must first be an annihilator follows directly from the account of valuation in Part I: if tables of goods are deposits of earlier overcomings, then making a new one is not an addition but a displacement. The chapter is assigned as an extension rather than a core reading because its force is rhetorical and its content is largely stated more carefully elsewhere; it belongs here because it is where the psychological register and the evaluative use meet. Treated at length on revaluation of values.

    Read alongside: I, "On the Thousand and One Goals"; Ecce Homo, "Why I Am a Destiny" §1.

    Online edition: Part III, "Old and New Tables" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil7 reading assignments

1886, and the book where the idea is stated most carefully. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296 across nine parts; the free edition is divided by part, so each assignment below names its part and you will need Find for the number.   • Complication · Part I

    Beyond Good and Evil §22 · Part I

    Laws of nature called bad philology

    Part I, "Prejudices of Philosophers" (§§1–23).

    Question: Nietzsche anticipates the objection that his counter-reading is also only an interpretation, and welcomes it. Can a position survive being granted that?

    Why it belongs: The most controlled of the published gestures towards the cosmological register, and the one that shows how Nietzsche wanted such gestures taken. He argues that the physicists' talk of nature's conformity to law is not a reading of the evidence but a democratic interpretation projected onto it, and offers a rival reading on which the same phenomena show a tyrannically ruthless and inexorable enforcement of power-claims. Then he concedes the obvious objection before it is made. That concession is the passage's real content: it marks the claim as interpretation competing with interpretation, not as physics replacing physics, and it is the strongest published evidence about how much weight the third register was meant to bear.

    Read alongside: §36; the cosmological question; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Part I: locate §22 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

    Beyond Good and Evil §36 · Part II

    The experiment, stated as a supposition

    Part II, "The Free Spirit" (§§24–44) — not Part I, where §13 and §22 sit. Zimmern opens it "Supposing that nothing else is 'given' as real but our world of desires and passions"; Kaufmann's English differs.

    Question: What has been shown by the end of the section, and what has only been proposed?

    Why it belongs: The hypothesis in its most careful published form. See the first encounter for the full explanation, and the cosmological question for what rests on its grammar.

  • Extension · Part II

    Beyond Good and Evil §44 · Part II

    The philosophers of the future, and what they are free from

    Part II, "The Free Spirit" (§§24–44), where it is the closing section. Earlier versions of this page paired §44 with §259; they are in different parts and make different claims, and are given separately here.

    Question: Nietzsche distinguishes his "free spirits" from the levellers who use the same name. Is the distinction one of aims or of temperament?

    Why it belongs: The section belongs to this theme through the figure it describes rather than through any statement about life or the world. The coming philosophers are characterized by what they can bear: hardness towards themselves, a taste for testing convictions rather than holding them, and a refusal of the comfortable freedoms the levelling free-thinkers of the day were demanding. That is the psychological register applied reflexively, to the kind of person who could carry out the enquiry the book proposes. Read it as a portrait with a purpose — Nietzsche is recruiting — and note that the recruitment includes explicit contempt for democratic aims, treated on free spirits and the herd and the last man.

    Read alongside: §211; Free spirits.

    Online edition: Part II: locate §44 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · Part V

    Beyond Good and Evil §186 · Part V

    Moralities as evidence about the people who hold them

    Part V, "On the Natural History of Morals" (§§186–203), where it is the opening section.

    Question: Nietzsche says philosophers have tried to give morality a foundation when the task was to describe it. Is the description he proposes free of the valuation he is criticizing?

    Why it belongs: The charter for the method. Moralities, on this account, are sign-languages of the affects: what a morality forbids and demands tells you about the condition of those who hold it, and the philosophers who thought they were founding morality were only stating their own in a learned form. What makes the section matter here is that it converts the psychological hypothesis into an instruction for reading, and hands that instruction to the Genealogy. It is also where the reflexive problem becomes unavoidable, since the instruction applies to Nietzsche's own morality too.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §12; Psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Part V: locate §186 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · Part VI

    Beyond Good and Evil §211 · Part VI

    Knowing described as legislating

    Part VI, "We Scholars" (§§204–213).

    Question: If a philosopher's knowing is a kind of creating, what stops any assertion from qualifying?

    Why it belongs: The most direct published statement that the pursuit of truth is itself an exercise of the drive the book has been describing. Nietzsche separates philosophical labourers, who systematize what has been achieved, from philosophers proper, whose determining of value is a commanding and whose will to truth he identifies with will to power. Read it beside the Genealogy's third essay, which makes the same identification with far more argument, and notice the risk the section runs: once knowing is legislating, the difference between a philosopher and a propagandist has to be supplied by something the passage does not name.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §27; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Part VI: locate §211 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Part IX

    Beyond Good and Evil §259 · Part IX

    Life described as appropriation, and the political conclusion drawn from it

    Part IX, "What Is Noble?" (§§257–296) — a different part from §44, and a separate assignment. Read §§257–258 first; the argument depends on them.

    Question: The section moves from a claim about what life is to a claim about what a society may do. Where exactly does the move happen, and what licenses it?

    Why it belongs: The hardest passage on this page, and the one that should not be read past. Nietzsche grants that refraining from injury and exploitation can be good manners among equals within a body, and then argues that the body itself must do to other bodies what its members refrain from doing to each other — that life is essentially appropriation, injury, overpowering of the alien and weaker, and that exploitation is a consequence of the will to power rather than a corruptible feature of a particular society. This is the point at which the account of living activity becomes a political claim, and this site does not endorse it. Two things are worth separating while reading: whether the description of organisms is accurate, and whether anything about how human beings may treat each other follows from it even if it is. Nietzsche runs them together; a reader need not. Further treatment on master and slave morality.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §189; Genealogy II §12.

    Online edition: Part IX: locate §259 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals4 reading assignments

1887, and the book in which the methodological register is not stated but used. Free translation Horace B. Samuel; the links open whole essays, so use Find for section numbers. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale.

  • Development · Essay I

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I §13

    Whether a doer can stand apart from the deed

    Question: Identify the grammatical argument, then assess the analogy with birds and lambs. Do they carry the same weight?

    Why it belongs: The section that clears the ground the hypothesis needs. Nietzsche argues that the subject who could have acted otherwise than its strength dictates is an artefact of grammar, invented by the weak so that restraint can be counted as merit; behind the deed there is no neutral doer choosing it. If that is right, then explaining an action by a drive is not leaving out a decision-maker, because there was none to leave out. Two cautions. The grammatical argument and the animal analogy are doing different work, and the analogy is the weaker. And rejecting a wholly separate doer does not establish that human restraint is impossible or that objecting to injury is unreasonable. Treated more fully on critique of metaphysics.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §36; Recurrence, time, becoming.

    Online edition: First Essay: locate §13 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core · Essay II

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II §12

    Why a practice outlives the purpose it was made for

    Question: What happens to explanations that read a practice's present function back into its origin?

    Why it belongs: The methodological register stated outright, with punishment as the worked case. See the first encounter for the full explanation, including the stronger claims in the same section that require separate assessment.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §186; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Second Essay: locate §12 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Essay III

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §15

    Power exercised by the one who has least of it

    Question: If weakness can govern, what is left of the distinction between strong and weak that the rest of the book relies on?

    Why it belongs: The case the hypothesis most needs, and the best evidence that "power" here does not mean domination in the ordinary sense. The ascetic priest does not command by force; he takes the resentment of the suffering and redirects it onto the sufferers themselves, giving their condition a moral interpretation and himself an indispensable role. That is an exercise of power by someone the book has been calling weak, and it is the most fully worked application of the analysis in the corpus. Read it with the section's difficulty attached: the hierarchy of healthy and sick that frames it, including a misogynistic passage in §14, is part of the argument and is examined on critique of Christianity and women, marriage, and gender.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra II, "On the Tarantulas"; Daybreak §113.

    Online edition: Third Essay: locate §15 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Essay III

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §27

    The method applied to the person using it

    Question: Does an enquiry that explains the value of truth by a drive undermine its own results?

    Why it belongs: The place where the theme becomes reflexive and therefore serious. Having argued that unconditional devotion to truth continues the ascetic ideal rather than opposing it, Nietzsche follows truthfulness into criticism of its own authority — and his own enquiry is part of what is being criticized. For this page the significance is specific: the will to truth is analysed as a form of the drive the book has been tracking, which means the analysis cannot exempt itself. Whether that is a fatal circularity or the honest consequence of the position is a question the section raises and does not close.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Third Essay: locate §27 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols3 reading assignments

1888, and compressed. Section numbers restart in each chapter; Ludovici's free edition uses different chapter titles, given with each assignment. The links open chapters, so use Find for section numbers. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

  • Development · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–6

    Clearing away what the hypothesis would otherwise have to accommodate

    Ludovici: "'Reason' in Philosophy."

    Question: If the categories of reason are useful fictions, what is the status of the drives the hypothesis appeals to?

    Why it belongs: Negative work that the theme depends on. Nietzsche attacks the philosophers' hatred of becoming and their preference for what does not change, argues that the senses do not lie about change but are contradicted by the reason imposed on them, and traces the belief in being to a confidence in grammar. The relevance here is structural: an account of the world as forces in relation needs the substances, subjects and enduring things of ordinary metaphysics to be removable, and this chapter is where the removing is done. The same chapter also raises the obvious objection to the hypothesis, since drives look like exactly the kind of posit the chapter is suspicious of. Treated at length on critique of metaphysics.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §13; Recurrence, time, becoming.

    Online edition: "'Reason' in Philosophy" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Morality as Anti-Nature" §6

    The standard stated in the negative

    Ludovici: "Morality as the Enemy of Nature."

    Question: Can a morality be condemned for being against life without a prior account of what life requires?

    Why it belongs: The evaluative use in its most formulaic late shape: a morality that condemns the affects as such is read as an expression of declining life rather than as a mistake about ethics, and is called a formula for decadence. The assignment belongs here because it shows the criterion of The Antichrist §2 doing work before that book states it, and because it exposes the structure of the argument clearly enough to be challenged — everything depends on the standard of ascending and descending life, which is asserted rather than defended.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §2; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: "Morality as the Enemy of Nature": locate §6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes" §14

    Against explaining life by the struggle to survive

    Ludovici: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age." The section is headed "Anti-Darwin."

    Question: Nietzsche argues that numbers, cunning and patience often defeat the exceptional individual. Does that tell against evolutionary theory, or against his own standard?

    Why it belongs: The late restatement of the objection, and a useful place to see the theme's limits. Nietzsche challenges a picture of life organized chiefly by scarcity and a struggle for existence, stressing abundance and rivalry for power instead, and then argues that survival does not favour the types he admires, since cunning, cooperation and sheer numbers can defeat an isolated superior individual. Note what the section does not do. It does not refute evolutionary theory, and it cannot: biological fitness was never offered as a certificate of artistic or intellectual superiority, so showing that the two come apart is not a discovery about biology. The claims that weakness generally prevails and that abundance is life's normal condition need evidence the section does not supply.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §349; Beyond Good and Evil §13.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age": locate §14 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888

    The Antichrist §6

    The criterion applied to pity

    Mencken's English speaks of the tonic passions that augment the energy of the feeling of aliveness; Kaufmann renders the same argument differently.

    Question: The argument is that pity multiplies suffering and preserves what is declining. What evidence would tell against it?

    Why it belongs: The criterion doing its harshest work, and the clearest case for reading the late evaluative register with resistance. Nietzsche treats pity as a depressant that drains strength, spreads suffering by contagion, and preserves forms of life he judges to be in decline. The analysis is continuous with The Gay Science §13, but the change of register is total: what was there a redescription of a motive is here a verdict on whether a disposition should exist. The implications about which lives are worth preserving are ethically serious and this site does not follow them; the passage is assigned because the theme cannot be understood without seeing where it goes.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §13; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: The Antichrist: locate §6 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo3 reading assignments

Written in 1888, published in 1908. Nietzsche's retrospect on his own books, which makes it the nearest thing to an authorial gloss on the theme and also the least disinterested source for it. Ludovici's free edition titles the chapters "Why I Am So Wise", "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" and "Why I Am a Fatality". Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

  • Extension · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §6

    Being unable to hold a grudge, offered as evidence of strength

    Question: Is freedom from resentment a sign of the condition Nietzsche describes, or a claim he is making about himself?

    Why it belongs: The personal counterpart of the criterion. Nietzsche presents his own inability to sustain resentment — the quickness with which an injury is finished — as a physiological fact rather than a virtue, and as the mark of a constitution with enough force to be done with things. The passage is useful here because it shows the theme's vocabulary at its most intimate and at its least testable: a claim about someone's own inner economy, made by that person, in a book of self-presentation. Read it with that status attached.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §15; Master and slave morality.

  • Development · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Write Such Good Books — Beyond Good and Evil"

    The author's account of what the 1886 book was doing

    Ludovici: "Why I Write Such Excellent Books"; the chapter on Beyond Good and Evil sits within it and the link opens that chapter.

    Question: Nietzsche calls the book a critique of modernity and describes it as looking downward. Does that match what §36 actually does?

    Why it belongs: The closest the published corpus comes to a late authorial commentary on the theme, and a good test of retrospective accounts generally. The chapter is short, it is written two years after the book and in a very different key, and it emphasizes the polemical and aristocratic aspects rather than the methodological caution.   • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am a Destiny" §1

    The criterion restated as a personal vocation

    Ludovici: "Why I Am a Fatality." The declaration that he is not a man but dynamite appears in this section; renderings differ between translations.

    Question: What would it take to assess a claim pitched at this scale?

    Why it belongs: The last word, and a limit case. The chapter opens by predicting that Nietzsche's name will be attached to a crisis without precedent and describes him in terms of explosive force, and it transfers the whole evaluative apparatus onto a single person and a single collision with an entire moral inheritance. It belongs to this theme as the endpoint of the drift the page tracks: from a hypothesis about motives, through a method of reading, to a criterion, to a destiny. Read it beside Genealogy II §12, written a year earlier by the same author working far more carefully.

Notebook material

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. He planned a major work under a title of this kind and abandoned the plan, and the books of 1888 are what he did with the material instead. The entries can show what he was working out in private. They do not establish what the published works claim, and they should not be used to settle a question the published works leave open.

References below use the expanded 1,067-section compilation, followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and by the linked Ludovici translation. A manuscript reference is given only where it is securely known. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different dates. Edition guidance.

Four entries, offered as an appendix rather than as a second route through the theme. They are here because readers meet them attributed to Nietzsche as doctrine, and because a page about this idea that omitted them would be avoiding the problem rather than addressing it. There is no short starting sequence for them: the published texts above are the route, and these are supplementary. Where a notebook entry and a published text pull in different directions, the published text governs.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    KSA 11, 38[12] / The Will to Power §1067

    The world described as force, and named at the end

    WTP §1067 is an editorial number in the expanded posthumous compilation, not an original notebook number; the manuscript reference KSA 11, 38[12] is the securely known one for this entry. Free translation Anthony M. Ludovici.

    Question: The entry is composed as a crescendo. How much of its force is argument and how much is arrangement?

    Why it belongs: The most famous formulation of the cosmological register, and the one most often quoted as Nietzsche's settled view. The entry describes the world as a quantity of force without beginning or end, neither growing nor diminishing, eternally creating and destroying itself, and closes by naming it will to power — and naming the reader as that too. Three things to hold onto. It is a note Nietzsche did not publish. Its placement at the very end of the compilation, which makes it read like a conclusion, is an editorial decision. And nothing in the published works asserts this without hedging; the nearest published relatives, Beyond Good and Evil §22 and §36, both frame the claim as interpretation or supposition.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §36; Recurrence, time, becoming.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    The Will to Power §1066

    An argument sketched, not completed

    Question: What would the argument need in order to work, and does the entry supply it?

    Why it belongs: The entry that sketches the inference from a finite quantity of force through a finite number of states across infinite time to the conclusion that states must recur. It matters to this page because it is where the cosmological register and eternal recurrence are supposed to join, and because it shows the register at its most exposed: the assumptions about finitude and about what would count as a repeated state are asserted rather than defended. Nietzsche neither completed nor published it. The recurrence side of this material is treated on eternal recurrence and recurrence, time, becoming.

    Read alongside: §1067; Eternal recurrence.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    The Will to Power §668

    A working note on drives and organic life

    Question: Does describing a drive as a quantum of force explain anything the published psychology does not?

    Why it belongs: One of the notes in which Nietzsche tries to state what a drive is in terms that would also apply below the level of an organism. These entries are where the psychological and cosmological registers are being fused, and where it is easiest to see that the fusion was a project rather than a result: the vocabulary is provisional, the formulations change from entry to entry, and none of it reached print in this form. Compare it against Beyond Good and Evil §36, which proposes the same unification and marks it as a supposition.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §36; Body and physiology.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    The Will to Power §481

    The line about facts and interpretations

    Question: The entry applies its own claim to itself. Does that rescue it or dissolve it?

    Why it belongs: The most quoted sentence in the compilation, and almost always quoted without its status. The entry holds that there are no facts, only interpretations — and immediately adds that this too is an interpretation. It belongs to this theme because it is the methodological corollary the cosmological register would need, and because the published parallel, Beyond Good and Evil §22, makes the same concession in almost the same breath. Treated as an epistemological claim on perspectivism.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §22; Perspectivism.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained above.

  • Seven years, one idea. Read Daybreak §262 and then The Antichrist §2. Both are about power and both are short. What has been added, and what has been lost?
  • The grammar of the hypothesis. Take §36 and §22 together and list every conditional and concession in them. Then read KSA 11, 38[12] / WTP §1067 and list the same. The difference between the two lists is the whole of the cosmological question.
  • The case that decides it. Read Genealogy III §15 and ask whether the ascetic priest confirms the hypothesis or stretches it past the point where it could be false.
  • Where the analysis becomes a politics. Put Genealogy II §12 beside Beyond Good and Evil §259. One offers a method; the other draws a conclusion about how people may be treated. Mark the sentence where the second stops following from the first.

A question to carry forward

Take an institution you know from the inside — a workplace, a school, a family, a congregation. Apply Genealogy II §12 to it: separate how it arose from what it now does. Then ask whether you needed any claim about the universe to do so.
