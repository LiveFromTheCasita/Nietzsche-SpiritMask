By what standards should we judge the values that already govern our lives?

Revaluation—also translated as transvaluation—asks about the worth of values themselves. Nietzsche examines the conditions that produced moral judgments, the needs they serve, and the possibilities they encourage or obstruct. This changes the question from whether an action follows an accepted rule to whether that rule deserves its authority.

Historical inquiry, criticism, and creation interact throughout the readings. Knowing where a value came from does not by itself settle its worth; rejecting an inherited standard does not yet establish another. Nietzsche proposes ways of judging through life, power, creation, and the development of human possibilities. These proposals require examination in turn: whose life is enhanced, which possibilities count, and what costs do his hierarchies impose?

The guide follows that inquiry across the earlier books, Zarathustra’s speeches, and the late polemics and self-interpretations. It keeps the demands of learning and self-transformation alongside the ambitions of philosophers who would legislate for others.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter introduces the question of value, the work of creation, the authority of a legislator, and the standards Nietzsche proposes. Continue through the fuller route, or choose a book in the work survey. The separate notebook guide includes a seven-entry starting route.

Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order. Move from questioning accepted values to the work and authority of creating them. The final two readings test what Nietzsche means by a standard drawn from life and power, including the consequences of applying it to other people.

  1. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§3–6

    Calling the value of moral values into question

    Question: What must we know to evaluate moral values instead of taking their goodness for granted?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche separates questions about moral origins from his deeper concern with morality's value, then makes historical investigation necessary to that critique. His dispute with Paul Rée concerns explanation; his dispute with Schopenhauer concerns the elevation of pity and self-denial. Section 6 explicitly demands examination of the value of values, asking how morality affects human possibilities, including those of the future.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §186; Beyond Good and Evil §211; Genealogy II §§11–12.

    Online edition: Preface: locate §§3–6 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    The Gay Science Book IV, §335

    Investigate the conscience that tells you what is right

    Question: What must be learned before creating one’s own tables of value can become a serious task?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche asks how a moral judgment arose and why its holder obeys it. Confidence and persistence can conceal inherited habit, reward, or poor self-knowledge. He turns from judging actions by presumed universal rules toward examining valuations and creating individual ideals. The conclusion makes this a demanding project of learning the world’s laws and necessities: intellectual honesty and inquiry belong inside creation, rather than being obstacles to it.

    Read alongside: Genealogy Preface §§3–6: questioning the value of moral values; The Gay Science §344: questioning inquiry’s own commitment; Zarathustra: the collective histories of valuation.

    Online edition: Read §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On a Thousand and One Goals

    Peoples create the tables by which they live

    Common: The Thousand and One Goals.

    Question: How do different peoples produce their values, and what could a goal for humanity mean?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra surveys contrasting valuations shaped by peoples’ needs, surroundings, and relations with their neighbors. The earliest creators are peoples; the individual arrives later. Values emerge through human activity and collective histories, while the closing question asks whether humanity itself yet exists as a unity with a goal. This combines the diversity of inherited tables with a further problem of direction, rather than settling for an inventory of preferences.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335: examining the history of one’s conscience; The Gay Science §301: the human production of significance; Beyond Good and Evil §211: creating a direction for the future.

    Online edition: Read On a Thousand and One Goals (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §211

    The philosopher as creator and legislator of values

    Question: What distinguishes understanding inherited valuations from taking responsibility for the ends that should guide humanity?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes philosophical workers, who organize existing valuations, from philosophers whose task is to create values and determine humanity's direction. Scholarship, criticism, historical understanding, and experience of many perspectives remain necessary preparation. Creation here makes claims on a shared future and demands responsibility for its ends; the passage does not describe an individual's unexamined preference as sufficient authority to legislate.

    Read alongside: Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Gay Science §335; Beyond Good and Evil §§212–213.

    Online edition: Part VI: locate §211 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    Twilight of the Idols Morality as Anti-Nature, §§1–6

    Life evaluates through its interpreters

    The linked translation titles this Morality as the Enemy of Nature.

    Question: If every valuation arises within life, how can Nietzsche distinguish values that enhance life from those that oppose it?

    Why it belongs: The chapter contrasts transforming passions with eradicating them, then argues that no living evaluator can judge life from outside it. Even hostile valuations express a particular kind of life. Nietzsche distinguishes forms that develop desire and productive opposition from those that condemn their conditions of existence. The passage makes life a demanding interpretive problem: it does not provide an external measuring instrument or equate affirmation with satisfying every impulse.

    Read alongside: Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Gay Science §335; The Antichrist §2.

    Online edition: Morality as Anti-Nature, §§1–6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    The Antichrist §2

    A criterion of power—and its human cost

    Question: What follows when increasing power becomes the measure of good, happiness, and the value of other lives?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche recasts goodness, badness, and happiness through power, weakness, and overcoming resistance. The compressed formulas lead directly to a demand that the weak and ill-constituted perish and be helped to do so. The passage makes the proposed criterion unmistakable while exposing what is at stake in accepting it. Revaluation here requires judging Nietzsche’s standards and their consequences, not merely admiring the confidence with which he replaces inherited definitions.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Morality as Anti-Nature; Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Antichrist §57.

    Online edition: §2 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Continue with these investigations after the first encounter. Each assignment opens its explanation and reading links in the work survey. Read within each group in order, using the question to guide the comparison.

1. How do origins bear on worth?

What can historical and psychological inquiry establish, and what further judgment does evaluation require?

  1. Human, All Too Human — Volume I, §§1–2
    Begin with the history of concepts and the challenge to timeless human nature.
  2. Daybreak — §103
    Distinguish rejecting moral explanations from simply reversing every approved action.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §186
    Ask what comparison and description must precede a supposed foundation of morality.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§11–12
    Separate origins from later purposes and interpretations.

2. Which standards survive their former justification?

Can a critic leave an inherited belief behind while continuing to value through it?

  1. The Gay Science — Book III, §108
    Consider the long survival of God’s shadows.
  2. The Gay Science — Book V, §344 (1887)
    Examine the authority granted to truth within apparently godless inquiry.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§24–26
    Test proposed secular alternatives against the values they continue to serve.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27
    Let inherited truthfulness question its own value.

3. What does creating values demand?

Compare the declaration of a new value with the work of learning, shaping, and transforming a life.

  1. The Gay Science — Book IV, §290
    Read self-formation as an exercise in sustained discipline.
  2. The Gay Science — Book IV, §301
    Ask how human valuing contributes to the world of significance.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Way of the Creator
    Examine the demands and dangers of leaving accepted standards behind.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming
    Consider why even a creator’s values may have to be overcome.
  5. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — III, On Old and New Tablets, §§1–8 and 26–30
    Read the opening and concluding sequences of a teaching whose new tablets remain unfinished.

4. What can life and power tell us about value?

Whose capacities count, and what assumptions enter the proposed measure?

  1. The Gay Science — Book V, §370 (1887)
    Distinguish similar desires arising from abundance and from impoverishment.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §259
    Confront the place of appropriation and exploitation in the account of life.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — The Problem of Socrates, §§1–12
    Test Nietzsche’s reinterpretation of a revered philosophical figure.
  4. Twilight of the Idols — What I Owe the Ancients, §§1–5
    Examine the models of form, power, and affirmation he draws from antiquity.

5. Who is entitled to legislate—and at whose expense?

How does the ambition to cultivate new human possibilities relate to hierarchy and coercion?

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §§202–203
    Read the proposed future philosophers beside the attack on prevailing European morality.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §§212–213
    Ask what qualifies the philosopher to oppose the present.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man, §§35–37
    Examine the consequences of the late judgments about morality, life, and improvement.
  4. The Antichrist — §57
    Confront the attempt to authorize caste and unequal rights through an account of nature.

6. How does Nietzsche present the project as accomplished or underway?

Compare the work of criticism with his late declarations about what it has achieved.

  1. The Antichrist — §§12–14
    Read intellectual discipline and a naturalized account of humanity as practices of revaluation.
  2. The Antichrist — §62
    Examine the polemical verdict that ends The Antichrist’s numbered text.
  3. Ecce Homo — Why I Am a Destiny, §1
    Assess the claim to a decisive philosophical rupture.
  4. Ecce Homo — Why I Am a Destiny, §§7–8
    Return from the retrospective claims to the evidence and standards of the readings.

Keep these distinctions in view

A value’s origin and its worth
An account of where a value came from can reveal the needs, conflicts, and interests it carries. Evaluating that value also requires asking what it does and by what standard its effects should be judged. A discreditable origin does not by itself settle every question about present worth.
Revaluation and inversion
Simply praising what an inherited morality condemns can leave its categories and central concerns in place. Nietzsche also asks how those categories arose, what forms of life they serve, and whether different questions or standards are needed. The slave revolt itself is an example of creative valuation that he subjects to criticism.
Creating values and choosing preferences
The readings connect creation with inquiry, practice, self-formation, and conflict with inherited habits. An announced preference does not establish that a person can live through it, justify it, or bear its consequences. Cultural legislation also reaches far beyond an individual’s private choices.
Diverse valuations and equal worth
The variety of moralities supplies material for comparison. Nietzsche does not take that variety to establish that all valuations are equally good. His rankings nevertheless need examination: which capacities support the judgment, whose interests it serves, and what the judgment excludes.
Life as a proposed standard
Morality as Anti-Nature treats even judgments against life as expressions of a particular kind of life. The contrast therefore cannot simply be life against something outside life. Ask which condition, development, or possibility Nietzsche favors when he uses life to assess a value.
Power and its consequences
Power can involve organizing drives, overcoming resistance, or creating forms, as well as commanding and exploiting others. The broader uses do not cancel the harsher ones. Read the proposed standard alongside Nietzsche’s explicit demands about whose flourishing should be promoted or sacrificed.
Self-legislation and legislation for others
The Gay Science’s account of becoming creators and Beyond Good and Evil’s philosopher-legislators operate at different scales. The second raises questions about authority, education, hierarchy, and the future of other people. Personal independence does not by itself answer those questions.
Criticism and affirmation
Historical inquiry, criticism, and the formation of new aims repeatedly affect one another. They need not be treated as two completed halves of a single program. A critique may already depend on a proposed value, while an affirmative proposal remains subject to further criticism.
Questioning truth’s value and rejecting inquiry
The demand to assess the will to truth asks what authorizes an unconditional commitment to truth. Elsewhere Nietzsche insists on observation, learning, and intellectual honesty. Keep that tension visible instead of treating revaluation as permission to discard inconvenient evidence.
The philosophical project and the posthumous compilation
Nietzsche’s statements about revaluation occur in different books, late self-interpretations, and working notes. The Will to Power arranges notebook selections after his death. Its editorial sequence does not supply a finished system that resolves the differences among those statements.

Human, All Too Human5 reading assignments

Volume I (1878) investigates the formation of moral concepts and the assumptions behind responsibility. Its historical method prepares later questions about the value of values, but discovering an origin does not by itself settle a value’s worth. The two preface assignments belong to the retrospective preface dated 1886; their language of perspective and rank should not simply be projected back into every earlier aphorism.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§1–2

    Investigating how supposedly opposite values develop

    Zimmern: Chemistry of Ideas and Sensations (§1); Inherited Faults of Philosophers (§2).

    Question: What changes when elevated feelings and human nature itself are treated as things that have developed?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche proposes a chemistry of moral, religious, and aesthetic feelings, challenging the assumption that something highly valued must have a miraculous or wholly different origin. He then criticizes philosophers who treat present humanity as an eternal standard. These passages establish a historical inquiry into valuation; they do not establish that everything with humble origins is worthless. Compare that distinction with the Genealogy’s explicit question about the value of values.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§3–6: from origins to the value of values; Daybreak §38: moral judgments reshape the experience of impulses.

    Online edition: Volume I, §§1–2: First Division, opening sections (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §39

    The changing location of moral responsibility

    Zimmern: The Fable of Intelligible Freedom.

    Question: Does feeling guilty establish that a person could have acted, or existed, otherwise?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche traces responsibility from consequences to actions, motives, and finally a person’s nature. His challenge depends on an argument about necessity and on rejecting Schopenhauer’s inference from guilt to freedom, not simply on showing that judgments have a history. The passage asks readers to reconsider the authority of remorse and the grounds of blame, including blame directed at themselves.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §107: admiration after moral desert; Daybreak §148: reassessing actions after abandoning false distinctions; The Gay Science §335: examining the authority of moral judgments.

    Online edition: Volume I, §39: near printed page 59 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §107

    Admiration and understanding without moral desert

    Zimmern: Irresponsibility and Innocence.

    Question: What forms of appreciation remain when praise and blame lose their claim to judge desert?

    Why it belongs: The loss of responsibility first appears painful, but Nietzsche imagines a gradual change in habits of judgment: strength, beauty, and abundance can still be admired without being treated as merits. His account also refuses to despise the errors through which knowledge developed. This is an early proposal for transformed evaluation, with its own strong assumptions about necessity and egoism, rather than merely a negative preliminary to the later project.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39: the argument against responsibility; Daybreak §103: different motives for actions still worth performing; Twilight — Morality as Anti-Nature: assessing moral condemnation.

    Online edition: Volume I, §107: near printed page 107 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, Preface (1886), §1

    Suspicion turns back upon the critic’s own supports

    Question: What if the investigator of moral illusions has also needed protective self-deceptions?

    Why it belongs: Writing retrospectively in 1886, Nietzsche describes his books as provoking suspicion toward accepted valuations. He also admits his own need for protective attachments and self-deceptions, including his earlier relations to Schopenhauer and Wagner. The passage complicates a simple story of replacing illusion with unqualified honesty: the critic must ask how the pursuit of truth, artistic invention, and the conditions of continuing to live belong together.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Preface §§6–7: learning to direct one’s virtues; Daybreak — Preface: inherited honesty turns against morality.

    Online edition: Volume I, Preface (1886), §1: opening section (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, Preface (1886), §§6–7

    Directing one’s virtues and confronting the problem of rank

    Question: How does freedom from inherited reverence become an inquiry into perspective and rank?

    Why it belongs: The free spirit learns to direct virtues that formerly directed him and to recognize the limits and costs of every valuation. Nietzsche connects this education to unequal breadth of perspective and to an explicitly hierarchical problem of rank. The resulting freedom is neither the simple reversal of former convictions nor the claim that all perspectives are equally valuable; the retrospective account sets a demanding task of evaluation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Preface §1: suspicion and self-preservation; Beyond Good and Evil §211: philosophers as legislators; The Antichrist §2: an explicit proposed standard of value.

    Online edition: Volume I, Preface (1886), §§6–7: near printed page 9 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak6 reading assignments

The 1881 aphorisms investigate inherited judgments, the experiences those judgments produce, and the possibility of learning to feel and act from different motives. Denying the truth of moral assumptions does not require doing the opposite of every customary action. The preface was dated 1886 and interprets the earlier inquiry through morality’s own demand for honesty.

  • Development

    Daybreak Preface (1886), §§1–5

    Morality’s own honesty undermines its authority

    Question: How can a critique of morality grow out of the very conscience it questions?

    Why it belongs: The underground investigator of §§1–2 prepares §3’s question about morality’s power to prevent criticism. Section 4 then locates a paradox: inherited honesty requires withdrawal of belief from claims no longer credible, so morality participates in its own undoing. Section 5 asks for patient, careful reading. This preface was dated 1886; its retrospective account should not be mistaken for an opening statement written in 1881.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§3–6: morality becomes a problem; Daybreak §103: exactly what Nietzsche denies; Human, All Too Human — Preface §1: the critic’s difficult relation to truth.

    Online edition: Preface (1886), §§1–5: complete author’s preface (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §35

    Inherited judgments can speak through present feelings

    Kennedy: Feelings and their Descent from Judgments.

    Question: When a feeling seems immediately authoritative, whose judgment might it carry?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche challenges the advice to trust feelings by proposing that inclinations and aversions can transmit judgments inherited from others. Emotional immediacy therefore does not guarantee an independent or well-grounded evaluation. This gives revaluation a practical difficulty: reconsidering an explicit belief may leave its emotional descendants intact. The question is how reason and experience can examine what feels self-evident without assuming that every feeling must be false.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §104: borrowed valuations become second nature; The Gay Science §335: questioning apparently immediate moral certainty.

    Online edition: §35: near printed page 41 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §38

    Moral interpretation changes how an impulse is experienced

    Kennedy: Impulses Transformed by Moral Judgments.

    Question: Can a changed valuation alter the pleasure, shame, or name attached to an impulse?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that an impulse receives its moral name and much of its emotional character through its relations with prevailing judgments. His comparisons of humility, envy, hope, and anger show how different valuations can transform experience. These broad historical comparisons are claims to assess, but their central question is precise: what seems naturally good or shameful may already be the product of an interpretation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §§1–2: investigating apparent opposites historically; Zarathustra — On a Thousand and One Goals: peoples and their different valuations; Twilight — Morality as Anti-Nature: passions and their transformation.

    Online edition: §38: near printed page 43 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §103

    Denying moral assumptions while changing motives for action

    Kennedy: There are Two Classes of People who deny Morality.

    Question: Why does rejecting moral judgments not require reversing every moral recommendation?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes denying people’s professed moral motives from denying the truth of moral judgments even when those judgments actually motivate them; he explicitly adopts the second position. Yet many conventionally moral actions should still be encouraged, and many conventionally immoral actions resisted, for different reasons. Revaluation therefore requires learning and eventually feeling differently, not a blanket permission to do whatever inherited morality forbids.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §148: correcting both inflated and depressed valuations; The Gay Science §335: examining judgments and creating values; Human, All Too Human §107: transformed habits of appreciation.

    Online edition: §103: There are Two Classes of People who deny Morality (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §104

    How borrowed valuations come to feel like one’s own

    Kennedy: Our Valuations.

    Question: How independent are the judgments by which we choose whose evaluations to trust?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes personal valuations from the far more numerous ones adopted from others. Fear and habit can turn borrowed judgments into second nature, while the authorities we trust may remain those selected through childhood judgments. The passage complicates the instruction to create one’s own values: a sincere sense of ownership does not show that a valuation has actually been examined or made independent.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §35: inherited judgments within feelings; Human, All Too Human — Preface §§6–7: directing inherited virtues; The Gay Science §335: becoming creators of values.

    Online edition: §104: near printed page 100 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §148

    Correcting the scale after false distinctions collapse

    Kennedy: Looking Far Ahead.

    Question: How can rejecting an inflated moral category also reveal what has been unfairly devalued?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche challenges definitions of moral action that require pure selflessness or free will. Removing those distinctions risks initially undervaluing formerly honored actions, because the supposedly selfish and unfree categories have themselves been disparaged. He anticipates a difficult readjustment of esteem and conduct, while welcoming relief from bad conscience. The issue is how to reassess actions and life after exposing errors in the inherited scale.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §103: changed motives need not mean opposite actions; Human, All Too Human §39: responsibility and the assumption of freedom; Genealogy — Preface §§3–6: examining the value of moral values.

    Online edition: §148: near printed page 158 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science10 reading assignments

The first four books appeared in 1882; Book V was added in 1887. These readings connect the historical force of valuations with the work of examining and creating them. Self-observation, knowledge, and sustained practice complicate any picture of creation as an arbitrary choice. The later book also questions the inherited commitments and diagnostic confidence of the investigators themselves.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book II, §58

    Inherited names become part of the world we inhabit

    Question: Why does discovering a valuation’s origin fail to undo its power?

    Why it belongs: Names, reputations, and habitual measures acquire force through generations of belief until they help constitute what people experience as reality. Simply exposing their beginnings does not dissolve their effects. Nietzsche therefore connects criticism with creation: new names and valuations can gradually make a different human world. The passage supplies an important link between explaining inherited values and doing the further work of transforming them.

    Read alongside: Genealogy Preface §§3–6: origins and the value of values; The Gay Science §301: creators who mistake themselves for spectators.

    Online edition: Read §58 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book III, §108

    The commitments that survive a discarded belief

    Question: Which inherited valuations can persist after their former authority has lost credibility?

    Why it belongs: The image of a dead God’s surviving shadow separates an announced break from the prolonged work still required afterward. The aphorism does not provide a list of those residues or a replacement morality. For this theme, it opens the question of what remains active inside new valuations, including the investigators’ own commitments. Read the later examination of truth’s authority as one development of that problem.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §344: the inherited authority of truth; Zarathustra: the old idol-priest within the innovators.

    Online edition: Read §108 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    The Gay Science Book III, §270

    A brief demand to become oneself

    Question: What would becoming oneself require if one’s present conscience also has a history?

    Why it belongs: This exceptionally brief exchange makes becoming oneself the demand of conscience, but does not explain how that task should be understood or accomplished. Its force becomes clearer beside the later scrutiny of conscience in §335 and the sustained labor of character formation in §290. Together they prevent the aphorism from functioning as a simple endorsement of whatever a person already happens to prefer.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335: investigating conscience and creating oneself; The Gay Science §290: the work of giving character a form.

    Online edition: Read §270 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book IV, §283

    The people who prepare a different future

    Question: Which habits and capacities must be cultivated before new intellectual possibilities can emerge?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche imagines preparatory people whose courage depends on patience, independent judgment, work without recognition, and an ability both to command and to obey. His combative rhetoric culminates in a demand for dangerous inquiry. Read the provocation with the preceding list of capacities: the preparation involves sustained formation and intellectual risk. It is not simply a celebration of impulsive defiance or an assurance that every disruption creates something valuable.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211: preparation and philosophical legislation; Zarathustra: testing the would-be creator.

    Online edition: Read §283 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book IV, §290

    Giving character a form through daily work

    Question: How can a person reshape what is given without pretending to begin from nothing?

    Why it belongs: Character becomes material for an art that surveys strengths and weaknesses, practices change, and sometimes reinterprets what cannot be removed. Nietzsche emphasizes prolonged exercise and the constraint of a taste. He also distinguishes natures that welcome a single governing style from those that need a freer appearance. The passage offers a model of disciplined self-formation while leaving different forms of accommodation and self-satisfaction in view.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335: creation requires learning necessities; Zarathustra: carrying, refusing, and beginning to create.

    Online edition: Read §290 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book IV, §301

    The hidden creativity of the supposed spectator

    Question: How do thought and feeling help make the world that practical action takes for granted?

    Why it belongs: The contemplative person imagines being merely an observer, while continually producing valuations, perspectives, and differences of significance that others learn and enact. Nietzsche asks these creators to recognize their participation in forming the world that matters to human beings. His account links valuation to shared practices and transmission: what begins in thinking and feeling can become embodied and ordinary, rather than remaining a private opinion.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §58: valuations acquire a lasting reality; Zarathustra: peoples as creators of values.

    Online edition: Read §301 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    The Gay Science Book IV, §335

    Investigate the conscience that tells you what is right

    Question: What must be learned before creating one’s own tables of value can become a serious task?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche asks how a moral judgment arose and why its holder obeys it. Confidence and persistence can conceal inherited habit, reward, or poor self-knowledge. He turns from judging actions by presumed universal rules toward examining valuations and creating individual ideals. The conclusion makes this a demanding project of learning the world’s laws and necessities: intellectual honesty and inquiry belong inside creation, rather than being obstacles to it.

    Read alongside: Genealogy Preface §§3–6: questioning the value of moral values; The Gay Science §344: questioning inquiry’s own commitment; Zarathustra: the collective histories of valuation.

    Online edition: Read §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science Book V, §343 (1887)

    An open horizon and an unsettled inheritance

    Question: Why does the loss of Christian belief make inherited morality a new problem?

    Why it belongs: The loss of credibility of the Christian God threatens structures built upon that belief, including European morality. Nietzsche anticipates consequences that remain largely unrecognized, while the free spirits described here experience an opening for inquiry. This supplies a historical setting for reassessment, not a completed program of new values. Their immediate cheerfulness coexists with danger and with a long task whose outcome has not been secured.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §108: beliefs can outlive their announced authority; Zarathustra: different responses to a demand for earthly meaning.

    Online edition: Read §343 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Gay Science Book V, §344 (1887)

    The critic must also examine the value of truth

    Question: Can inquiry question inherited values while treating its own highest commitment as unquestionable?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche investigates the unconditional value assigned to truth and traces it to a Christian and Platonic inheritance that can survive atheism. He asks what warrants truth at any cost, especially when appearance and error also serve life. This extends criticism into the activity conducting it. These speeches connect the histories of peoples’ values with the difficult possibility of new creation. Beyond Good and Evil10 reading assignments

Revaluation requires examining the valuations already operating within philosophy, then asking who could create and sustain different ones. These readings move from criticism of philosophical assumptions to comparison of moralities and the responsibilities of future philosophers. Their alternatives remain strongly hierarchical: Nietzsche's rejection of equality, account of exploitation, and claims about inherited capacities cannot be reduced to personal authenticity.

    The inheritance of a struggle against dogmatism

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche attacks dogmatism, especially the Platonic good in itself and its Christian inheritance, while crediting the struggle against it with producing an exceptional tension of spirit. His free spirits retain that tension without announcing a settled goal. This frames revaluation as a task undertaken with historically formed capacities, rather than a fresh beginning requiring the past to leave no trace.

On the Genealogy of Morals10 reading assignments

The Genealogy connects investigation of moral origins with the different question of what moral values are worth. Its essays pursue conflicts between valuations, the emergence and reinterpretation of bad conscience, and the functions of the ascetic ideal. These are interlocking inquiries rather than three steps in a single sequence of inversions. Their histories prepare evaluation without making origin alone decide value.

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§3–6

    Calling the value of moral values into question

    Question: What must we know to evaluate moral values instead of taking their goodness for granted?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche separates questions about moral origins from his deeper concern with morality's value, then makes historical investigation necessary to that critique. His dispute with Paul Rée concerns explanation; his dispute with Schopenhauer concerns the elevation of pity and self-denial. Section 6 explicitly demands examination of the value of values, asking how morality affects human possibilities, including those of the future.

Twilight tests familiar ideals by asking what kinds of life they express and cultivate. Its accounts of passions, causality, antiquity, and modern institutions also reveal disagreements about what should count as enhancement. The Antichrist8 reading assignments

The Antichrist tests Christian values against power, cultivation, and Nietzsche’s preferred forms of life. Its polemics also expose exclusionary demands, idealized hierarchy, and hostile generalizations about peoples and traditions. These are substantive parts of the proposed revaluation, alongside its criticism of inherited authority and its defense of intellectual inquiry.

  • Core

    The Antichrist §2

    A criterion of power—and its human cost

    Question: What follows when increasing power becomes the measure of good, happiness, and the value of other lives?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche recasts goodness, badness, and happiness through power, weakness, and overcoming resistance. The compressed formulas lead directly to a demand that the weak and ill-constituted perish and be helped to do so. The passage makes the proposed criterion unmistakable while exposing what is at stake in accepting it. Revaluation here requires judging Nietzsche’s standards and their consequences, not merely admiring the confidence with which he replaces inherited definitions.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Morality as Anti-Nature; Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Antichrist §57.

    Online edition: §2 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §§3–7

    Which human possibilities should be cultivated?

    Question: Why does the preservation of life fail to settle which forms of life Nietzsche wants to enhance?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes deliberately cultivating a higher type from assuming that history guarantees improvement. He attacks Christianity for opposing that type, defines corruption through the choice of what harms an organism, and makes pity a principal target. The sequence connects a criterion with a disputed program of cultivation. Its contrast between preservation and enhancement shows why life, in this polemic, cannot simply mean protecting every existing person or maximizing collective survival.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §2; Beyond Good and Evil §211; Twilight, Skirmishes §§35–37.

    Online edition: §§3–7 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §§8–11

    The theological reversal of what counts as valuable

    Question: How can calling a standpoint holy determine in advance what it accepts as true and good?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche attacks the theologian’s elevation of ideals over actuality, describing theology itself as a revaluation that exchanges the meanings of true and false. He extends the charge to Kant’s protection of a true world and his account of impersonal duty. This sequence shows that revaluation names a process Nietzsche also attributes to opponents. The issue is which values a transformation establishes, not the novelty of overturning established judgments alone.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Reason in Philosophy; Twilight, Morality as Anti-Nature.

    Online edition: §§8–11 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §§12–14

    Methods and a changed picture of the human being

    Question: How can intellectual methods embody a revaluation before a new table of moral rules is announced?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche contrasts philosophical conviction and priestly authority with cautious inquiry, explicitly calling free spirits an embodied revaluation in §13. Section 14 then removes humanity from a privileged divine origin and reinterprets will and consciousness within embodied life. The assignment concerns methods and a changed account of the human being, rather than merely restating a criterion of enhancement. Its challenges to spiritual privilege also invite scrutiny of Nietzsche’s replacement explanations.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335; Twilight, The Four Great Errors; The Antichrist §§8–11.

    Online edition: §§12–14 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §§17–19

    Revaluing the conception of God

    Question: By what standard does Nietzsche judge the transformation of a deity into universal goodness or pure spirit?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche opposes the assumption that a universally good, abstract deity necessarily represents progress. He interprets that transformation as a loss of powers and a condemnation of earthly existence, then asks why new gods have not been created. His evaluation therefore concerns the kind of divinity valued, not only belief or unbelief. The account’s anti-Jewish imagery and claims about national decline belong to its polemical construction and should not pass as neutral history.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Reason in Philosophy; Zarathustra, On a Thousand and One Goals.

    Online edition: §§17–19 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §24

    The polemical account of priestly revaluation

    Question: How does Nietzsche distinguish the vitality of value-creators from the values they promote?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche attributes a far-reaching inversion of values to Jewish priestly self-preservation and presents Christianity as its continuation. He simultaneously credits Jewish people with exceptional vitality, describing the deployment of decadent values as a strategy of power. That distinction complicates a simple equation of powerful creators with life-affirming results. His sweeping, hostile generalizations require scrutiny as part of the argument, rather than adoption as an established account of Jewish history.

    Read alongside: Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Antichrist §§8–11; Ecce Homo, On the Genealogy of Morals.

    Online edition: §24 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §57

    Legislation, sacred authority, and unequal rights

    Question: What justifies the hierarchy that a lawgiver fixes after an era of experimentation?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes sacred law as the consolidation of earlier experimentation, secured through claims of revelation and tradition. Yet he also endorses an idealized caste order and presents unequal rights as natural necessities. The passage therefore goes beyond showing that laws are made: it defends specific exclusions and seeks to end further experimentation. His portrayal of Manu supplies a polemical model of hierarchy, not neutral anthropology or a general argument for freely chosen values.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211; The Antichrist §2; Twilight, Skirmishes §§38–40.

    Online edition: §57 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §62

    The concluding indictment of Christian values

    Question: What standards authorize Nietzsche’s final condemnation, including his attack on equality?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche concludes by accusing Christianity of reversing values, producing guilt, and condemning earthly life. He also attacks equality before God and its revolutionary consequences, then proposes reckoning time from Christianity’s end. This is the book’s closing indictment, not the separate Law Against Christianity. Reading the verdict beside the opening criterion exposes both its evaluative grounds and the contested political commitments embedded in its demand for revaluation.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §2; Ecce Homo, Why I Am a Destiny, §§7–8; The Antichrist §57.

    Online edition: §62 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo5 reading assignments

Nietzsche retrospectively organizes his life and books around revaluation. These precise sections illuminate that self-interpretation while leaving its claims of exceptional authority open to examination. They also complicate a fixed division between preliminary criticism and a later affirmative project.

  • Complication

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Clever, §9

    Becoming capable of the task before understanding it

    Question: How does Nietzsche’s account of becoming himself complicate the idea of consciously designing a valuing self?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche answers the question posed by the book’s subtitle through an account of concealed preparation. Errors, detours, and even apparently altruistic commitments can serve an organizing task before it becomes conscious. He describes revaluation as requiring many capacities held in an order without erasing their differences. This gives the personal formation of a valuator a definite passage to examine, while complicating any simple prescription to discover an identity and deliberately realize it.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335; Beyond Good and Evil §211; Ecce Homo, Why I Am a Destiny, §1.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Clever, §9 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo On Beyond Good and Evil, §§1–2

    Criticism of modernity after Zarathustra

    Question: Why does Nietzsche retrospectively place this critical book after an affirmative achievement?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents Beyond Good and Evil as a turn toward the negative work of revaluation after Zarathustra, while identifying its indications of a noble, affirmative type. He describes the book’s deliberate hardness and its search for collaborators. This retrospective complicates a simple chronology in which genealogy clears the ground before affirmation begins: criticism, selection, and affirmative possibilities remain intertwined within his own account of the project.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211; Zarathustra, On a Thousand and One Goals.

    Online edition: On Beyond Good and Evil, §§1–2 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo On the Genealogy of Morals, whole subsection

    Three psychological openings toward revaluation

    Question: How does Nietzsche connect the origins of moral authority with the need for an alternative ideal?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche summarizes the Genealogy through resentment, inward-directed cruelty, and the ascetic ideal’s power in the absence of competitors. He calls its essays psychological preparations for revaluation and claims Zarathustra introduced a counter-ideal. His compressed retrospective helps connect the three inquiries, but should be compared with their fuller arguments: it selects an organizing story rather than replacing the book’s qualifications and tensions with a final explanation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; The Antichrist §24; Zarathustra, On a Thousand and One Goals.

    Online edition: On the Genealogy of Morals, whole subsection (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo Why I Am a Destiny, §1

    Revaluation as a claimed historical turning point

    The linked translation titles this chapter Why I Am a Fatality.

    Question: How does Nietzsche claim affirmative purpose for an undertaking he describes through catastrophe and conflict?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents revaluation as humanity becoming conscious of inherited falsehoods through an unprecedented crisis. He denies wanting believers or sainthood, declares himself affirmative despite his opposition, and predicts profound political upheaval. This is a forceful statement of the scope he claims for the task. Its rhetoric should be read as philosophical self-presentation and provocation, rather than proof that his evaluations are established or that the project is complete.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Preface; Beyond Good and Evil §211; The Antichrist §62.

    Online edition: Why I Am a Destiny, §1 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Ecce Homo Why I Am a Destiny, §§7–8

    Putting the highest moral ideals on trial

    The linked translation titles this chapter Why I Am a Fatality.

    Question: How does exposing an ideal’s hostility to life also expose the standards of its critic?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche gathers his accusations against Christian morality around the depreciation of body, sexuality, self-concern, and earthly purposes. He describes the exposure of this valuation as a historical catastrophe and condemns the preservation of people he judges should perish. The passage gives revaluation its most sweeping retrospective form, while making its exclusions impossible to ignore. Its interpretation of morality and its alternative measures must both remain objects of criticism.

    Read alongside: Twilight, Morality as Anti-Nature; The Antichrist §2; The Antichrist §62.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

25 numbered entries in 16 assignments develop 6 questions raised by the published works. Begin with the shorter starting route, then follow the questions that interest you.

These notes investigate how values arise, what they do, how they can be assessed, and what creating or transforming them would demand. The selections retain tensions between personal experimentation and hierarchical legislation, and between inherited standards and proposed criteria of life and power. They offer several approaches to revaluation without treating the compilation as Nietzsche’s completed plan for it.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche’s notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Read these experiments alongside the published works. Their provisional status does not remove the need to examine their proposed standards for judging values, their accounts of creation, and the human consequences of their hierarchies.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the arrangement brings together notes from different dates. The compilation’s four books should not be treated as a completed Revaluation of All Values or as Nietzsche’s final plan for that project.

What makes a value worth questioning?3 assignments · 5 entries

Distinguish the history of a valuation, its effects, and the grounds on which it might be assessed.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §254

    Origin is not yet a verdict on worth

    Question: What more must we know after discovering where a moral valuation came from?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche explicitly separates inquiry into the origin of a value from criticism of its worth, while allowing that an origin can unsettle its prestige. He asks what the value’s rule produces, for whom, and in relation to what, then proposes life understood through will to power. The passage makes genealogy a preparation for evaluation while leaving the proposed standard itself available for examination.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; The Antichrist — §2.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §256, §257, §258

    Valuations as expressions of conditions of life

    Question: What can a moral judgment reveal about the forms of life that need or sustain it?

    Why it belongs: These notes relate morality to conditions of life and treat valuations as evidence about the people and communities from which they emerge. Section 258 then asks what standard remains when the commanding position of moral values is challenged. The physiological and diagnostic vocabulary forms part of Nietzsche’s evaluative interpretation; the readings invite scrutiny of its claims and of the relation between explanation and judgment.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §294

    The feeling of conscience and the question of value

    Question: Why might confidence in one’s conscience repeat an inherited judgment without justifying it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes conscience as carrying forward established condemnations and approvals, then challenges the evidential force of inner peace and elevated feeling. The conviction accompanying an action cannot settle its worth. This makes revaluation demanding at the level of self-examination: sincere feeling may disclose an attachment, while the history, grounds, and consequences of the judgment still require investigation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6.

What would actually change in a revaluation?3 assignments · 5 entries

Look beyond replacing beliefs to the alteration of goals, needs, and the order among values.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §28

    A replacement that leaves the old standard intact

    Question: Why might a new answer preserve the problem that discredited an earlier belief?

    Why it belongs: This brief note criticizes attempts to escape nihilism without revaluing inherited values. Its challenge is useful for judging any proposed replacement: what assumptions about worth still govern it, and what has actually changed? The note establishes a demand rather than a completed procedure, so a claim to have overcome old beliefs still needs an account of the standards through which new commitments will be assessed.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; Beyond Good and Evil — §211.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §260

    From inherited tables to experimental goals

    Question: What becomes possible when values are understood through the purposes and communities they have served?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes the world as colored by valuations and compares conflicting tables of values formed around particular conditions of existence. He then turns toward experimental goal-setting when their old functions no longer command belief. The passage introduces a personal demand to posit aims while also asking about a goal beyond the individual, a tension to retain when comparing it with the later language of philosophical legislation.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals; The Gay Science — §335; Beyond Good and Evil — §211.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1006, §1007, §1008

    A changed hierarchy and newly felt needs

    Question: How would changing the place of moral values alter what a culture can recognize and pursue?

    Why it belongs: Section 1006 treats the removal of morality’s supreme rank as a change affecting the whole order of values. Section 1007 asks for recognition of capacities obscured by inherited judgments, while §1008 makes revaluation depend on accumulated forces and newly felt needs. Together they give the project conditions and material to work upon, complicating the picture of a person simply inventing any values they please.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; Beyond Good and Evil — §211; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature.

From what standpoint could values be assessed?3 assignments · 5 entries

Examine proposals about interpretation, preservation, enhancement, and the plurality within the person who evaluates.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §590, §605

    Giving meaning and directing action

    Question: What distinguishes creating a goal from discovering facts or merely changing moral vocabulary?

    Why it belongs: Section 590 treats meaning and valuation as perspectival interpretation. Section 605 distinguishes the investigation of facts from the creative direction of purposes, emphasizing action and the shaping of circumstances. These notes make revaluation consequential: new concepts alone do not accomplish the task. The proposed direction must also answer for the form of life and the relations of power it helps bring about.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §211; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §675, §715

    Life, preservation, enhancement, and power

    Question: If every valuation expresses a form of life, how can one valuation be judged better than another?

    Why it belongs: These notes interpret valuing through will to power and through the conditions of preservation and enhancement of organized living processes. Section 675 includes negative judgments of existence within the same activity of valuing. That creates a further question for revaluation: appealing to life or power does not yet explain which forms should be preferred, or how conflicts among their conditions of flourishing should be decided.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist — §2; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §259

    The evaluator’s conflicting commitments

    Question: Can the ability to understand opposed valuations become a resource for judgment?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects values to purposes of preservation and describes a person divided among conflicting commitments. Yet he also imagines this multiplicity enabling a wider understanding of other people and their claims. The same internal tension appears as a liability and a condition of possible wisdom, inviting readers to ask how an evaluator could organize differences without pretending to occupy a position untouched by valuation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6.

How can a proposed new value be tested?2 assignments · 2 entries

Ask what animates a valuation and how its creator can acknowledge the limits of the claim being made.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1009

    Abundance, reaction, and the source of a judgment

    Question: What changes when the same apparent achievement grows from abundance, dependency, fear, or a search for resistance?

    Why it belongs: The note offers diagnostic questions about spontaneous activity, reaction, strength, need, and the organization of conflicting capacities. It asks readers to examine what a valuation expresses before judging it by its declared ideals alone. Its contrasts belong to Nietzsche’s account of rank and character, so their application also requires scrutiny: a confident claim of strength cannot establish its own accuracy simply by making the claim.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; The Antichrist — §2; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1011

    Creating values without sanctifying the unknown

    Question: How can creators acknowledge their role in valuation without making their new standards unquestionable?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche begins with a limit on what is known, then asks how far people create their valuations and can give history meaning through them. The note also provocatively reopens the relation between truth, appearance, and value. It leaves a demanding question for the project: how to accept responsibility for interpretation while resisting the temptation to treat a newly preferred perspective as a final revelation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals.

Who may legislate values, and with what authority?3 assignments · 6 entries

Keep the hierarchy and power in these proposals visible alongside their account of philosophical responsibility.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §287, §856

    Revaluation and an unequal order of rank

    Question: Who receives different standards when revaluation is directed toward a new hierarchy?

    Why it belongs: Section 287 preserves herd morality within a restricted sphere and assigns different valuations to leaders and independent types. Section 856 asks about those capable of undertaking revaluation and connects rank with power, war, and danger. Together they reveal commitments that exceed personal self-expression: the proposal distributes authority unequally and raises direct questions about the forms of domination it would legitimate.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §211; The Antichrist — §2; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §422, §972

    The philosopher as investigator and legislator

    Question: What responsibility arises when a philosopher can no longer present new values as discoveries or divine commands?

    Why it belongs: These passages distinguish criticism and the ordering of inherited knowledge from the determination of purposes. Section 972 examines how lawgivers can evade responsibility by treating their preferred good as eternal or revealed, then asks what their task becomes when those protections fail. The philosopher’s authority is therefore part of the problem: the aspiration to command must be assessed together with the values being proposed.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §211; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §979, §980

    Creating future types and the educator’s deception

    Question: What could justify shaping other people through values they have not themselves chosen?

    Why it belongs: Section 979 joins the creation of new values to the deliberate cultivation of human types. Section 980 then grants the philosopher as educator disturbing privileges, including deception and the strategic use of praise or contempt. These claims expose the coercive possibilities within the legislative ideal and should remain visible when asking whether revaluation can support the self-creation described in other passages.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §211; The Gay Science — §335; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On a Thousand and One Goals.

What would an affirmative revaluation have to sustain?2 assignments · 2 entries

Consider affirmation and recurrence as demanding experiments whose success remains a question.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1041

    Affirming the difficult features of existence

    Question: Could affirmation include what an inherited morality has condemned without merely reversing every judgment?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes an experimental philosophy that confronts nihilism while seeking an affirmation broad enough to include the disturbing aspects of existence. The task reaches beyond changing the sign attached to particular qualities and remains connected to his hierarchy of human possibilities. Read it as an account of what the project would have to endure and transform, with its own standards of affirmation still requiring examination.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature; The Antichrist — §2; On the Genealogy of Morality — Preface §§3–6.

    Recurrence as a test of changed values

    Question: What would need to change in valuing for the thought of recurrence to become endurable?

    Why it belongs: This outline asks conditionally about recurrence and its possible effects, then connects the ability to endure it with a transvaluation of values. Uncertainty and creative activity are among the changes it proposes. Its abbreviated form makes the status of the exercise important: the note sketches a test of a changed relation to existence, without proving recurrence or establishing a completed program for revaluation.
