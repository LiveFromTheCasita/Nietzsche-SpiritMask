What would it mean to overcome present humanity—and who could judge the result?

Zarathustra announces the overman, or Übermensch, as the meaning of the earth and contrasts this possibility with the last man’s satisfaction with a comfortable, unchanging life. The announcement opens a drama of teaching, misunderstanding, creation, and preparation. The book gives us images and demands to interpret, without a detailed portrait of an achieved overman.

The later writings approach related questions through philosophers, higher types, and the creation of values. Read those comparisons closely: Zarathustra, his higher men, a future philosopher, and the overman have different roles. Nietzsche’s language also raises difficult questions about rank, suffering, heredity, and the people expected to bear the costs of exceptional lives.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves from Zarathustra’s announcement to creation, the higher men, and Nietzsche’s later explanations. Continue through the fuller route, or use the work survey to follow a particular book. The notebook guide includes its own seven-entry starting route.

Use the preferred translation where possible. Each assignment gives a precise section or chapter and a named online translation. Some links open a nearby printed page or a whole part; use Find for the section number or alternative chapter title shown. Thomas Common and Anthony M. Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order. Begin with the announced possibility, examine the work of creation and the limitations of the higher men, then compare the later discussions of value creation and human types. End with Nietzsche’s own explanation of how the term was misunderstood.

  1. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Prologue §§3–5

    An earthly future and an audience that wants something else

    The link opens the Prologue; begin at section 3 and stop after section 5. Common uses Superman for overman.

    Question: What does Zarathustra ask humanity to overcome, and why does the marketplace prefer the last man?

    Why it belongs: The announcement gives the overman an earthly orientation, presents humanity as a dangerous crossing, and opposes the last man’s settled satisfaction. Its images also connect hope with sacrifice and the disappearance of present forms. The crowd’s response makes the teaching’s failure part of its meaning. Read both the proposed future and the difficulty of communicating why anyone should desire it.

    Read alongside: Prologue §§6–10; On the Higher Man.

    Online edition: Prologue §§3–5 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Three Metamorphoses

    Bearing, refusing, and beginning to create

    Common: The Three Metamorphoses.

    Question: Why does freedom from an inherited command still fall short of creating new values?

    Why it belongs: The camel seeks difficult burdens, the lion wrests freedom from an inherited authority, and the child figures the affirmative beginning of creation. This sequence distinguishes capacities that the proclamation of the overman leaves compressed. The chapter never simply identifies the child with an achieved overman; treat the images as questions about transformation rather than a timetable guaranteeing arrival at a higher type.

    Read alongside: On Self-Overcoming; On the Gift-Giving Virtue.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra IV, On the Higher Man, §§1–20

    Promising and unsuccessful higher men

    Common: The Higher Man. Read all twenty numbered sections.

    Question: Why does Zarathustra value the higher men while asking them to overcome themselves?

    Why it belongs: The speech keeps the overman as its concern while addressing higher men who have failed or only partly succeeded. Zarathustra both challenges and encourages them, making honesty, courage, creation, laughter, and their limits part of the examination. His contempt for prevailing equality and comfort also remains explicit. These listeners carry possibilities and unresolved problems; the text does not identify them with an accomplished overman.

    Read alongside: Prologue §§3–5; The Sign; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Read §§1–20 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §211

    Philosophers who create values

    Question: How does creating values differ from understanding and organizing inherited ones?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes philosophical scholarship from a philosopher’s proposed task of determining humanity’s direction. Learning to see through many perspectives prepares that task without completing it; creation becomes legislation and a claim to command. This offers a precise comparison with Zarathustra’s teaching, while leaving open whether such philosophers are forerunners, analogues, or something different from the overman. The section ends by questioning their existence.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — On the Gift-Giving Virtue; Beyond Good and Evil §203.

    Online edition: Part VI: locate §211 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    The Antichrist §§3–4

    Higher types: accident and intention

    Mencken uses superman in §4; this guide uses overman.

    Question: How does Nietzsche distinguish cultivating higher types from believing that humanity inevitably progresses?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche rejects inevitable human progress and identifies exceptional higher types across different cultures. Yet §3 explicitly asks which type should be willed and cultivated: past accident is the problem, not a prohibition on intention. Section 4 even includes peoples among possible exceptional cases. These passages connect higher humanity with cultivation without specifying a settled program or defining Zarathustra's overman.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, Prologue; The Antichrist §57.

    Online edition: §§3–4 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    Ecce Homo Why I Write Such Good Books §1

    Nietzsche challenges familiar images of the overman

    Ludovici titles the chapter Why I Write Such Excellent Books. This is its opening section, before the separate discussion of Zarathustra.

    Question: Which inherited ideals distort the figure before the reader has understood it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche objects to interpreting the overman through familiar ideals of sainthood, genius, Darwinism, or heroic greatness. His provocative preference for Cesare Borgia over Parsifal also resists a reassuring moral portrait. This is a late clarification, but it supplies contrasts rather than a complete definition; ask which standards of admiration the reader must reconsider.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, Prologue; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Why I Write Such Good Books §1 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

    The end of Zarathustra’s waiting and the prospect of his children.
  2. Ecce Homo — Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1, 6
    Recurrence and the overman’s reality in the work.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — What I Owe to the Ancients §5
    Affirmation includes becoming and the loss of the highest types.
  4. Ecce Homo — Why I Am a Destiny §5
    A human possibility judged through conflicting standards.

Keep these distinctions in view

The overman and the higher men
The higher men in Zarathustra’s cave remain addressees of a teaching that points beyond them. Their distinction from the overman is explicit; their limitations help reveal what the teaching demands.
A dramatic figure and a later comparison
The future philosopher, the noble soul, Goethe, Napoleon, and Dionysus belong to different arguments and literary settings. Compare what they create, affirm, or command before treating any one of them as the overman under another name.
A future possibility and a realized literary figure
Zarathustra repeatedly prepares what is still to come. Ecce Homo also describes the overman as becoming real in the work. Keep that tension open: an exclusively distant ideal and an already completed historical example are both interpretations that require argument.
Creation and freedom from authority
The lion wins the freedom to refuse inherited commands; the child represents an affirmative beginning. Leaving a teacher or rejecting an ideal raises a further question about the capacity to create values.
Cultivation, inheritance, and selection
The texts discuss education and self-discipline alongside heredity, marriage, breeding, and the organization of social ranks. The meaning and reach of cultivation have to be established passage by passage; translating a term does not settle the political or biological questions.
Greatness and justification
Calling a person or type higher does not by itself justify the suffering, inequality, or sacrifice assigned to others. Examine Nietzsche’s proposed standards and their costs alongside the achievements they are meant to make possible.
Completed works and notebook experiments
The Will to Power is an editorial compilation of notes from different dates. Its entries can sharpen comparisons, but their arrangement is not a completed argument Nietzsche published. Editorial caution also leaves the actual claims in those notes open to criticism.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra13 reading assignments

The overman enters through a dramatic announcement whose audience misunderstands it. Follow the changing relations between that announcement, the creation of values, future generations, and Zarathustra’s own education. The higher men and the teacher remain figures to examine rather than ready-made examples of the overman. Common translates Übermensch as Superman; this guide uses overman. Read Nietzsche’s chapters separately from the edition’s introduction and appended commentary.

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Prologue §§6–10

    The fallen tightrope walker and a change of audience

    The link opens the Prologue; continue from section 6 through section 10.

    Question: How does the failed performance change Zarathustra’s understanding of teaching?

    Why it belongs: The tightrope walker’s death turns the earlier image of crossing into an event Zarathustra must answer. He attends to the dying man, carries his body, and eventually seeks living companions capable of creating values with him. The transition complicates a simple opposition between compassion and the overman’s future, while exposing the unresolved relationship between independent companions and Zarathustra’s direction.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On Child and Marriage

    Creating beyond oneself across generations

    Common: Child and Marriage.

    Question: How does the hope for a future beyond present humanity enter the choice to have children?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra addresses marriage and parenthood through the ambition to create something beyond the parents. Bodily formation, self-command, and the hope for the overman are intertwined here, so the future cannot be reduced to an entirely private change of attitude. The speech also contains contemptuous and gendered judgments about partners and marriages; these belong to the passage’s assumptions and require examination.

    Read alongside: Upon the Blessed Isles; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Gift-Giving Virtue, §§1–3

    Abundance, earthly values, and independence from the teacher

    Common: The Bestowing Virtue. Read all three sections.

    Question: What connects giving, creating values, and learning to leave Zarathustra behind?

    Why it belongs: The speech joins an overflowing capacity to give with bodily life and the revaluation of earthly things. It also imagines a future people from whom the overman might arise. The final section tells the disciples to leave and question their teacher. That demand complicates the collective hope: preparing a future requires more than acquiring a belief in Zarathustra or copying his example.

    Read alongside: Prologue §§6–10; Upon the Blessed Isles.

    Online edition: Read §§1–3 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, Upon the Blessed Isles

    Preparing a future one may not personally embody

    Common: In the Happy Isles.

    Question: What is the difference between becoming the overman and helping make the overman possible?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra redirects hope from gods toward a future that human creation might prepare. He distinguishes producing the overman from becoming its ancestors, then connects creation with suffering, transformation, and an image carved from resistant stone. The passage gives the future a generational dimension and makes the costs of creation visible. Its rhetoric of willing also invites comparison with the difficulty posed by an unchangeable past.

    Read alongside: On Child and Marriage; On Redemption; Beyond Good and Evil §203.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On the Tarantulas

    Revenge, inequality, and the teacher’s own test

    Common: The Tarantulas.

    Question: How does opposition to revenge coexist here with an explicit defense of inequality?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra attacks revenge concealed in demands for equality, then links his love of the overman to inequality, contest, and ascent. The chapter therefore raises political questions within the published text itself. Its ending turns the accusation back toward the speaker: bitten by the tarantula, he needs help to resist revenge. His own vulnerability complicates the authority of the diagnosis.

    Read alongside: On Redemption; Beyond Good and Evil §257.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Self-Overcoming

    Life and the values it must surpass

    Common: Self-Surpassing.

    Question: How might a value become an obstacle to the life that created it?

    Why it belongs: The speech interprets life through commanding, obeying, risk, and will to power, then explains why created values cannot remain permanently authoritative. It supplies a framework for asking what overcoming humanity would involve beyond individual achievement. Read it as Zarathustra’s account to be examined: connecting it to the overman does not by itself settle whether that figure is a person, a future possibility, or an image directing creation.

    Read alongside: On the Three Metamorphoses; Beyond Good and Evil §211.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Poets

    Can the overman become another poetic idol?

    Common: Poets.

    Question: What happens when Zarathustra’s criticism of poets also reaches his own teaching?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra includes himself among poets, challenges a disciple’s belief in him, and mocks gods and overmen placed in an imaginary realm above the earth. This gives the figure an internal challenge: an earthly hope can itself become an inflated poetic invention. The exchange requires readers to examine the authority and uses of Zarathustra’s images without assuming that either belief or dismissal has already been justified.

    Read alongside: Prologue §§3–5; Upon the Blessed Isles; Ecce Homo, Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Redemption

    Fragmented humanity and a will unable to change the past

    Common: Redemption.

    Question: How could creating a human future transform our relation to what has already happened?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra describes present people, apparent geniuses, and himself through images of incompleteness, then asks how creation could give inherited fragments a different meaning. The will’s inability to undo the past becomes a source of revenge. His speech stops before fully resolving that problem, and the hunchback questions his changing manner of address. The scene tests both the projected future and the teacher who promises it.

    Read alongside: On the Tarantulas; On Old and New Tablets §§1–4; Genealogy II §§24–25.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, On Old and New Tablets, §§1–4

    Revisiting the overman among unfinished values

    Common: Old and New Tables. Read its opening through section 4, stopping before section 5.

    Question: What remains unfinished when Zarathustra gathers his teaching into new tablets?

    Why it belongs: Surrounded by broken and unfinished tablets, Zarathustra recalls the overman, humanity as a bridge, and the task of redeeming the past through creation. His instruction connects overcoming oneself with obligations to a remote future, while insisting that humanity cannot simply be jumped over. The opening also anticipates the lion and doves of the ending, linking his retrospective account with a sign he still awaits.

    Read alongside: On Redemption; The Sign.

    Online edition: Read §§1–4 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra IV, The Sign

    A new departure without a demonstrated arrival

    Question: What has changed at the end: the world, Zarathustra, his audience, or his readiness?

    Why it belongs: The lion and doves announce a change that the higher men cannot share: they retreat when the lion roars, while Zarathustra turns from pity toward his work and leaves the cave. His anticipated children remain ahead of the narrative. The ending provides signs and renewed readiness rather than a plainly identified overman; what those signs warrant remains a question for the reader.

    Read alongside: On the Higher Man; On Old and New Tablets §§1–4; On the Gift-Giving Virtue.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil9 reading assignments

The future philosopher, the noble soul, and the higher human type invite comparison with Zarathustra’s overman, but their different tasks and settings matter. These selections ask who could create values, what conditions might produce such people, and what costs Nietzsche assigns to their emergence. His arguments include political hierarchy, hereditary formation, and coercion alongside education and self-command.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §§61–62

    Religion as an instrument of human formation

    Question: Who decides which kinds of people should flourish, and what means may they use?

    Why it belongs: The philosopher employs religion to shape different ranks of people, while Nietzsche attacks religions that become independent ends. Education appears alongside marriage customs, heredity, rule, obedience, and the preservation or destruction of types. This complicates any account of future humanity as a wholly private achievement. The language cannot be reduced to either a harmless cultural metaphor or a single settled biological program.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §203; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Part III: locate §§61–62 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §188

    The disciplines behind freedom

    Question: How can sustained constraint produce capacities that later appear free?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche attributes artistic mastery and intellectual flexibility to prolonged obedience, including disciplines whose original purposes later become questionable. He also acknowledges capacities destroyed by the same process and extends his claim from individuals to peoples, ages, and humanity. Read this as an account of conditions and costs of formation, rather than a guarantee that obedience produces an overman.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §225; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — On Self-Overcoming.

    Online edition: Part V: locate §188 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §203

    Making humanity’s future a task

    Question: What would it mean for future philosophers to take responsibility for humanity’s direction?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche hopes for philosophers capable of revaluing inherited judgments and preparing long-term experiments in human formation. He presents this hope through explicit opposition to democratic equality and anxiety about the failure of exceptional people. The passage makes deliberate preparation central, challenging the idea that higher types can only be unplanned accidents. Its proposed commanders require interpretation alongside, rather than identification with, Zarathustra’s overman.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §211; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Part V: locate §203 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §225

    The creator and the suffering creature

    Question: What could justify the suffering Nietzsche associates with human elevation?

    Why it belongs: The human being appears as both material to be shaped and the creator doing the shaping. Nietzsche argues that great suffering has produced depth and opposes measures of value centered on pleasure and pain. His rhetoric includes making suffering greater, not merely finding meaning after misfortune. The reader must assess who undergoes this formation, who directs it, and whether its promised achievements justify its costs.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §188; Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259.

    Online edition: Part VII: locate §225 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §256

    Higher men and a European future

    Question: What do exceptional artists disclose about a future they cannot yet embody?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reads major European artists and thinkers as anticipations of a cultural synthesis that exceeds national boundaries. Their achievements coexist with exhaustion, imbalance, and religious retreat; admiration does not make them completed exemplars. The passage provides a historical comparison for the higher men in Zarathustra and resists reducing excellence to national identity. It neither simply renames the overman nor identifies every named figure with that possibility.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — On the Higher Man; Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes §49.

    Online edition: Part VIII: locate §256 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259

    Human elevation and its social costs

    Question: Does inward self-surpassing depend on the subordination of other people?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects increasing distance within the soul to aristocratic differences of rank and slavery. The following sections defend the sacrifice of many to a privileged few and treat exploitation as integral to life. Reading the sequence prevents its psychological language from obscuring its social commitments. These are claims about the conditions of human elevation to examine, not proof that the overman requires or vindicates every hierarchy.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §287; On the Genealogy of Morals I §§16–17.

    Online edition: Part IX: locate §§257–259 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §287

    Self-reverence and the recognition of nobility

    Question: Can certainty about oneself establish nobility, and how could another person recognize it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche locates nobility in a fundamental belief about oneself, distinguishing it from admirable works or a longing to become noble. This complicates a simple program of acquiring a higher identity through achievements. Read it against §§257–259: the inward description does not explicitly withdraw the account of social rank. Its noble soul also remains a figure to compare with the overman rather than an interchangeable name.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259; Ecce Homo — Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

    Online edition: Part IX: locate §287 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §§295–296

    Dionysus, transformation, and thoughts that fade

    Question: What changes when human transformation is presented through a tempting god and an unstable written image?

    Why it belongs: Dionysus provokes new possibilities in those he encounters and imagines humanity becoming stronger, more evil, and more profound. The narrator’s hesitation belongs to the scene. This is a comparison with Zarathustra’s transformative teaching, not a declaration that Dionysus is the overman. Read on: §295 is the penultimate aphorism; §296 questions what writing preserves of living thought, before the concluding poem.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — On the Gift-Giving Virtue; Ecce Homo — Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

    Online edition: Part IX: locate §§295–296 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals3 reading assignments

The Genealogy asks what inherited valuations permit humanity to become. Its first essay actually uses the overman term in a troubling description of Napoleon; its second anticipates a future redeemer and then hands the question back to Zarathustra. These distinct uses complicate the claim that the name simply disappears into an unambiguous vocabulary of higher types.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §6

    Judging morality by the futures it permits

    Question: What if the values that protect the present also restrict humanity’s future?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche calls for an investigation of the conditions that produced moral values and of the effects those values have. His provocation is that the apparently good person might obstruct possibilities of greater human power and flourishing. This supplies a question against which to test the overman’s significance: which future is being valued, by what standard, and on whose behalf? It does not yet supply that future’s portrait.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; Beyond Good and Evil §203.

    Online edition: Preface §6: near printed page 8 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I §§16–17, including the closing Note

    Napoleon and the unresolved conflict of values

    Question: How does this actual use of the overman term unsettle an exclusively ideal or apolitical reading?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes Napoleon through a conjunction of monstrosity and overhumanity—Samuel renders the terms as Monster and Superman. This appears within a polemical history of aristocratic and priestly valuations, not a simple endorsement of Napoleon as Zarathustra’s fulfilled teaching. The next section invites renewed conflict, while its Note asks how different standards favor majorities, minorities, or stronger types. The hierarchy and historical claims require scrutiny.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259; Ecce Homo — Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

    Online edition: Essay I §§16–17: near printed page 53 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II §§24–25

    A future redeemer—and the return to Zarathustra

    Question: What would redemption from life-denying ideals require, and why does Nietzsche interrupt his own answer?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche imagines redirecting bad conscience against ideals hostile to earthly life, then anticipates someone who could overcome nihilism and restore a goal. This hope is framed through strength, conflict, and the costs of destroying ideals. In §25 the speaker stops and reserves the territory for Zarathustra. Read that turn alongside the dramatic teaching: the future figure is a close comparison, not an explicitly completed identification with the overman.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — On Self-Overcoming.

    Online edition: Essay II §§24–25: near printed page 115 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols3 reading assignments

These late portraits connect exceptional achievement with inheritance, expenditure, self-formation, and affirmation. They offer comparisons with the overman while raising questions about which features of an exceptional individual can represent a future human possibility.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §44

    Genius inherits and expends accumulated strength

    Ludovici titles the chapter Skirmishes in a War with the Age.

    Question: Does exceptional achievement imply a sustainable form of human flourishing?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents genius as the discharge of strength accumulated across generations, giving both historical and physiological conditions a role. Napoleon exemplifies the capacity to dominate an age, while exhaustion and sterility may follow exceptional expenditure. This is a demanding comparison with the overman: greatness can consume its bearer and does not automatically promise lasting improvement for everyone.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§3–4; Beyond Good and Evil §257.

    Online edition: Skirmishes §44 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49

    Goethe as a portrait of organized abundance

    Ludovici titles the chapter Skirmishes in a War with the Age.

    Question: What does this portrait illuminate about greatness, and where does the comparison with the overman stop?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche's Goethe cultivates a broad range of capacities, disciplines himself into a whole, and reaches an affirmative relation to existence. The portrait gives concrete content to ideas of strength, breadth, and self-formation. Compare it with the later praise of Zarathustra in Ecce Homo, where even Goethe falls short: the texts do not establish one interchangeable set of exemplary figures.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, On Self-Overcoming; Ecce Homo, Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1, 6.

    Online edition: Skirmishes §49 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Twilight of the Idols What I Owe to the Ancients §5

    Affirmation beyond the preservation of a type

    Ludovici titles the chapter Things I Owe to the Ancients.

    Question: How does affirming becoming relate to the loss even of humanity's highest achievements?

    Why it belongs: The Dionysian account joins affirmation to becoming, destruction, and the sacrifice of the highest types. It complicates the expectation that Nietzsche's goal must be a perfected person who secures a final, stable condition. Its relevance is a question about the overman's relation to affirmation; Dionysus, eternal recurrence, and the overman retain distinct roles in the texts.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, The Sign; Ecce Homo, Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1, 6.

    Online edition: What I Owe to the Ancients §5 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Antichrist2 reading assignments

These passages distinguish the cultivation of higher types from belief in inevitable progress. Their connection to the overman must be examined together with Nietzsche's explicit commitments about hierarchy and the social conditions of exceptional development.

  • Core

    The Antichrist §§3–4

    Higher types: accident and intention

    Mencken uses superman in §4; this guide uses overman.

    Question: How does Nietzsche distinguish cultivating higher types from believing that humanity inevitably progresses?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche rejects inevitable human progress and identifies exceptional higher types across different cultures. Yet §3 explicitly asks which type should be willed and cultivated: past accident is the problem, not a prohibition on intention. Section 4 even includes peoples among possible exceptional cases. These passages connect higher humanity with cultivation without specifying a settled program or defining Zarathustra's overman.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, Prologue; The Antichrist §57.

    Online edition: §§3–4 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §57

    Cultivation, caste, and unequal rights

    Question: What political commitments accompany Nietzsche's account of the conditions for higher types?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche praises a caste order assigning intellectual rulers, warrior guardians, and a working majority unequal rights, treating this hierarchy as physiological necessity. His account links exceptional cultivation to inherited social authority and the subordination of others. Read its affirmative language about difficulty and self-mastery alongside these political commitments; it is a troubling comparison for the overman, not an explicit definition of that figure.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§3–4; Beyond Good and Evil §257.

    Online edition: §57 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo5 reading assignments

Nietzsche's retrospective distinguishes the overman from several contemporary interpretations while also bringing the figure unusually close to Zarathustra himself. These claims should remain in conversation with the drama's future-oriented language and its unsettled ending.

  • Core

    Ecce Homo Why I Write Such Good Books §1

    Nietzsche challenges familiar images of the overman

    Ludovici titles the chapter Why I Write Such Excellent Books. This is its opening section, before the separate discussion of Zarathustra.

    Question: Which inherited ideals distort the figure before the reader has understood it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche objects to interpreting the overman through familiar ideals of sainthood, genius, Darwinism, or heroic greatness. His provocative preference for Cesare Borgia over Parsifal also resists a reassuring moral portrait. This is a late clarification, but it supplies contrasts rather than a complete definition; ask which standards of admiration the reader must reconsider.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, Prologue; The Antichrist §§3–4.

    Online edition: Why I Write Such Good Books §1 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Ecce Homo Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1, 6

    Zarathustra, recurrence, and the overman's reality

    Question: How does this retrospective realization of the figure fit the book's future-oriented teaching?

    Why it belongs: Section 1 presents recurrence as the book's fundamental thought; §6 presents Zarathustra as capable of encompassing opposites and affirming existence. Here Nietzsche describes the overman as becoming intensely real in the work. Preserve the tension with the drama's deferred future: this praise complicates an exclusively distant ideal, without establishing a completed historical example outside the book.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, The Sign; Twilight, Skirmishes §49.

    Online edition: Thus Spoke Zarathustra §1 · Thus Spoke Zarathustra §6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo Thus Spoke Zarathustra §3

    Inspiration and the creation of a figure

    Question: What changes when Nietzsche describes poetic creation as something that happens through him?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes the arrival of images, rhythms, and language as an involuntary event of unusual intensity. This helps explain the form through which the overman is presented and complicates a picture of Nietzsche calmly designing an ideal person. Read it as his account of composing Zarathustra, rather than a direct account of how someone becomes an overman.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo, Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1, 6; Zarathustra, On the Gift-Giving Virtue.

    Online edition: Thus Spoke Zarathustra §3 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo Beyond Good and Evil §2

    A related project in a different literary form

    Question: How does a critique of modern humanity carry forward the questions raised by Zarathustra?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents Beyond Good and Evil as a critique of modern science, art, and politics that also indicates a noble, affirmative type. He emphasizes changes in perspective and literary method after Zarathustra. This supports a substantive connection between the books while leaving readers to test, rather than assume, an identity between the overman and the future philosopher.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §203; Beyond Good and Evil §211.

    Online edition: Beyond Good and Evil §2 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo Why I Am a Destiny §5

    Why the good might fear a different human possibility

    Ludovici titles the chapter Why I Am a Fatality.

    Question: Why would familiar standards of goodness make the overman appear threatening?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects the standing of conventional goodness with the devaluation of exceptional strength. He then returns to Zarathustra's warning that even the higher men might see the overman as evil. The contrast concerns competing valuations and the capacity to confront reality, including its disturbing aspects; it offers neither a timetable for the overman's arrival nor a simple endorsement of every transgression.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra, On the Higher Man; Genealogy, Essay II §§24–25.

    Online edition: Why I Am a Destiny §5 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

14 numbered entries in 10 assignments develop 5 questions raised by the published works. Begin with the shorter starting route, then follow the questions that interest you.

Sections 866 and 1001 explicitly use the overman term; the other notes explore related figures and questions whose relationship to Zarathustra’s overman needs to be examined.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche’s notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Read these experiments alongside the published works. Their provisional status does not remove the need to examine the claims they make about hierarchy, selection, and power.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the arrangement brings together notes from different dates.

What problem would a higher possibility answer?2 assignments · 2 entries

Begin with the loss of confidence in humanity and the kinds of strength Nietzsche imagines might survive it.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §27

    Nihilism and the missing higher possibility

    Question: Why might the suppression of exceptional possibilities make humanity seem less worthy of hope?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects nihilism to the absence of higher types and to the elevation of the majority’s needs into universal values. His account makes the fate of exceptional individuals a test of confidence in humanity, while also displaying the hierarchy built into that test. Read this as a diagnosis motivating the question of a higher possibility; the note leaves the identification of that possibility with Zarathustra’s overman unresolved.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; The Antichrist — §§3–4.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §55

    What kind of strength can endure a loss of meaning?

    Question: Why does this note’s account of the strongest end with moderation, uncertainty, and the capacity to bear suffering?

    Why it belongs: This long note traces the collapse of a moral interpretation of existence and asks what could follow it. Its final account of strength emphasizes the ability to live with uncertainty, tolerate suffering, and avoid dependence on extreme beliefs. The same note retains an unequal order of commanders and subordinates, so its moderation must be examined within that hierarchy and its distinction between healthier and declining forms of life.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; On the Genealogy of Morals — II §§24–25.

When does the notebook name the overman?2 assignments · 3 entries

Compare the passages that explicitly use the term with the broader language of a highest or future human being.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §866

    The overman and the machinery of society

    Question: What does the overman metaphor add to this proposed relationship between an exceptional person and the labor of many?

    Why it belongs: This is a particularly direct connection: Nietzsche explicitly associates the overman term with a higher type opposed to the reduction of people to specialized functions. Yet the exceptional figure also depends on the collective machinery and exploitation being described. The note therefore joins a criticism of human diminishment to a profoundly unequal proposal for overcoming it, and asks the reader to confront the relation between those two claims.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; Beyond Good and Evil — §257; Ecce Homo — Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1000, §1001

    A future standard and a deliberately spare goal

    Question: What would it mean to assess present values by the possibility of a future kind of human being?

    Why it belongs: Section 1000 makes a future highest human being the standard for present evaluation and assigns a task to those who glimpse that possibility. The following entry explicitly names the overman as the goal but gives almost no account of its realization. Their brevity makes the published distinctions especially important: the references to future and highest people cannot by themselves supply a complete definition of the overman.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — IV, On the Higher Man; Ecce Homo — Why I Write Such Good Books §1.

What would cultivation cost, and who would command?3 assignments · 6 entries

Keep the plans for hierarchy and selection visible while asking whether the highest person is the same as the political ruler.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §954

    Producing a ruling countertype

    Question: Who is being cultivated, and whose subordination is imagined as the condition of that achievement?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche asks about the deliberate production of a type opposed to the increasingly uniform European herd. He also imagines democracy culminating in slavery that could support ruling and commanding figures. This makes the proposal’s political asymmetry explicit: the development of one type depends on the position assigned to others. The note’s question form and notebook status leave these claims available for scrutiny without dissolving their disturbing content.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §203; Beyond Good and Evil — §257; The Antichrist — §§3–4.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §957, §960

    Cultivation, selection, and a future ruling caste

    Question: How are education, coercion, race, and political authority joined in this projected transformation of humanity?

    Why it belongs: These notes imagine moral legislation and the deliberate rearing of a new ruling caste across generations. Their vocabulary includes race, aristocracy, coercion, unequal rights, and command over humanity’s future, alongside intellectual discipline and self-control. Reading cultivation here requires attention to all of these dimensions and to the lives subordinated to the project; the passages also leave open how their proposed rulers relate to Zarathustra’s overman.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §203; Beyond Good and Evil — §257; The Antichrist — §§3–4.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §997, §998, §999

    Exceptional value and political office

    Question: What distinguishes the highest person from a ruler when rank is measured through the creation of values?

    Why it belongs: These short entries give exceptional individuals a justificatory importance far beyond their own lifetimes. Section 998 distinguishes the highest people from rulers whom they may use as instruments, while §999 locates supreme rank in the direction of values across long stretches of time. Taken together, they complicate an identification of greatness with political office while preserving the unequal valuation of human beings on which the argument rests.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §203; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — IV, On the Higher Man; The Antichrist — §§3–4.

What would a stronger person have to organize?2 assignments · 2 entries

Consider how the demands on a higher type change when attention turns to conflicting drives, pity, and the formation of character.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §966

    Strength as the organization of opposing drives

    Question: Does a stronger person need fewer conflicting impulses, or a greater capacity to sustain and organize them?

    Why it belongs: Moralities appear here as local orders among competing instincts, and the highest human being can sustain a greater multiplicity of powerful impulses. Strength takes the form of organization within tension, a useful comparison with the published accounts of self-overcoming and Goethe. The note helps specify a possible condition of a higher type, while leaving its connection to the dramatic figure of the overman to be established.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming; Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §983

    Commanding power and the capacity for pity

    Question: Can strength gain command of pity while retaining a capacity for benevolence?

    Why it belongs: The note asks for a formation of character that gives a person command of benevolence and pity, then combines Roman imperial power with the inward life associated with Christ. Its startling composite complicates a picture of strength based only on the removal of familiar virtues. Treat it as an experiment in what a powerful character might contain; it does not settle the meaning of the overman.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming; Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — IV, On the Higher Man.

What world would have to be affirmed?1 assignment · 1 entry

End with a broader image of becoming, keeping its relation to the overman an interpretive question.

  • Notebook reading

    The Will to Power §1067

    The Dionysian world of creation and destruction

    Question: How would this account of becoming change what it means to affirm existence?

    Why it belongs: The compilation closes with a world pictured as interacting forces, recurring transformation, and inseparable creation and destruction. The passage provides a cosmological setting against which questions of affirmation and self-overcoming can be considered. It does not explicitly name the overman, and its position at the end belongs to the editors’ arrangement; neither its imagery nor that position turns it into a final definition of the theme.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — Prologue §§3–5.

A question to carry forward

What would distinguish a transformation of human possibilities from an ideal that merely celebrates the ambitions of those who propose it?
