Nietzsche is not trying to disprove Christianity. What is he trying to do instead, and what would count as succeeding?

Christianity, in the preface to Beyond Good and Evil, is Platonism for the people: the popular form of a metaphysics that puts the real and the valuable somewhere other than here. The critique that follows from this is genealogical and psychological before it is theological. Nietzsche cares much less about whether God exists than about what kind of soul finds the Christian story compelling, and what kind of culture results from many centuries of taking that story as the source of value.

The answer he develops runs through the priestly type, the operation of ressentiment, the inversion of an older set of valuations, and finally the production of a human being whose deepest commitment is against life as it is. Whether that account is right is a separate question from whether it is well aimed, and this page tries to keep the two apart. Much of the historical material is weak; much of the psychological material is acute; the two are frequently welded together in a single sentence.

One distinction has to be held from the start. The figure of Jesus is not the main target of Nietzsche's attack, though the type itself is not spared — The Antichrist treats him with a strange and often-noted care, as a psychological type whose practice Nietzsche describes almost sympathetically even while diagnosing that type as decadent and rejecting the evaluation behind it. What he attacks is the church built on top of that figure, the apparatus of guilt and redemption, and above all the moral psychology that apparatus presupposes and reinforces. Readers who arrive expecting an attack on belief in God will find something narrower and stranger.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter is short on purpose: six precise assignments rather than whole parts of books. It moves from the middle period's patient estrangement, through the psychology of belief, to the inversion of values and the late polemic. The large blocks — Beyond Good and Evil Part III, Genealogy III, and the central chapters of The Antichrist — are where the argument is worked out at length, and they belong to the fuller route and the work survey. For a complete reading of the late polemic, use the Antichrist course.

Essential first encounter

Six short assignments, read in order and in publication sequence. The route begins with the middle period, where the treatment is most patient, and ends with the late polemic, where it is least. Reading them in this order makes the change of register visible, which is itself part of what a reader needs to assess.

  1. Core

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §113

    Making a familiar thing strange

    Zimmern: Christianity As Antiquity.

    Question: Nietzsche asks the reader to hear the claims as if for the first time. What does the estrangement expose that familiarity conceals?

    Why it belongs: Hearing Sunday bells, Nietzsche asks how it is possible that such claims — a god who fathers a son by a mortal, a sacrifice that cancels guilt, punishment beyond death — are still believed, and treats the survival of the belief as the thing requiring explanation. It is the method of the whole theme in one short section, and it is conducted without polemical heat, which makes it a good place to start. The estranging move is a rhetorical device as much as an argument; notice how much work it does and how little it establishes on its own.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §114; The Gay Science §347.

    Online edition: §113 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Daybreak §68

    Paul read as a psychological problem

    Kennedy: The First Christian.

    Question: What is Nietzsche explaining here — a doctrine, a person, or the relation between them?

    Why it belongs: The long section reconstructs Paul as someone unable to keep the law he revered, and reads the doctrine of a law abolished by Christ as the resolution of that private torment. It introduces the theme's central structural claim — that the church's founding ideas can be read as the working-out of a particular psychology — and it introduces the separation of Paul from Jesus that The Antichrist will make explicit. As reconstruction it is speculative: Nietzsche is inferring an inner life from the letters, and the inference is a hypothesis rather than a finding.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§28–35; Genealogy I §§7–10.

    Online edition: §68 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§46–47

    What faith was, and what it costs

    Zimmern titles this part The Religious Mood; Kaufmann, The Religious Nature.

    Question: §46 describes faith as a sacrifice of the spirit. What is being given up, and to whom is the sacrifice addressed?

    Why it belongs: §46 contrasts the faith of earliest Christianity — demanding, sceptical of southern freedom of spirit, requiring the subjection of the intellect — with the milder modern version; §47 surveys the religious neurosis across cultures and asks about its recurring conditions, noting the proximity of sensuality and asceticism. Two sections from early in Part III, chosen so the first encounter meets the mature treatment without taking on the whole part. Part III itself runs §§45–62, and the whole of it belongs to the fuller route.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§45–62; The Gay Science §347.

    Online edition: Part III (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §347

    The need to believe, examined as a need

    Common: Believers and their Need of Belief.

    Question: If the strength of a faith measures the believer's need rather than the doctrine's truth, what would disconfirm that diagnosis?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that the craving for certainty betrays a lack of self-reliance, and that what is wanted is not a particular doctrine but support of any kind. The section generalizes the critique beyond Christianity — political and scientific faiths are included — which is what keeps the theme from collapsing into an attack on one religion. It is also where the diagnostic method is most exposed to the charge of being unfalsifiable, since any conviction can be redescribed as a need; that objection is worth holding onto through the rest of the route.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §358; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

    Online edition: §347 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§7–10

    The inversion, and the birth of ressentiment

    Question: Nietzsche calls this a revolt in morality. What exactly is claimed to have been reversed, and by whom?

    Why it belongs: The analytical core of the theme. §7 introduces the priestly mode of valuation and the reversal of the aristocratic equation; §8 names the Jewish-Christian inheritance as its vehicle; §§9–10 develop ressentiment as a creative force that says no first and invents the concept of the evil enemy. This is where the critique of Christianity stops being a critique of a belief and becomes an account of how a whole scheme of value was produced. The historical narrative is contestable at nearly every point, and the passages contain language about peoples that is ugly and is Nietzsche's own; it is not softened here. Note also that he explicitly praises the achievement he is attacking, which any flat reading of the essay misses.

    Read alongside: Master and slave morality; The Antichrist §§24–27.

    Online edition: Essay I §§7–10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each essay are numbered separately) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    The Antichrist §§39–43

    The centre of gravity placed outside life

    Question: §43 makes the charge that the beyond displaces life itself. Is that a claim about doctrine, about psychology, or about consequences?

    Why it belongs: Five sections rather than the twenty of the old route, chosen because they carry the polemic's central charge. Nietzsche distinguishes the practice of the figure he calls the only true Christian from what the church made of it, describes the holy lie as the priest's instrument, and argues that placing the centre of gravity of life in the beyond deprives life of its centre altogether. Read after the Genealogy, the continuity of the analysis is clear; so is the change of register, from argument to indictment. The claims about the early church are Nietzsche's polemical history, not a scholarly reconstruction, and §§39–43 should be checked against the sources rather than accepted from him.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§28–35; The Antichrist §§61–62.

    Online edition: §§39–43 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Continue with these five investigations after the first encounter. This is where the large blocks belong: Beyond Good and Evil Part III entire, the middle sections of Genealogy III, and the central run of The Antichrist. Each assignment below opens its explanation and reading links in the work survey.

1. What exactly is the target?

Before assessing the critique, establish what it is aimed at: a doctrine, an institution, a psychology, or a consequence.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §§45–62, Part III entire
    The sustained treatment, including the uses Nietzsche thinks religion can be put to.
  2. The Gay Science — §358
    The Reformation read as a rebellion of the simple against a form.
  3. The Antichrist — §§10–12
    German philosophy treated as theology in disguise.
  4. Human, All Too Human — §132
    The need for redemption examined before the late vocabulary exists.

2. The priestly type and the inversion

The account that does most of the work. Follow it across three books and ask what kind of evidence each version rests on.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §§7–10
    The revolt in morality, and ressentiment as creative.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §16
    Two opposed valuations named as a struggle still undecided.
  3. The Antichrist — §§24–27
    The same inversion retold as a historical event, at polemical speed.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §195
    The compressed version, and the phrase that has been most abused.

3. Jesus separated from the church

The distinction the page insists on. Test how firmly Nietzsche holds it and what he needs it for.

  1. The Antichrist — §§28–35
    Jesus as a psychological type, described almost without hostility.
  2. Daybreak — §68
    Paul as the founder of what followed.
  3. The Antichrist — §§39–43
    The gap between the practice and the institution.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Priests
    The same figure handled dramatically, and with more pity.

4. What it does to a soul

The psychological payload of the critique, and the part of it that is hardest to dismiss.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§11–22
    The ascetic priest as physician to a suffering he also deepens.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§19–22
    Guilt before a creditor who cannot be repaid.
  3. The Antichrist — §7
    Pity attacked as a depressant, in the polemic's least guarded form.
  4. Daybreak — §77
    Why torturing a soul provoked no horror when torturing a body did.

5. The polemic at full pitch, and its limits

Read the closing indictment and the retrospective together, and ask what the change of register gains and costs.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature
    The charge against a morality hostile to the passions.
  2. Twilight of the Idols — The 'Improvers' of Mankind
    Christianity set beside another system of breeding and taming.
  3. The Antichrist — §§61–62
    The closing sentence of judgment, in its actual wording.
  4. Ecce Homo — Why I Am a Destiny §§7–9
    The campaign claimed, retrospectively, as the centre of his work.

Keep these distinctions in view

Jesus and the church
The Antichrist separates the practice it attributes to Jesus from the institution Paul is said to have built. Nietzsche is not attacking the figure; he is attacking what was made of him and the psychology that made it. Losing this distinction makes the book unreadable.
Refutation and diagnosis
Nietzsche is not arguing that Christian claims are false. He is asking what kind of person needs them and what holding them produces. That is a different enterprise, and it neither requires nor supplies a disproof.
Ressentiment and ordinary objection to harm
Ressentiment names a specific reactive formation: value created by first saying no to an outside. It is not a synonym for resentment, for anger at injury, or for any complaint made by the weak. The site protects this distinction throughout; see master and slave morality.
Historical claim and psychological claim
The account of priestly castes, of the Judaean priesthood, and of the early church is history, and weak history. The account of what guilt and asceticism do to a person is psychology, and much stronger. They arrive interwoven and have to be assessed separately.
Nietzsche's polemic against Judaism and his polemic against Christianity
Genealogy I and The Antichrist §§24–27 make claims about a priestly people that have been quoted in bad faith for a century. The passages are ugly; they also sit beside others in which Nietzsche attacks antisemites directly and reserves his contempt for Christian Europe rather than for Jews. State what is there, in its own words, and do not smooth either side of it away.
Middle period and late period
Human, All Too Human and Daybreak treat Christianity as a historical formation to be understood. The Antichrist treats it as an enemy to be destroyed. The analysis is largely continuous; the register is not, and the change is itself evidence about the late work.
Description and endorsement
Showing that a morality was produced by weakness is not an argument that the strong should rule, and Nietzsche does not always keep the two apart. Where a passage moves from diagnosis to prescription, the move should be marked rather than absorbed.
What replaces it
The critique is largely negative. What a post-Christian valuation would consist in is the subject of revaluation of values, and the fact that the destructive half is far more developed than the constructive one is a real feature of the corpus, not an oversight of this page.

Human, All Too Human4 reading assignments

The Third Division, "The Religious Life," is where the 1878 book treats the subject. The tone is investigative rather than hostile, and these sections are the best evidence that the late polemic rests on a long and patient analysis rather than replacing one.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §111

    Where religious practice came from

    Zimmern: The Origin of the Religious Cult.

    Question: If cult practice began as an attempt to influence nature, what has changed when the same forms are directed at a moral god?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reconstructs religious ritual as an early attempt to compel natural forces by the same means used on people, and treats the shift to a moral relationship with a deity as a later development. It supplies the historical depth behind the later claim that Christianity is one formation among others. The reconstruction draws on the comparative religion of his day and should be read as a hypothesis of that period.

    Read alongside: §113; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: §111 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §113

    Making a familiar thing strange

    Zimmern: Christianity As Antiquity.

    Question: What does the estrangement expose that familiarity conceals?

    Why it belongs: The theme's method in one short section. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §114.

    Online edition: §113 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §114

    What is un-Greek in Christianity

    Zimmern: What Is Un-greek in Christianity.

    Question: The comparison is with Greek religion rather than with unbelief. What does choosing that comparison make visible?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche contrasts a religion in which gods are not moral judges and human beings are not sinners with one organized around guilt and redemption, and treats the difference as the decisive one. The section establishes early that the critique proceeds by comparison with another live possibility rather than by appeal to a secular alternative. His picture of Greek religion is idealized and selective, which is worth holding in view.

    Read alongside: Twilight — Morality as Anti-Nature; Apollonian and Dionysian.

    Online edition: §114 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §132

    The need for redemption examined

    Zimmern: Of the Christian Need of Redemption.

    Question: Nietzsche offers a psychological account of conversion. What would count against it?

    Why it belongs: The section reconstructs the experience of sin, grace and redemption as a sequence of interpretations placed on ordinary states of feeling, and argues that the sequence explains itself without the theology. It is the middle period's fullest treatment of the psychology the late books assume, and it is far more careful in saying what it is and is not claiming. Note the absence of contempt: Nietzsche presents the believer as mistaken about the causes of his own states, not as despicable.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§11–22; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §132 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak3 reading assignments

The 1881 book contains the corpus's most extended psychological reconstruction of a founding figure, and its treatment of asceticism precedes the Genealogy's by six years.

  • Core

    Daybreak §68

    Paul read as a psychological problem

    Kennedy: The First Christian.

    Question: What is being explained — a doctrine, a person, or the relation between them?

    Why it belongs: The founding structural claim of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§28–35.

    Online edition: §68 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §72

    What the thought of an afterlife does to this life

    Kennedy: After Death.

    Question: How does a belief about what follows death change the value of what precedes it?

    Why it belongs: The section examines the pressure exerted by the prospect of judgment and eternal consequence, and treats it as the source of a distinctive kind of fear rather than of comfort. It is the middle-period version of the charge that The Antichrist §43 makes at full volume, and the comparison shows how much the late formulation gains in force and loses in care.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§39–43; Death of God.

    Online edition: §72 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §77

    Torture of the body and torture of the soul

    Kennedy: The Tortures of the Soul.

    Question: Why does the same culture that recoils from physical cruelty accept the doctrine of eternal punishment without protest?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche observes that we shudder at the thought of torturing a body while a doctrine of everlasting punishment has tormented souls for centuries with an entirely good conscience, and asks how the asymmetry survives. It is the middle period's sharpest single charge, and it is made without any of the late vocabulary of decadence or ressentiment — which is worth noting, since the charge does not depend on that apparatus. The passage also shows the critique operating as an appeal to a moral response his readers already have, rather than against morality as such.

    Read alongside: §72; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: §77 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

The 1882 book supplies the announcement of what has happened to the belief; Book V, added in 1887, supplies the analysis of belief as such. The madman's announcement is treated at length on Death of God and appears here only for its bearing on this theme.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §125

    The announcement, and its reception

    Common: The Madman.

    Question: The madman's hearers do not believe in God either. Why is he still unintelligible to them?

    Why it belongs: The section matters here for one reason: it distinguishes the loss of a belief from the loss of everything the belief supported, and makes the second the real event. For this theme it means that the critique of Christianity cannot finish when assent is withdrawn — the valuations outlive the doctrine. The section is treated fully on Death of God; it is cross-referenced rather than duplicated here.

    Read alongside: Death of God; Nihilism.

    Online edition: §125 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §347

    The need to believe, examined as a need

    Question: What would disconfirm the diagnosis that faith measures need?

    Why it belongs: The generalization of the critique beyond Christianity. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — III, On Apostates; The Antichrist §§50, 52, 54–55.

    Online edition: §347 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §358

    The Reformation read as a rebellion of the simple

    Common: The Peasant Revolt of the Spirit.

    Question: Nietzsche prefers the church he is attacking to the movement that reformed it. What does that preference reveal about the critique?

    Why it belongs: The section reads Luther's revolt as the resentment of the straightforward against an institution that had become subtle, spiritual and complicated, and regrets what was destroyed. It is the single best guard against reading Nietzsche as simply anti-Christian: what he values is form, distance and achieved complexity, and he will credit a church with those against a reformer. It also shows the critique's aristocratic commitments plainly, which is uncomfortable and should not be edited out.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§45–62; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: §358 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra2 reading assignments

The book dramatizes rather than analyses, and its treatment of religious figures is markedly more generous than the late polemic's. The free Common edition numbers chapters I–LXXX continuously and uses different titles; match by part and chapter title.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Priests

    The adversary addressed with pity

    Common: The Priests.

    Question: Zarathustra says his blood is related to theirs. What does the admission change?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra calls the priests his enemies and then says he suffers with them and that they are prisoners marked out, and asks for a redeemer they would recognize. Set beside The Antichrist, the difference in bearing is striking: the same analysis delivered with compassion rather than contempt. Which version is the considered one is a real question about the late work.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§24–27; III, On Apostates.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, On Apostates

    Those who go back

    Common: The Apostates.

    Question: Why does the return to piety after unbelief interest Nietzsche more than piety that never lapsed?

    Why it belongs: The chapter describes those who were briefly free and then crept back to faith, and treats the relapse as a failure of nerve rather than a change of mind. It matters for this theme because it locates the critique's real target: not doctrine but the need that keeps reaching for one, which is the same point The Gay Science §347 makes analytically.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §347; Free spirits.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil3 reading assignments

Part III, §§45–62, is the book's sustained treatment. The free Zimmern edition titles this part The Religious Mood where Kaufmann has The Religious Nature; it has no per-section anchors, so links open the part and you should use Find for the section number.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§46–47

    What faith was, and what it costs

    Question: What is given up in the sacrifice of the spirit, and to whom is it addressed?

    Why it belongs: Two sections from early in Part III, taken as a short slice for the first encounter. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §§45–62.

    Online edition: Part III (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§45–62, Part III entire

    The sustained treatment, including the uses of religion

    Question: §§61–62 describe what religion can do for a philosopher who does not believe. How does that sit with the rest of the critique?

    Why it belongs: The whole part, assigned here rather than in the first encounter because eighteen sections is a fuller-route commitment rather than an entry point. It contains the analysis of the religious neurosis, the saint as a problem for psychology, §56 on the most world-affirming ideal, and — in §§61–62 — a coldly instrumental account of religion as a means of ruling and of cultivating types. That instrumental passage is the part most often left out of summaries, and it complicates any reading of the critique as straightforwardly liberating: Nietzsche objects to what Christianity has produced, not to the use of religion as an instrument.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §358; Eternal recurrence.

    Online edition: Part III (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §195

    The compressed version of the inversion

    Question: The section is four lines long and makes a large historical claim. What is it resting on?

    Why it belongs: The inversion of values attributed to a priestly people, stated in its most compressed published form, a year before the Genealogy expands it. It belongs here because the compression is the problem: with the argument removed, what remains is an assertion about a people that has been quoted in isolation and in bad faith. Read it with Genealogy I §§7–10 supplying what it omits, and with the key distinction above about Nietzsche's two polemics in view.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§7–10; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals4 reading assignments

The 1887 book supplies the theme's analytical core across all three essays. The Preface and each essay are numbered separately in every edition, and Nietzsche's closing Note follows Essay I §17. For a complete reading of all three essays in order, use the twelve-lesson course.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§7–10

    The inversion, and the birth of ressentiment

    Question: What is claimed to have been reversed, and by whom?

    Why it belongs: The analytical core of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation, including the note on the passages about peoples.

    Read alongside: Essay I §16; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§7–10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §16

    A struggle described as still undecided

    Question: If the struggle is undecided, what is the status of the genealogy that describes it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche sets the two opposed valuations against each other as a conflict running through millennia, names Rome and Judaea as its symbols, and says the fight continues and has not been concluded. It matters because it presents the inversion as a historical contest rather than a completed catastrophe, which leaves room for the revaluation project. The Rome-and-Judaea framing is also where the essay's rhetoric is at its most dangerous, and reading it as a symbolic opposition does not make the wording disappear.

    Read alongside: Essay I §§7–10; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: Essay I §16 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§19–22

    Guilt before a creditor who cannot be repaid

    Question: How does a debt to ancestors become a debt that increases the more it is acknowledged?

    Why it belongs: These sections trace the obligation owed to founders and ancestors up to the point where the creditor is a maximal god and the debt becomes unpayable, then read the Christian device of a god sacrificing himself as the only conceivable settlement and as the moment guilt turns fully inward. It is the theme's strongest structural argument, and it is where the critique of Christianity and the account of bad conscience become the same argument. Keep bad conscience distinct from the slave revolt: they are different mechanisms in different essays.

    Read alongside: Suffering and cruelty; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Essay II §§19–22 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§11–22

    The priest as physician to a suffering he also deepens

    Question: If the ascetic priest genuinely relieves suffering, in what sense is the relief an injury?

    Why it belongs: The long central run of the third essay, assigned here rather than in the first encounter because twelve sections is a fuller-route commitment. Nietzsche presents the ascetic priest as the direction-changer of ressentiment, turning the sufferer's search for a cause inward, and catalogues the means — the deadening of feeling, mechanical activity, small joys, the organization of the weak, and the orgy of feeling produced by guilt. The analysis credits the priest with real skill and real usefulness while arguing that the treatment makes the patient sicker. This double judgment is the most philosophically serious thing in the theme, and flattening it into simple denunciation loses the argument entirely.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §132; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Essay III §§11–22 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols2 reading assignments

The 1888 book compresses the charge into two chapters. Section numbers restart in each chapter and Ludovici's chapter titles differ from Kaufmann's; see the edition guidance.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols Morality as Anti-Nature

    A morality at war with the passions

    Ludovici: Morality as the Enemy of Nature.

    Question: Nietzsche distinguishes destroying a passion from spiritualizing it. Which does he attribute to the church, and on what grounds?

    Why it belongs: The chapter accuses the church of attacking the passions at the root rather than transforming them, and treats that hostility as a sign of an inability to exercise moderation. It states the positive standard the whole critique is measured against — that a morality should be assessed by whether it strengthens or weakens the life that holds it. Whether that standard can do the work asked of it is a fair question, and it is not one Nietzsche argues for here.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §114; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: Morality as Anti-Nature (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols The 'Improvers' of Mankind

    Christianity beside another system of breeding and taming

    Question: What is the comparison meant to establish, and does the evidence support it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche sets Christian moral training beside the Law of Manu, argues that both are systems of taming or breeding rather than improvement, and claims that all such systems have been immoral by their own lights. It is the most direct comparative moment in the late work. It is also where the historical material is weakest: Nietzsche's Manu material came through Louis Jacolliot's 1876 popular treatment, which Nietzsche described as a French translation; it is not a reliable text of the Law of Manu, and his account of the caste system cannot be taken as a reliable description. The chapter contains some of his most brutal formulations, and they are his.

    Read alongside: Genealogy as method; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: The 'Improvers' of Mankind (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Antichrist7 reading assignments

The 1888 polemic is the theme's largest single text, and the one where register and argument are hardest to separate. The free Mencken text retains all 62 section numbers but omits portions of §§29, 35 and 38 and the separate Law Against Christianity; the course follows Kaufmann. See the edition guidance and the Antichrist course for a complete reading.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §7

    Pity attacked as a depressant

    Question: Is the objection to pity an empirical claim about its effects, or a stipulation about what counts as strength?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche calls Christianity the religion of pity, argues that pity multiplies misery by preserving what is ripe for destruction, and names it a practical nihilism. It is among the least guarded passages in the corpus and among the most often quoted against him. State it plainly: the passage says what it appears to say, and the reading that softens it into a warning about sentimentality has to account for the rest of the section. It is also where the critique's consequences for actual people become impossible to keep abstract.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §77; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: §7 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §§10–12

    German philosophy as theology in disguise

    Question: If a secular philosophy inherits a theological structure, what would show it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that the theologian's instinct survives in philosophy, with Kant as the central case, and that idealism is a way of keeping the beyond after the beyond has been given up. It shows that the target is not confined to the churches, and it connects directly to the claim on perspectivism that the will to truth is the ascetic ideal's latest form.

    Read alongside: Perspectivism and the will to truth; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: §§10–12 (trans. H. L. Mencken; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §15

    An entire vocabulary called imaginary

    Question: What is the argument here, as opposed to the classification?

    Why it belongs: The section lists God, soul, spirit, free will, sin, redemption and grace as an imaginary world of causes and effects, and reads the whole scheme as produced by a particular condition. It is the compressed form of the theme's central claim, and a good place to see how much of the late method is carried by tone and classification rather than by demonstration.

    Read alongside: Genealogy as method; Human, All Too Human §132.

    Online edition: §15 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §§24–27

    The inversion retold as a historical event

    Question: How does this version differ from Genealogy I, and which differences are argumentative rather than rhetorical?

    Why it belongs: The same inversion described in the Genealogy, now narrated as a specific sequence of events involving a priestly reworking of a people's history and the falsification of its god. The historical claims are stronger, more specific and considerably less supported than the 1887 versions. As with Genealogy I, the language about a people is Nietzsche's own and is not softened here; and as there, it sits alongside his repeated contempt for the antisemites of his own day. Both facts belong in any honest account.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§7–10; Beyond Good and Evil §195.

    Online edition: §§24–27 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §§28–35

    Jesus as a psychological type

    Question: Nietzsche describes this figure almost without hostility. What is he separating, and what does the separation cost the polemic?

    Why it belongs: The strangest and most important stretch of the book for this theme. Nietzsche reconstructs Jesus as a type incapable of resistance or resentment, for whom the kingdom is a present condition rather than a promise, and insists that what followed was the opposite of what this figure practised. The portrait is drawn with evident care and even admiration, and it is also frankly speculative — Nietzsche concedes the difficulty of recovering the type from the sources and proceeds anyway. Note that the free Mencken text omits portions of §§29 and 35; use the preferred edition for these sections if you can.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §68; §§39–43.

    Online edition: §§28–35 (trans. H. L. Mencken; this text omits portions of §§29 and 35) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    The Antichrist §§39–43

    The centre of gravity placed outside life

    Question: Is the charge about doctrine, psychology, or consequences?

    Why it belongs: The polemic's central charge, in five sections rather than twenty. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §72; §§61–62.

    Online edition: §§39–43 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    The Antichrist §§61–62

    The closing judgment, in its actual wording

    Question: The book ends in the form of a verdict. What has been argued for, and what is simply pronounced?

    Why it belongs: §61 treats the Renaissance as a revaluation that the Reformation destroyed; §62 delivers the closing indictment, calling Christianity the one great curse and ending in a formal condemnation. It is the furthest point the rhetoric reaches, and reading it is the only way to assess what the late Nietzsche actually wrote rather than a tidied summary of it. The judgment is pronounced, not demonstrated, and the gap between the two is the honest finding of this theme.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §358; Ecce Homo — Why I Am a Destiny §§7–9.

    Online edition: §§61–62 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo1 reading assignment

Written in 1888 and published posthumously. Retrospective throughout, and evidence about how Nietzsche understood his own campaign rather than about its effects.

  • Extension · retrospective

    Ecce Homo Why I Am a Destiny §§7–9

    The campaign claimed as the centre of the work

    Ludovici: Why I Am a Fatality.

    Question: How much of this account of the critique is borne out by the books themselves?

    Why it belongs: The closing chapter gathers the attack on Christian morality into Nietzsche's self-description as a destiny and as the first immoralist, and ends in the formula about Dionysus against the Crucified. It shows how he wanted the campaign placed, and the register is at its most extreme. Read as evidence of intention, not of achievement; the claims about his own historical significance are assertions.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§61–62; Revaluation of values.

Notebook material

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Its entries can show what he was working out in private. They do not establish what the published works claim, and on this theme the published works are extensive enough that the notebooks add little the books do not already say more carefully.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. These are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different dates. Edition guidance.

The compilation's editors gathered a large quantity of material on Christianity into Book One, under a heading about the critique of religion. Three entries are given here, identified individually rather than as a range. No short starting route is offered: on this theme the published polemic is long, complete and unambiguous, and a notebook route would add emphasis without adding argument.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §200

    The charge stated without argument

    Question: What does this entry add to The Antichrist, and what does it leave out?

    Why it belongs: The entry calls Christianity the most fatal and seductive lie yet in existence. It is useful chiefly as a control: the published books say the same thing with a case attached, and setting the two side by side shows what the argument contributes and how little is left when it is removed. Read it as a note to self, which is what it is.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§61–62.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §207

    Early Christianity read politically

    Question: If the early movement is described as the abolition of the state, what kind of critique is being made?

    Why it belongs: The entry treats primitive Christianity as a withdrawal from civic obligation — the refusal of oaths, of military service, of law courts — and reads it as an attack on the conditions of any political order. It shows a line of thought the published books gesture at but never develop, and it is a good example of the notebooks containing directions Nietzsche did not pursue rather than doctrines he suppressed.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §§24–27; The herd and the last man.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §216

    What happens when the powerful adopt it

    Question: Does a morality change when it is taken up by those it was formed against?

    Why it belongs: The entry considers the case where masters become Christians, and asks what a morality of the weak becomes in the hands of the strong. It sharpens a question the Genealogy raises but does not settle: whether the inversion is a permanent feature of the values themselves or a function of who holds them. As with the others, it is a working note and should not be read as a conclusion.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §16; Master and slave morality.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The same analysis, two registers. Read Human, All Too Human §132 beside The Antichrist §15. What does the ten-year gap change — the argument, or only its temperature?
  • The founder and the institution. Follow Daybreak §68 into The Antichrist §§28–35 and then §§39–43. How firmly does the separation hold, and what would break it?
  • Where the critique turns aristocratic. Read The Gay Science §358 with Beyond Good and Evil §§61–62. What is Nietzsche defending when he defends the church against the Reformation?
  • What survives the loss of the belief. Pair The Gay Science §125 with Genealogy III §§11–22. If the valuations outlive the doctrine, what would actually count as the end of Christianity?

A question to carry forward

Nietzsche's diagnosis can absorb almost any response: assent confirms it, objection can be read as the need it describes. What evidence would tell against the diagnosis, and does the critique provide any way of producing it?
