"Body am I entirely, and nothing else; and soul is only a word for something about the body." The sentence is easy to quote and hard to hold, because "body" is doing at least five different jobs across the corpus and they do not all support each other.

The naturalism is real. Nietzsche thinks the concepts that have organized European philosophy — the soul, the unified ego, free will, the responsible subject — describe nothing, and that the work of philosophy now includes explaining why they seemed to. He is attentive to the physiological literature of his century, he treats health and illness as philosophically relevant rather than as biographical noise, and in the late books "physiological" becomes his standard word for the level at which a judgment is to be explained.

But the word "body" in Nietzsche is not the body of a physiology textbook. It names an organism understood as a plurality of drives in some arrangement with each other, with consciousness as a late and partial instrument attached to it. That is a philosophical proposal, not a scientific finding, and Nietzsche's own confidence about it runs well ahead of anything the nineteenth century could establish. Taking it seriously means being able to say which of his claims is empirical, which is conceptual, and which is a way of redescribing something already believed.

The risk on this page is specific, and it is the reason for its structure. Once "the body" is available as an explanation, it can be used to explain anything: a moral judgment, a metaphysical system, a preference in music, an entire culture. At that point it has stopped being an argument and become a vocabulary. The section below separates the jobs the word is doing, so that a reader can tell when a passage is making a claim and when it is applying a label.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves chronologically from the middle-period discovery of the drives, through the dramatic statement and the dismantling of the willing subject, to the late physiological vocabulary and its autobiographical demonstration. Continue through the fuller route, which is organized around the five distinct uses of the term, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook readings are a short optional appendix rather than a full route.

Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order and in chronological sequence. The route begins with the drives rather than with the famous sentence, because the claim about the body only does philosophical work once there is an account of what the body is supposed to consist of. The late physiological vocabulary comes last, where its dependence on the earlier arguments is visible.

  1. Core · 1881

    Daybreak §119

    Experience as the surface of something not observed

    Kennedy: Experience and Invention. The free edition is The Dawn of Day.

    Question: Nietzsche says our waking life is as invented as our dreams. What exactly is supposed to be doing the inventing?

    Why it belongs: The most important middle-period section on this theme and the right place to start. Nietzsche proposes that the drives are nourished by whatever the day happens to supply, that no one knows their own drives as such, and that what we call experience is an interpretation laid over a play of impulses we do not observe — so that even our moral self-assessments are a kind of invention. It puts the theme on a footing that is psychological before it is physiological, and it introduces the plurality the later "body" is supposed to name. It is also careful in a way the late work often is not: Nietzsche presents the account as a conjecture about something inaccessible, which is the honest form of the claim.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §119 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Despisers of the Body"

    Body am I entirely

    Common: The Despisers of the Body. Common renders the key sentence "Body am I entirely, and nothing more; and soul is only the name of something in the body."

    Question: The speech replaces one unity with another. What kind of unity is the body supposed to be, and how does it differ from the soul it replaces?

    Why it belongs: The dramatic statement, and the source of nearly every quotation on this subject. Zarathustra addresses those who despise the body, tells them that the body is a great reason and the spirit a small one, calls the body a plurality with one sense, a war and a peace, a herd and a herdsman, and describes the self behind the "I" as something that dwells in the body and is the body. Read it carefully and quickly twice: the rhetoric moves fast, and the substantive claim — that the organizing work attributed to a conscious self is done by something the conscious self does not see — is easy to lose in it. Note also what it does not do. It asserts the reversal without arguing for it, and the argument has to come from the sections on either side of it in this route.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions"; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Despisers of the Body" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §§13, 17, 19

    Taking the willing subject apart

    Part I, "On the Prejudices of Philosophers." Section numbers run continuously across the book's nine parts.

    Question: §19 breaks willing into sensations, a thought and an affect of command. Which of those three is doing the work the word "will" was supposed to do?

    Why it belongs: The argued version of what Zarathustra declaims. §13 says that a living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength and that self-preservation is only one consequence of that. §17 argues that a thought comes when it wishes and not when "I" wish, so that the "I" in "I think" is an interpretation of the process rather than an observation of it. §19 is the central one: willing is analysed into sensations of the state one leaves and the state one moves toward, a ruling thought, and above all an affect of command — and Nietzsche adds that we are at once the one commanding and the one obeying, which is why the success of the command gets mistaken for a simple act of will. Read §19 slowly. It is the closest thing in the published work to an account of what a body is on this theme, and it is where the concept of the drives becomes a claim about agency.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §12; Will to power.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  4. Development · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §382

    A health that has to be given up to be had

    Kaufmann: The great health. The free edition is titled The Joyful Wisdom.

    Question: "Health" here is not the absence of illness. What is it the name of, and is the usage doing argumentative work or rhetorical work?

    Why it belongs: The section describes a health that one does not merely have but must keep acquiring because one keeps giving it up, and connects it to an ideal of a spirit that plays naively with everything that has counted as holy and good. It matters on this page because it is where the physiological vocabulary stops being descriptive and becomes evaluative: health names a capacity to sustain experiments and to recover from them, not a bodily state a physician could confirm. That shift is worth marking, because most of the late uses of "healthy" and "sick" inherit it, and a reader who takes them as medical claims will be misreading them — while a reader who takes them as purely metaphorical will miss that Nietzsche thought they were continuous with the medical sense.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2; Wagner and decadence.

    Online edition: §382 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "The Four Great Errors"; "'Reason' in Philosophy" §1

    Inner causes reconsidered, and the senses defended

    Ludovici: "The Four Great Errors" and "'Reason' in Philosophy." Section numbers restart in each chapter.

    Question: If the inner world of motives contains no causes, what happens to introspective reports about why one did something?

    Why it belongs: "The Four Great Errors" is where the naturalism turns into a method. Nietzsche argues that we took our concept of cause from an inner experience of willing that does not contain causes, that we then supply imaginary causes after the fact for states whose real sources are physiological, and that the sense of free will is one such construction. The point for this theme is not the denial of freedom but the model of self-knowledge it replaces: the conscious report is an interpretation of a condition, not a record of it. "'Reason' in Philosophy" §1 supplies the complementary charge — that philosophers hate becoming and prefer a mummified world — which is the reason the body has been the standing casualty of the tradition.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§2–3; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: "The Four Great Errors" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; for "'Reason' in Philosophy" use the contents) · Preferred edition.

  6. Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2; "Why I Am So Clever" §1, §10

    The doctrine applied to its author

    Free edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici). Written in 1888 and first published in 1908.

    Question: Nietzsche treats nutrition and climate as philosophical questions. Is that the doctrine being demonstrated, or the doctrine being tested?

    Why it belongs: The chapters where the naturalism is carried out in the first person: illness and recovery described as the conditions under which certain perspectives became available, and the practical questions of diet, place and daily rhythm treated with complete seriousness as matters philosophy has neglected. It is genuinely illuminating about what the doctrine is supposed to mean in practice, and it is the place where its weakness is most exposed — the claims about what his own physiology explains are unfalsifiable, and they are made by someone with an obvious interest in them. Read it as the doctrine's most vivid application and its least independent evidence, and keep The Gay Science §382 in view as the more careful version of the same thought.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §382; Amor fati.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; for "Why I Am So Clever" use the contents) · Preferred edition.

Five things "the body" is doing

The word does at least five different jobs, and they have different standards of evidence. Separating them is the single most useful discipline on this theme, because the slogan "body am I entirely" makes them look like one claim.

1. Physiology — a claim about explanation
That certain judgments, moods and philosophical positions are to be explained by the state of the organism that produced them. This is an empirical claim and it can be wrong. In the late work it is asserted far more often than it is supported, and Nietzsche's own examples — Wagner, Socrates, Saint Paul — are diagnoses at a distance of centuries. Twilight, "The Four Great Errors" and the late polemics.
2. Drives — a claim about composition
That what acts in a person is a plurality of impulses in some arrangement, rather than a single agent. This is a conceptual proposal about how to describe a person, and most of Nietzsche's distinctive psychology depends on it. Daybreak §119, Beyond Good and Evil §12 and §19.
3. Affects — a claim about access
That feelings and inclinations are not noise interfering with judgment but the means by which anything is registered as mattering at all. This is why Genealogy III §12 can say that more affects mean a more complete concept, and it is the point where this theme and perspectivism stop being separable.
4. Embodiment — a claim about the self
That there is no thinking thing standing behind or above the organism, so that the "I" is an interpretation rather than an observer. This is the anti-Cartesian claim, and it is argued for — in Beyond Good and Evil §§16–17 — rather than merely announced. Zarathustra I, "On the Despisers of the Body."
5. Interpretation — a claim about what conscious reports are
That what reaches consciousness has already been simplified, generalized and translated, so that introspection delivers an interpretation and not a record. The Gay Science §354, Daybreak §119. This is the claim that makes the other four hard to check, including by Nietzsche.

The five do not entail one another. A reader could accept that the self is not a Cartesian ego (4) while doubting that a particular philosopher's argument is explained by his digestion (1). The drives (2) can be accepted as a description without the physiological reductions (1) following. And claim (5) sits awkwardly with claim (1), since a doctrine that makes conscious reports unreliable also makes it difficult to say how the physiological diagnosis was arrived at. That tension is not resolved in the published work, and this page does not resolve it either. What it does is mark which claim is being made where, so that a passage can be assessed on the right terms.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Five investigations, one for each use of the term. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below.

1. The drives, and what they are supposed to be

Start with the composition claim, because the rest of the theme leans on it. Ask in each case whether the drives are being observed, inferred, or posited.

  1. Daybreak — §119
    The unobserved play of impulses beneath what we call experience.
  2. Daybreak — §38
    The same drive wearing different moral costumes as the evaluation changes.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §12
    The soul kept as a topic and abandoned as an atom: soul as a social structure of drives and affects.
  4. Daybreak — §560
    Cultivating drives like a gardener — and the question of who is supposed to be doing the gardening.

2. The self that was supposed to be doing it

The anti-Cartesian argument, which is the best-supported part of the theme.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §§17, 19
    A thought that comes when it wishes; willing analysed into commanding and obeying.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Despisers of the Body"
    The self that is the body, and the "I" as its instrument.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §13
    The doer added to the deed by grammar, and what that removes.
  4. Human, All Too Human — §39
    The middle-period dismantling of intelligible freedom, and its cost.

3. What consciousness is for

The interpretation claim. Follow the proposal that awareness is a late instrument shaped by a social requirement.

  1. The Gay Science — §354
    Consciousness developed under the pressure of the need to communicate.
  2. Human, All Too Human — §11
    Language taken for knowledge of the world, and the reification of words.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "The Four Great Errors"
    Imaginary causes supplied after the fact for states with other sources.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §230
    Knowing described as an appetite to incorporate and simplify, not a mirror.

4. Affects as access rather than interference

The claim that makes this theme inseparable from the account of knowledge. Test whether it can be held without collapsing into either sentimentalism or relativism.

  1. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions"
    Passions renamed as virtues, and the danger in the renaming.
  2. The Gay Science — §290
    Giving style to one's character: the constructive use of the drives.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "Morality as Anti-Nature"
    Moralities that attack the passions at the root, read as an inability to discipline them.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §6
    Philosophy as the involuntary memoir of the philosopher who wrote it.

5. Physiological explanation, and where it overreaches

The most exposed claim on the page. Read the strongest applications and ask what would count against them.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§6–10
    The philosopher's asceticism read as working conditions rather than conviction.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§17–19
    The priest's remedies described as physiological interventions under a spiritual description.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "Skirmishes" §§47–49
    Beauty, decline and the affirmative type given bodily descriptions.
  4. Ecce Homo — "Why I Am So Wise" §6
    The diagnostic instrument claimed as a sense of smell, with no way of checking it.

Keep these distinctions in view

The body as substrate and the body as plurality
The first move refuses the soul/body pair. The second, and more important, refuses the assumption that the body is one thing: it is described as a structure of drives in a relation of command and obedience. Readings that replace the soul with a simple organism have taken the first step and stopped.
Physiology as science and physiology as vocabulary
Nietzsche read the physiological literature of his time and used its terms. In the late polemics "physiological" often marks the level at which he thinks a judgment should be explained rather than reporting anything established. Both uses are present, sometimes in the same paragraph, and telling them apart is most of the work.
Health as condition and health as evaluation
The Gay Science §382 makes the great health a capacity that is repeatedly lost and reacquired. Once that is in place, "healthy" and "sick" in the late work are doing evaluative work with a medical surface. That does not make them empty, but it does mean they cannot be cashed out by any actual diagnosis.
Drives and instincts
Nietzsche uses Trieb, Instinkt and Affekt with less system than a reader might hope, and translators differ. Do not build an interpretation on a distinction between them that the German does not sustain; do notice when a passage needs the drives to be many and when it needs them to be organized.
Explaining a belief and refuting it
A physiological account of why someone holds a view is not an argument against the view. Nietzsche knows this — Human, All Too Human §1 and Daybreak §103 are explicit about the difference — and the late polemics regularly proceed as if he did not.
The body against metaphysics, and the body as metaphysics
The critique of substance applies to the body too. If unities are suspect, an organism described as "a great reason" needs to say why it is not another posited unity. Beyond Good and Evil §19 is the closest thing to an answer, and the critique of metaphysics page carries the objection.
Naturalism and reduction
Nietzsche's naturalism is a demand that explanations stay within the world rather than a claim that everything is really chemistry. The Gay Science §373 calls a merely mechanical reading of the world one of the stupidest possible interpretations, which is a useful check on any account of this theme that makes him a materialist of that kind.

Human, All Too Human3 reading assignments

The 1878 book is where the naturalism becomes a programme. The register is analytic and the conclusions are stated with more hesitation than the late work allows. Preferred edition: Hollingdale.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §1

    A chemistry of concepts and sensations

    Zimmern: Chemistry of Ideas and Sensations.

    Question: The proposal is to explain the highest things from the lowest. What does the metaphor of chemistry commit Nietzsche to?

    Why it belongs: The opening section asks what would follow if the most valued things were entangled with their apparent opposites, and proposes a chemistry of moral, religious and aesthetic ideas. It is the programme statement for the naturalism, and it is careful about its own status: the question is raised, the chemistry is proposed, and no result is claimed. Read it as the standard the late physiological diagnoses should be held to.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The Four Great Errors"; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: §§1–2 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §11

    Language mistaken for knowledge of the world

    Zimmern: Language As a Presumptive Science.

    Question: If subject-and-predicate makes us posit a thing behind every activity, what does it make us posit behind thinking?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that people believed they had knowledge of the world in language and acquired an enormous quantity of error instead, and that treating words as things has done lasting damage. On this page it supplies the mechanism behind the persistence of the soul: not a doctrine anyone chose but a habit built into how a sentence is put together. It is the middle-period version of what Zarathustra will declaim and Twilight will compress.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Despisers of the Body"; Aphorism and style.

    Online edition: §11 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §39

    The fable of intelligible freedom

    Zimmern: The Fable of Intelligible Freedom.

    Question: Nietzsche traces the history of the feeling of responsibility. Does showing how a feeling arose settle whether it is appropriate?

    Why it belongs: The section reconstructs how responsibility came to be attached first to actions, then to motives, then to the person's whole nature, and concludes that at each stage the attribution was a mistake. It is the earliest full statement of the position Twilight's fourth error compresses, and it is more careful about the argument's form. It also shows where the naturalism hurts: Nietzsche says the consequence is that nobody is answerable for anything, and does not soften it.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The Four Great Errors"; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: §39 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak4 reading assignments

The laboratory of the drives, and the book that does most for this theme without using its late vocabulary. Preferred translation: Hollingdale; the free edition is The Dawn of Day, translated by J. M. Kennedy.

  • Development

    Daybreak §38

    One drive, several moral costumes

    Kennedy: Impulses Transformed by Moral Judgments.

    Question: If the same impulse can be praised or condemned depending on the surrounding valuation, what has the moral judgment been about?

    Why it belongs: The section observes that a drive can be named a virtue in one setting and a vice in another without any change in the drive itself, so that what varies is the evaluation attached to it. It is the middle-period foundation for everything the Genealogy later does historically, and it is the clearest case of the composition claim doing real work: without the drives as a separate layer, there is nothing for the evaluations to be evaluations of.

    Read alongside: §119; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §38 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Daybreak §119

    Experience as the surface of something not observed

    Question: What is doing the inventing, if the conscious self is not?

    Why it belongs: The most important middle-period section on this theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §354; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §119 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Daybreak §202

    Moral categories and what medicine had begun to reclassify

    Kennedy: The Care of the Health.

    Question: Replacing moral description with medical description changes who is responsible. What else does it change?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche compares the treatment of the criminal with the earlier treatment of the sick, and argues that moral categories lag behind changes in how conditions come to be understood. It is the middle period's most concrete application of the naturalism, and it is also where the cost becomes visible: a vocabulary of sickness and health applied to conduct removes blame and introduces a different kind of authority. That trade-off is one the late work makes constantly and examines rarely.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: §202 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §560

    The gardener, and who is supposed to be holding the spade

    Question: If a person can cultivate their drives like a gardener, what part of them is doing the cultivating?

    Why it belongs: The section says one can deal with one's drives as a gardener does with plants, and that very few people know they may do this. It is the constructive counterpart to §119, and it is the sharpest internal problem for the theme: the gardener image needs an agent that stands over the drives, which is exactly what the composition claim was supposed to remove. Nietzsche never resolves this in the published work, and it is worth reading his later talk of commanding and obeying drives as an attempt to describe the gardening without reinstating the gardener.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §19; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: §560 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

Book IV (1882) supplies the constructive image of shaping a character; Book V (1887) supplies the account of consciousness and the great health. The year is marked on each entry.

  • Development · Book IV (1882)

    The Gay Science §290

    Giving style to one's character

    Kaufmann: One thing is needful. Common: One Thing is Needful.

    Question: The section is about arranging what one has, including the ugly parts. Is that compatible with being nothing but a plurality of drives?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes surveying one's strengths and weaknesses and fitting them into an artistic plan until even the weaknesses delight the eye, and says that in the end it is a single taste that rules, whether good or bad. It gives the composition claim a constructive form and it is the most attractive passage on this theme — which is a reason for care. The section says nothing about where the taste comes from, and the same problem as Daybreak §560 is present in a more appealing costume.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: §290 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core of the interpretation claim · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §354

    Consciousness shaped by the need to communicate

    Kaufmann: On the "genius of the species." Common: The "Genius of the Species."

    Question: If what becomes conscious is what can be shared, what follows about self-knowledge in particular?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche proposes that consciousness developed under the pressure of the need to communicate, that it is therefore bound to what is common and averageable, and that whatever becomes conscious is for that reason already generalized and falsified. It is the strongest published support for the claim that introspective reports are interpretations, and it gives the theme a mechanism rather than an assertion. Nietzsche offers it as a conjecture and says so; the speculative character is on the surface and should stay there.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §119; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §354 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §382

    A health that has to be given up to be had

    Question: What is "health" the name of here?

    Why it belongs: Where the physiological vocabulary becomes evaluative. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2; Amor fati.

    Online edition: §382 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra3 reading assignments

The dramatic statement and its immediate sequel. The two Part I speeches are printed consecutively and should be read that way; the Part III chapter shows what the doctrine costs the body that has to absorb it.

  • Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Despisers of the Body"

    Body am I entirely

    Question: What kind of unity is the body supposed to be?

    Why it belongs: The source of nearly every quotation on this theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §19; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Despisers of the Body" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions"

    Passions renamed, and the war between them

    Common: Joys and Passions.

    Question: Zarathustra warns that every virtue is jealous of the others. What does that presuppose about what a virtue is?

    Why it belongs: The speech that follows "On the Despisers of the Body" and supplies its content: a person's passions become their virtues by being named and owned, the virtues then contend with one another, and someone with many of them suffers accordingly. It is the plainest statement in Zarathustra of the composition claim — the person as a field of competing impulses rather than a bearer of qualities — and it is more useful than the famous speech before it, because it says what follows from the picture rather than announcing it.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §38; The Gay Science §290.

    Online edition: Part I, "Joys and Passions" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part III, "The Convalescent"

    A thought that puts the thinker to bed

    Common: The Convalescent.

    Question: Why is the response to a doctrine here physical rather than argumentative?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra calls up his abysmal thought, collapses, and lies as if dead for seven days before he can speak. The chapter is the theme's most direct dramatization of the claim that what a person can think is limited by what their organism can bear — and that a doctrine may therefore be true and still be unavailable. Read as narrative rather than as evidence; the eternal recurrence page takes the doctrinal content.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §382; Eternal recurrence.

    Online edition: Part III, "The Convalescent" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil4 reading assignments

The published book that argues for what the others assert. Part I contains the dismantling of the willing subject; Part VII contains the account of knowing as an appetite. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §6

    Philosophy as a confession

    Part I, "On the Prejudices of Philosophers."

    Question: If a philosophy grows from the moral intentions of its author, does that discredit it, explain it, or neither?

    Why it belongs: The section proposes that every great philosophy has been the personal confession of its author and an involuntary memoir, and that the moral intentions in a system are the seed the rest grew from. It is the methodological licence for reading positions as symptoms, and it is the point at which this theme becomes a way of reading other philosophers. It is also the claim most easily abused, since it gives no criterion for when the diagnosis is warranted.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§6–10; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Core of the composition claim

    Beyond Good and Evil §12

    The soul kept as a topic, discarded as an atom

    Part I, "On the Prejudices of Philosophers."

    Question: Nietzsche expels one conception of the soul and invites several others. What do the replacements have in common?

    Why it belongs: The section proposes getting rid of "soul atomism" — the soul as indestructible, eternal and indivisible, a monad — while insisting that the concept itself need not be abandoned, and names the alternatives: mortal soul, soul as subjective multiplicity, soul as a social structure of the drives and affects. This is the precise statement of what "body" is supposed to mean on this page, and it is the section that keeps the theme from being a simple materialism. It is often confused with §54, which makes a different point about modern philosophy's long attack on the old soul concept.

    Read alongside: §19; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§13, 17, 19

    Taking the willing subject apart

    Question: Which element of §19's analysis does the work the word "will" was supposed to do?

    Why it belongs: The argued core of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §12; Will to power.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §230

    Knowing described as an appetite

    Part VII, "Our Virtues."

    Question: The spirit is said to want to incorporate, simplify and also to be deceived. How do those two tendencies coexist in one account?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes the basic will of the spirit as an appetite to appropriate and assimilate what is foreign, to simplify and to shut things out, together with a counter-will to be deceived and a sudden impulse toward the uncertain. On this page it is the passage where knowing is itself treated as something an organism does, which is what the naturalism has to deliver if it is to be more than a story about mistakes. It is also where the vocabulary is at its most metaphorical: "incorporation" is doing a great deal of work with no account of what it consists in.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §354; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals3 reading assignments

The Genealogy makes the body the site of historical formation, and the third essay contains the most sustained physiological reading in the published work. The free edition numbers the Preface and each essay separately.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §13

    The lightning and its flash

    Question: If there is no doer behind the deed, what is the subject of a sentence about a person?

    Why it belongs: The section argues that popular morality separates strength from its expression as though there were a neutral substratum free to express strength or not, and compares this to saying that the lightning flashed — doubling one event into an agent and an act. It is the most concentrated published statement of the claim that the self is an addition, and it is where that claim acquires moral consequences. Read it here for the argument about grammar rather than for the master-and-slave material, which the master and slave morality page takes.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §17; Twilight, "The Four Great Errors".

    Online edition: Essay I (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each essay are numbered separately) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§6–10

    The philosopher's asceticism read as working conditions

    Question: Nietzsche says chastity and poverty were the conditions under which the type could work. What would count as evidence for a claim of that kind?

    Why it belongs: The sections treat the philosopher's traditional virtues not as convictions but as the regime under which the philosophical animal has managed to exist — the optimal conditions for its highest activity — and then argue that philosophy had to disguise itself as asceticism in order to be permitted at all. It is the most developed physiological reading in the published work and the best place to test it, because the claim is historical and about a class of people rather than about an individual, which makes it at least in principle checkable.

    Read alongside: Free spirits; Beyond Good and Evil §6.

    Online edition: Essay III §§6–10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§17–19

    Spiritual remedies with physiological effects

    Question: Nietzsche calls the priest's methods innocent in themselves and the interpretation of them false. Can the two be separated as cleanly as that?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche describes the priest's means against depression — reducing the feeling of life to a minimum, mechanical activity, small joys, the organization of the weak — and reads them as interventions in a physiological condition carried out under a spiritual description. This is the theme's most consequential application: an entire religious psychology re-read as a regimen. It is also where the physiological vocabulary is doing the most work with the least support, and the reader should notice how much of the argument would survive if the physiology were dropped and the practices described sociologically instead.

    Read alongside: Suffering and cruelty; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Essay III §18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel; opens nearby — use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols4 reading assignments

The 1888 compressions, and the place where the physiological vocabulary is thickest. Section numbers restart in each chapter and several chapter titles differ in the free edition.

  • Core

    Twilight of the Idols "The Four Great Errors"; "'Reason' in Philosophy" §1

    Inner causes reconsidered, and the senses defended

    Question: What happens to introspective reports if the inner world contains no causes?

    Why it belongs: Where the naturalism becomes a method. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: "The Four Great Errors" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§2–3

    The senses acquitted, reason charged

    Question: Nietzsche says the senses do not lie and that what we make of their evidence introduces the lie. What is the difference between the two, and where does it happen?

    Why it belongs: The sections defend the senses as showing becoming, passing away and change, and place the falsification in reason's treatment of that testimony — in the unity, thinghood, substance and duration it adds. It is the positive counterpart to the attack on the metaphysical tradition, and it is the passage that most directly reverses the tradition's ranking of body and mind. Whether the acquittal survives Nietzsche's own claim that what reaches consciousness is already generalized is a fair objection to put to it.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §354; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: "'Reason' in Philosophy" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Twilight of the Idols "Morality as Anti-Nature" §§1–3

    Attacking a passion at the root, and what that indicates

    Ludovici: "Morality as the Enemy of Nature."

    Question: Nietzsche's alternative is the spiritualization of the passions. What would that consist in, concretely?

    Why it belongs: The chapter argues that moralities which condemn the passions as such — extirpation rather than discipline — are the response of people too weakly constituted for moderation, and proposes the spiritualization of passion in their place. It is the clearest place to see the evaluative use of the physiological vocabulary, since the argument turns entirely on an unargued claim about the constitution of the moralists in question. Read it with Zarathustra I, "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions," which gives the alternative a content.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions"; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: "Morality as the Enemy of Nature" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §§47–49

    Beauty, decline and wholeness given bodily descriptions

    Ludovici: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age."

    Question: The portrait of Goethe is offered as a physiological type. Is anything in it actually physiological?

    Why it belongs: These sections apply the vocabulary at full stretch: aesthetic judgment traced to the species' own conditions of flourishing, decline described as a bodily fact, and Goethe presented as someone who disciplined himself to wholeness and stood in the middle of the universe with a trusting fatalism. It is included as a complication rather than a development because it is the clearest demonstration of the risk this page is organized around. The Goethe portrait is admirable prose and contains no physiology; the word is functioning as a stamp of approval. Read it beside Genealogy III §§6–10, where the same vocabulary is doing checkable work.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§6–10; Amor fati.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes" §§48–49 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use Find for §47) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo2 reading assignments

The doctrine applied to its author. Written in 1888, published in 1908, and the least independent evidence on the page. The free edition titles the later chapters "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" and "Why I Am a Fatality."

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2; "Why I Am So Clever" §1, §10

    The doctrine applied to its author

    Question: Is this the doctrine demonstrated or the doctrine tested?

    Why it belongs: The naturalism in the first person, on illness, recovery, nutrition and place. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §382; Amor fati.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; for "Why I Am So Clever" use the contents) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §6

    A faculty claimed, and no way to check it

    Question: Nietzsche claims a finer sense than anyone has had for signs of ascent and decline. What would a reader do with a claim in that form?

    Why it belongs: The section names the instrument the whole late project depends on — an ability to detect rising and declining life that is presented as a bodily sensitivity rather than as a method. It belongs on this page because it is the doctrine's limiting case. If the diagnoses in Twilight and The Antichrist rest on a faculty that only their author possesses, then the physiological readings cannot be assessed by anyone else, which is a serious problem for a position that presents itself as naturalistic. The section is worth reading precisely because it makes that difficulty impossible to overlook.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "Skirmishes" §§47–49; Wagner and decadence.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" §6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

Four numbered entries, offered as a short appendix rather than as a route. There is late notebook material on the drives and the affects, but on this theme it is thinner than the published work and mostly restates it: Beyond Good and Evil §§12 and 19, Daybreak §119 and The Gay Science §354 are the substantial texts, and the notes add emphasis rather than argument. The four below are included because each one states something the books leave implicit.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. These are experiments, and where they and the published text diverge, the published text governs.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different times.

Preferred edition: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Kaufmann. The numbered links open Anthony M. Ludovici's translation at a nearby printed page. Edition guidance.

Beginning from the body4 assignments · 4 entries

Two entries on the method and two on the difficulty the published work leaves open — what holds a plurality of drives together.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §492

    Beginning from the body

    Question: What does starting from the body offer that starting from consciousness does not?

    Why it belongs: The entry proposes the body and physiology as the methodological starting point, on the grounds that they present a richer and more clearly observable phenomenon than consciousness. It is the most explicit statement of this theme's programme anywhere, and it is unpublished — which is itself informative, since the published books argue for particular claims about the body without ever announcing the method this baldly. The physiological confidence runs well ahead of what Nietzsche could have established.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §354; Perspectivism.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §490

    The subject as a plurality

    Question: If the subject is a society of many subjects, what makes it one society rather than several?

    Why it belongs: The entry tests a plurality of interacting subjects in place of an assumed single one, with an analogy to cells that cooperate and compete. It extends the composition claim to the knower, which is where Beyond Good and Evil §§16–17 were already pointing, and it states plainly what those sections imply. The cell analogy is an exploratory hypothesis and not an established account of agency.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §12; Self-overcoming.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §668

    Commanding does not guarantee execution

    Question: If a command can fail inside a person, what does that tell you about the relation between the parts?

    Why it belongs: The entry observes that willing something is not the same as its happening, and treats the gap as evidence about how the parts of a person stand to each other. It is the counterweight to any reading of the drives as a tidy hierarchy, and it makes the difficulty in Daybreak §560 and The Gay Science §290 explicit: the gardener can want and not get.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; Beyond Good and Evil §19.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §966

    An order of rank within the person

    Question: Strength is described as the organization of opposed drives rather than their absence. What would show that an organization had been achieved?

    Why it belongs: The entry treats a person's strength as consisting in holding conflicting drives together in an order rather than in having few of them. It supplies the notebooks' answer to the question the published work leaves open — what a well-arranged plurality would look like — and it is also where the theme's political vocabulary and its psychological vocabulary meet, since "order of rank" is being used for both. That overlap is worth noticing rather than assuming; the master and slave morality page treats the social sense.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §290; Self-overcoming.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The gardener problem. Read Daybreak §560 and The Gay Science §290, then Beyond Good and Evil §19 and Will to Power §668. Is there an account of self-shaping that does not reintroduce the self it dispensed with?
  • Checkable and uncheckable diagnosis. Compare Genealogy III §§6–10 with Twilight, "Skirmishes" §§47–49. One is a historical claim about a class of people; the other is a portrait. What makes the difference?
  • The self-undermining worry. Follow The Gay Science §354 into Ecce Homo, "Why I Am So Wise" §6. If consciousness falsifies, how was the falsification noticed?
  • Body against substance. Read Zarathustra I, "On the Despisers of the Body" beside Beyond Good and Evil §12 and Genealogy I §13. Does "the body" escape the objection that removed the soul?

A question to carry forward

Pick a judgment you hold strongly. What would have to be true for a physiological explanation of it to be more than a way of changing the subject — and how would you tell?
