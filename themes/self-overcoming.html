Self-Overcoming and Self-Creation

How can we shape ourselves when we only partly understand the drives doing the shaping?

Nietzsche explores how habits, passions, tastes, and commitments acquire a form—and how that form can change. A person may cultivate capacities through discipline and experiment, yet the desire to govern one impulse is itself part of a struggle among impulses. The same questions extend to values: what happens when a moral tradition develops demands that undermine its own foundations?

Self-creation and self-overcoming meet here without becoming interchangeable. Some passages concern giving a character style; others concern leaving an inherited authority, transforming a virtue, or changing one’s relation to the past. Ecce Homo’s subtitle, How One Becomes What One Is, makes the difficulty explicit: becoming can involve detours and a direction recognized only afterwards.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter introduces cultivation, competing drives, value creation, and the limits of conscious planning. Continue through the fuller route to test those ideas, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook readings offer a separate optional investigation.

Use the preferred translation where possible. Each assignment gives a precise section or chapter and links to a named online translation. Essential first encounter

Six sections or chapters, read in order. Begin with an image of giving form to a life, question who directs it, then examine creation and the limits of knowing what one will become.

  1. Core

    The Gay Science §290

    Giving a character form

    Question: What holds the different parts of a character together, and what does that organization cost?

    Why it belongs: The artistic analogy includes adding acquired traits, removing parts of inherited nature, concealing what resists removal, and reinterpreting it. A governing taste creates a relationship among strengths and weaknesses through sustained practice. The section also considers people who find this constraint disabling, complicating any attempt to prescribe one style of self-formation to everyone.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §109; The Gay Science §§304–305.

    Online edition: §290 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Daybreak §109

    Which drive is doing the governing?

    Question: When we control an impulse, what gives the impulse opposing it its authority?

    Why it belongs: After describing six ways of moderating a drive, Nietzsche asks what motivates the attempt. The intellect participates in a struggle between competing impulses, and the desire to control one impulse belongs to that struggle. This makes self-command a problem within the drives themselves and complicates the image of a self directing them from outside.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; The Gay Science §290.

    Online edition: §109 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Three Metamorphoses

    From bearing values to creating them

    Common: The Three Metamorphoses.

    Question: Why is liberation from an inherited command not yet the creation of new values?

    Why it belongs: The camel bears difficult demands, the lion wins the freedom to refuse, and the child represents a new beginning in creation. The movement separates strength in obedience, the power of negation, and an affirmative capacity the lion does not yet possess. Treat these as dramatic figures to interpret, rather than a fixed developmental timetable.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Way of the Creator; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Gift-Giving Virtue, §§1–3.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Self-Overcoming

    When life and its values exceed their own forms

    Common: Self-Surpassing.

    Question: What must be overcome: an individual habit, a form of life, or the values that organize it?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra presents life through command, obedience, risk, and will to power, then turns to the instability of the values people create. What is loved and established can itself become something to overcome. The speech extends the theme beyond personal improvement into the creation and destruction of moral authority.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27; The Gay Science — §335.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    The Gay Science §335

    Examining conscience and creating values

    Question: What must we learn before we can take responsibility for the values by which we live?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche examines the histories and motives behind apparently self-evident moral judgments. Becoming oneself requires questioning their authority and developing individual valuations, while acknowledging how opaque actions remain. The ending connects this creative work to studying the world’s laws and necessities, making knowledge and intellectual honesty part of the task.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §270; Daybreak §103.

    Online edition: §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Clever §9

    Becoming without knowing the destination

    Question: How much of becoming oneself can be planned or known in advance?

    Why it belongs: This retrospective account gives detours and self-misunderstanding a role in development, while an organizing process works beneath consciousness. It directly complicates the earlier images of deliberate cultivation. Keep both possibilities in view: capacities can be shaped through practice without a transparent self already knowing the final form they will take.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (1886); Daybreak — §119.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Clever §9 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Keep these distinctions in view

Self-creation and self-overcoming
Giving a character form is one problem; transforming the commitments that organize a life is another. Self-overcoming also names changes within moral traditions. Ask what is being transformed in each passage.
Cultivation, removal, and extirpation
The artistic work of The Gay Science §290 includes adding, removing, concealing, and reinterpreting traits. Twilight questions a hostility to passions that attacks their very existence. These passages invite comparison about what is changed and what capacity remains.
Command and conscious intention
To describe an act as self-command leaves open who commands, who obeys, and why the command succeeds. Daybreak §109 and Beyond Good and Evil §19 place that question within a multiplicity of drives.
Coherence and the end of conflict
A character can acquire direction while retaining tensions. Some passages value internal opposition; others show virtues competing destructively. Organization has to be examined through what it enables and what it costs.
Freedom from and freedom for
The lion can refuse an inherited command before it can create new values. The creator’s departure from the herd raises a further question about the purpose and authority of that departure.
Formation and self-punishment
Self-directed severity can cultivate capacities, express inherited guilt, or make a life poorer. Genealogy II §§16–18 complicates a clean separation by locating creative form-giving within the history of bad conscience.
Affirmation and a completed achievement
Affirming a life concerns more than approving its successes. The questions about recurrence and amor fati return us to the whole course of becoming, including what a person did not choose or understand in advance.

Untimely Meditations: Schopenhauer as Educator3 reading assignments

This 1874 essay connects liberation from convention with the example of an educator and the cultivation of genius. Its account of a true nature that education releases, and its demand that many people serve rare exceptional individuals, should be examined alongside the later accounts of self-formation. The continuity of the question does not make every answer the same.

  • Earlier formulation

    Untimely Meditations III, Schopenhauer as Educator §1

    Educators as liberators

    Question: Do our deepest attachments reveal a self to be liberated, a possibility to develop, or both?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche turns from borrowed opinions to the people and things one has genuinely loved, treating their pattern as a route toward a higher self. Educators liberate that possibility. The passage also describes an essential nature that education cannot manufacture, so its language of liberation should not be equated without argument with unrestricted self-invention.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §290; Schopenhauer as Educator §3.

    Online edition: Schopenhauer as Educator §1 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Untimely Meditations III, Schopenhauer as Educator §3

    The example and its dangers

    Question: What makes a philosophical life an example worth following, and what threatens that life?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche looks for philosophy embodied in a way of living, then follows Schopenhauer through isolation, despair about truth, and the danger of moral or intellectual hardening. The portrait includes wounds, limitations, and a need for friendship. Its exemplary figure is therefore more vulnerable and dependent on support than an uncomplicated image of solitary heroism suggests.

    Read alongside: Schopenhauer as Educator §1; Human, All Too Human I, Preface §§1–7.

    Online edition: Schopenhauer as Educator §3 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Untimely Meditations III, Schopenhauer as Educator §6

    Cultivation for whose sake?

    Question: What follows if culture aims at producing rare exceptional people rather than developing everyone equally?

    Why it belongs: The section connects self-dissatisfaction with action to create conditions for genius, criticizing education subordinated to commerce, the state, fashionable manners, and scholarship. Its alternative explicitly asks many people to find their purpose in serving exceptional individuals. This exposes the social demands and unequal aims of cultivation, rather than presenting self-development as an identical project for everyone.

    Read alongside: Schopenhauer as Educator §1; Rank and social hierarchy.

    Online edition: Schopenhauer as Educator §6 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

Human, All Too Human3 reading assignments

The 1878 first volume explores intellectual emancipation and the revisability of convictions. Its 1886 preface, added eight years later, reconstructs that experience through separation, recovery, and experiments with perspective. Read this retrospective beside the original aphorisms: the account of a developing task and the image of wandering without a final destination create a productive tension.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (1886)

    Emancipation, recovery, and a task discovered afterwards

    Question: How much of a life’s direction becomes intelligible only in retrospect?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reconstructs the free spirit’s development through invented companions, a dangerous break with inherited loyalties, and gradual recovery. Mastery includes learning to use competing perspectives and one’s own virtues. The later account finds a task operating before it was consciously understood; it also connects that task to questions of rank.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §382; Human, All Too Human I §§637–638.

    Online edition: Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §272

    Keeping the capacity to develop

    Question: What enables someone to continue changing after their early education is over?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche contrasts intellectual rigidity with the unusual flexibility he attributes to Goethe. The aphorism sketches an individual’s passage through religious, metaphysical, artistic, and scientific outlooks. Its historical scheme is ambitious and contestable; its relevance here is the possibility that a person can continue revising the forms through which they understand themselves.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §307.

    Online edition: §272 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§637–638

    Revisable convictions and the wanderer

    Question: Must becoming oneself arrive at a final, settled identity?

    Why it belongs: Section 637 describes the danger of opinions congealing into convictions and the need to keep judgment mobile. Section 638 develops the wanderer who enjoys change while enduring loneliness and exhaustion. Its rejection of a final destination complicates any reading of self-creation as the completion of an already specified personal design.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human I, Preface §§1–7; The Gay Science §307.

    Online edition: §637 · §638 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak6 reading assignments

Daybreak gives self-cultivation both possibilities and limits. Its garden images invite us to shape our drives, while its psychology questions how well we know those drives and who is directing the work. These tensions belong together: cultivation takes place within an organism whose motives and development remain partly obscure.

  • Development

    Daybreak §103

    Learning to feel differently

    Question: What changes when we question a moral judgment’s assumptions while retaining some of the actions it recommended?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes doubting people’s professed moral motives from challenging the truth of moral judgments themselves. Rejecting their assumptions need not reverse every practical recommendation. The section ends by connecting renewed learning with a possible transformation of feeling, making the criticism of morality part of a slower change in the person.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335.

    Online edition: §103 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Daybreak §109

    Which drive is doing the governing?

    Question: When we control an impulse, what gives the impulse opposing it its authority?

    Why it belongs: After describing six ways of moderating a drive, Nietzsche asks what motivates the attempt. The intellect participates in a struggle between competing impulses, and the desire to control one impulse belongs to that struggle. This makes self-command a problem within the drives themselves and complicates the image of a self directing them from outside.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; The Gay Science §290.

    Online edition: §109 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Daybreak §117

    The limits of our perceptual world

    Question: How do the limits of our senses constrain what we can know about ourselves and the world?

    Why it belongs: The prison in this aphorism is the horizon established by an organism’s senses. Nietzsche questions our assumption that familiar measures of distance, size, and experience reveal things independently of that horizon. Its connection to self-overcoming is indirect but useful: even the person seeking transformation observes from within embodied limits.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §119.

    Online edition: §117 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §119

    How experiences nourish and express our drives

    Question: How far do our drives determine the meaning we give an experience?

    Why it belongs: Our knowledge of the drives is incomplete, and everyday events nourish them unevenly. Nietzsche then examines how an active drive interprets an event to suit its needs. The section complicates deliberate self-cultivation: both the material being cultivated and the story we tell about its development can escape our awareness.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §109; Daybreak §560; The Gay Science §335.

    Online edition: §119 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §174

    Cultivation with a hospitable gate

    Question: Could developing yourself benefit other people in ways that constant intervention does not?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche questions a morality organized around collective security and the removal of every difficulty. He then considers whether becoming someone others can enjoy might serve them better than perpetual, potentially intrusive assistance. The closing garden image joins protection and hospitality, giving self-cultivation a possible social value without making it continuous self-sacrifice.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §560; The Gay Science §§304–305.

    Online edition: §174 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §560

    Cultivating the garden of drives

    Question: What would it mean to cultivate a drive instead of treating your character as fixed?

    Why it belongs: The garden metaphor presents several possibilities: directing growth, intervening sparingly, or allowing impulses to contend with one another. Nietzsche challenges the assumption that character is finished and unchangeable. Read this permission to experiment alongside his accounts of competing drives and incomplete self-knowledge; the gardener’s freedom raises questions those accounts help us formulate.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §109; Daybreak §119.

    Online edition: §560 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science9 reading assignments

The first four books appeared in 1882; Book V, including §382, was added in 1887. These selections connect artistic formation with the criticism of conscience, changing convictions, and experiments in living. They also question excessive self-control, so the demand to shape a life remains open to correction from within the book itself.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §270

    The demand to become oneself

    Question: What could conscience demand if the person you are is still becoming?

    Why it belongs: This brief exchange gives conscience the task of directing a person toward becoming themselves. Its compression leaves the meaning of that task unresolved. Read it with the longer analysis in §335, which asks how conscience acquired its authority and connects becoming oneself with the creation and examination of values.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §335.

    Online edition: §270 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    The Gay Science §290

    Giving a character form

    Question: What holds the different parts of a character together, and what does that organization cost?

    Why it belongs: The artistic analogy includes adding acquired traits, removing parts of inherited nature, concealing what resists removal, and reinterpreting it. A governing taste creates a relationship among strengths and weaknesses through sustained practice. The section also considers people who find this constraint disabling, complicating any attempt to prescribe one style of self-formation to everyone.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §109; The Gay Science §§304–305.

    Online edition: §290 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §299

    Learning the artist’s way of seeing

    Question: How can a change of perspective alter the way an ordinary life is experienced?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche examines artistic techniques that make things attractive through distance, framing, partial concealment, and altered appearance. He asks readers to extend such practices into ordinary life. This develops the aesthetic side of self-creation while raising a question about how cultivated appearance relates to honesty and knowledge.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §290; The Gay Science §335.

    Online edition: §299 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    The Gay Science §301

    The creative work concealed in contemplation

    Question: How much of the world we experience depends on valuations that people have created?

    Why it belongs: The contemplative person mistakenly understands themselves as merely observing life. Nietzsche attributes creative power to the thinking and feeling that supply human valuations and perspectives. This extends the theme from arranging an individual character to participating in the creation of a meaningful world, while retaining the passage’s distinctions between different kinds of people.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §299; The Gay Science §335.

    Online edition: §301 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Gay Science §§304–305

    Purposeful activity and the danger of constant self-control

    Question: When does discipline support a life, and when does vigilance against oneself impoverish it?

    Why it belongs: Section 304 favors absorbing activity through which unsuitable attachments fall away. Section 305 examines the person who continually guards against their own impulses and thereby loses openness to experience and learning. Together they distinguish productive discipline from an exhausting fixation on restraint, giving the theme an essential internal criticism.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §290; Daybreak §109.

    Online edition: §304 · §305 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §307

    A changed life sheds its convictions

    Question: Does criticism change the person, or does a changed person become capable of a new criticism?

    Why it belongs: An opinion once needed by a person may become dispensable as their life changes. Nietzsche asks readers to see active forces at work beneath what feels like a purely intellectual victory. Revising a belief can therefore express a transformation already occurring, including tendencies that the person has not yet clearly understood.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human I §§637–638; Daybreak §119.

    Online edition: §307 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    The Gay Science §335

    Examining conscience and creating values

    Question: What must we learn before we can take responsibility for the values by which we live?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche examines the histories and motives behind apparently self-evident moral judgments. Becoming oneself requires questioning their authority and developing individual valuations, while acknowledging how opaque actions remain. The ending connects this creative work to studying the world’s laws and necessities, making knowledge and intellectual honesty part of the task.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §270; Daybreak §103.

    Online edition: §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    The Gay Science §341

    The demand to affirm this life again

    Question: What relationship to yourself and your life would make its exact repetition welcome?

    Why it belongs: The imagined announcement of eternal recurrence can provoke rejection or affirmation, and the thought itself could transform the hearer. For this theme, its force lies in testing one’s relationship to an entire life, including its smallest and most difficult events. It asks more than whether a particular achievement feels worthwhile.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §382.

    Online edition: §341 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §382 (Book V, 1887)

    A health repeatedly risked and regained

    Question: What capacities would allow someone to experiment with unfamiliar ways of valuing and living?

    Why it belongs: The explorers of unfamiliar values need a health they repeatedly acquire, sacrifice, and recover. Nietzsche connects this capacity with curiosity, danger, play, and an ideal that unsettles inherited standards of seriousness. Self-overcoming here involves renewed experiments and changed possibilities, without a promise that development brings permanent ease or immunity to suffering.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human I, Preface §§1–7; The Gay Science §§304–305.

    Online edition: §382 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra9 reading assignments

These dramatic speeches connect self-transformation with embodiment, the creation of values, generosity, and the limits of willing. Read Zarathustra’s claims together with his questions and interruptions; the drama does not supply a universal sequence of exercises or a finished portrait of the perfected self.

  • Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Three Metamorphoses

    From bearing values to creating them

    Common: The Three Metamorphoses.

    Question: Why is liberation from an inherited command not yet the creation of new values?

    Why it belongs: The camel bears difficult demands, the lion wins the freedom to refuse, and the child represents a new beginning in creation. The movement separates strength in obedience, the power of negation, and an affirmative capacity the lion does not yet possess. Treat these as dramatic figures to interpret, rather than a fixed developmental timetable.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Way of the Creator; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Gift-Giving Virtue, §§1–3.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Despisers of the Body

    The body and the self that acts

    Common: The Despisers of the Body.

    Question: What happens to self-command when the conscious ego is itself an instrument?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra describes the body through images of plurality, conflict, coordination, and rule, placing the conscious ego within a larger activity. This challenges the picture of a detached mind shaping passive bodily material. His accusation that contempt for the body expresses an inability to create beyond oneself is part of the argument to examine.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §§12, 19; Daybreak — §109.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions

    Passions become virtues—and virtues conflict

    Common: Joys and Passions.

    Question: Does transforming a passion into a virtue end the conflict within a person?

    Why it belongs: Passions acquire a different significance when organized around an aim, but the resulting virtues can compete for command and threaten their bearer. This speech complicates the promise of harmonious integration: having more virtues does not automatically make a life easier or stronger, and form-giving can carry destructive costs.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3; On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§16–18.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Way of the Creator

    Freedom for a task and the dangers of solitude

    Common: The Way of the Creating One.

    Question: What distinguishes creating a purpose from merely escaping an obligation?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra asks what freedom is for and whether the solitary person can establish values and bear responsibility for them. He also describes loneliness, self-doubt, and becoming one’s own judge and avenger. Separation from the herd therefore brings risks; it is neither proof of creative authority nor a sufficient achievement in itself.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations — III, Schopenhauer as Educator §1; On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§1–3.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Gift-Giving Virtue, §§1–3

    Cultivation, generosity, and leaving the teacher

    Also titled On the Bestowing Virtue; Common: The Bestowing Virtue.

    Question: What can a cultivated self give, and why must disciples eventually leave their teacher?

    Why it belongs: The speech links gathering inner riches with giving, earthly value creation, and the possibility of a future humanity. Its final section asks disciples to find themselves through independence from Zarathustra. This joins self-cultivation to relationships and generosity, while preserving the text’s distinctions of rank and its polemical contrast between forms of selfishness.

    Read alongside: Daybreak — §174; Ecce Homo — Preface §§1–4.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Self-Overcoming

    When life and its values exceed their own forms

    Common: Self-Surpassing.

    Question: What must be overcome: an individual habit, a form of life, or the values that organize it?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra presents life through command, obedience, risk, and will to power, then turns to the instability of the values people create. What is loved and established can itself become something to overcome. The speech extends the theme beyond personal improvement into the creation and destruction of moral authority.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27; The Gay Science — §335.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Those Who Are Sublime

    When the hero must learn grace

    Also titled On the Sublime Ones; Common: The Sublime Ones.

    Question: Could overcoming oneself require relinquishing strenuous heroism?

    Why it belongs: The solemn conqueror has mastered difficult things but still lacks laughter, beauty, and repose. Zarathustra asks for a transformation of the heroic will into a more gracious expression of power. This is a necessary counterweight to reading self-overcoming as ever greater effort, severity, or visible struggle.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §§304–305; Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On Redemption

    The will meets a past it cannot undo

    Common: Redemption.

    Question: How can a creative will relate to what has already happened without turning to revenge?

    Why it belongs: The will’s inability to change the past becomes an obstacle to its claim to liberate. Zarathustra connects this frustration with revenge and asks whether creation can transform the interpretation of what has been. His speech breaks off before explaining how; the interruption should remain part of the reading rather than disappear into a completed formula.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo — Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§6, 8; Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Clever §10.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, On the Spirit of Gravity, §§1–2

    Learning one’s taste and shedding inherited burdens

    Common: The Spirit of Gravity.

    Question: How can self-love involve learning and discrimination rather than accepting everything about oneself?

    Why it belongs: Inherited judgments make life heavy, but Zarathustra also describes the difficulty of discovering what is one’s own. Learning self-love involves practice, taste, appearances, and the ability to affirm and refuse. His refusal to offer a single path keeps the guide from treating self-overcoming as an identical program for every reader.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §290; Human, All Too Human — Volume I, §§637–638.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil6 reading assignments

These readings ask what self-command involves when the self comprises many competing drives. They connect discipline and creative authority while exposing the risks of treating any achieved virtue as a final identity.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §§12, 19

    A self of many drives

    The online links open Part I; use Find for the numbered sections.

    Question: If willing involves both commanding and obeying, who is doing the overcoming?

    Why it belongs: The critique of an indivisible soul and the analysis of willing unsettle the picture of one transparent agent directing its impulses. They give self-overcoming a harder problem: how does an organization of drives acquire direction, and how much of its success does consciousness mistakenly claim as its own?

    Read alongside: Daybreak — §109; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Despisers of the Body.

    Online edition: §12 · §19 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §188

    Freedom through sustained constraint

    The online link opens Part V; use Find for 188.

    Question: How can constraint become a condition of freedom?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects artistic freedom with prolonged obedience and exacting constraints. This helps distinguish a developed capacity from simply following an impulse. Keep the historical cost in view: the same disciplinary history that cultivates abilities also wastes and destroys them. Freedom does not mean that every formative constraint has disappeared.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §290; On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§1–3.

    Online edition: §188 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §212

    Outgrowing the virtues of an age

    The online link opens Part VI; use Find for 212.

    Question: When must a cultivated virtue itself become something to overcome?

    Why it belongs: The philosopher challenges the qualities an age most admires, asking what further human possibilities those qualities now obstruct. This extends self-overcoming beyond private habits to inherited standards of greatness. Nietzsche's preferred answer includes rank, solitude, and expanded responsibility; it should not be reduced to a general celebration of unconventionality.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming.

    Online edition: §212 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §225

    Creator and material

    The online link opens Part VII; use Find for 225.

    Question: What happens when the artist and the material being shaped are the same person?

    Why it belongs: The image of a person as both creator and creature makes the costs of formation difficult to evade. It is a demanding counterpoint to giving style to character: Nietzsche assigns suffering a formative role and challenges compassion that protects every existing condition. The passage opens that conflict rather than settling it.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§16–18; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3.

    Online edition: §225 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §227

    Honesty must question itself

    The online link opens Part VII; use Find for 227.

    Question: Can devotion to honesty become another obstacle to change?

    Why it belongs: Honesty is cultivated with the help of impulses that conventional morality distrusts, yet Nietzsche also warns against making it an ornament, a limitation, or a new sanctity. The reading tests whether self-overcoming can remain critical of its own guiding virtues, including the virtue through which it examines itself.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27.

    Online edition: §227 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §284

    Using affects without becoming their captive

    The online link opens Part IX; use Find for 284.

    Question: What would it mean to remain master even of one's virtues?

    Why it belongs: This portrait concerns the use of affects, selective reserve, and command over valued qualities. It belongs beside the accounts of cultivated character, while also making their aristocratic distance visible. Its central relevance is a changing relation to one's impulses and virtues, not merely discretion about what one has discovered.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Those Who Are Sublime.

    Online edition: §284 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals4 reading assignments

The Genealogy investigates the history and costs of self-command. It also shifts the scale: a moral tradition can undermine itself through the very commitments it has cultivated.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II §§1–3

    Becoming able to promise

    Question: How does a person acquire the capacity to answer for a future action?

    Why it belongs: Active forgetting, a durable memory of intention, and the sovereign individual belong to an account of acquired agency. Read the proud image of independence together with the violence of its prehistory. The connection to becoming oneself is interpretive; these sections do not simply present a personal program for autonomous living.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §188; On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§16–18.

    Online edition: Essay II §§1–3 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II §§16–18

    The troubling origins of inward formation

    Question: Can the forces that injure a person also become forces of creation?

    Why it belongs: These sections trace the inward redirection of instincts under social constraint. Continue through §18: the same force that produces self-directed cruelty also shapes ideals and new beauty. Bad conscience therefore cannot be treated simply as an obstacle removed before healthy creation begins; its relation to formation remains troublingly productive.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §225; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3.

    Online edition: Essay II §§16–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §10

    Philosophy's ascetic disguise

    Question: How can a practice emerge through conditions it later needs to challenge?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche proposes that philosophy became possible under the protection of ascetic roles and through severe work upon its own inherited valuations. He closes by asking whether it has really escaped that disguise. This complicates a simple opposition between liberating self-overcoming and an entirely external tradition of self-denial.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27; Untimely Meditations — III, Schopenhauer as Educator §3.

    Online edition: Essay III §10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §27

    A morality turns against its own foundations

    Samuel renders the key term for self-overcoming here as self-mastery.

    Question: What happens when truthfulness questions the value of truth itself?

    Why it belongs: Christian truthfulness first undermines inherited religious belief and then calls its own authority into question. Nietzsche connects this movement explicitly with self-overcoming. The passage changes the scale of the theme: inherited values can transform themselves through their own demands, even where no individual has designed the outcome in advance.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming; Beyond Good and Evil — §227.

    Online edition: Essay III §27 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols3 reading assignments

The late passages distinguish transforming passions from attacking their existence. Goethe supplies a portrait of achieved organization; the account of Roman style offers a narrower example of artistic formation.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3

    Transforming passions and retaining opposition

    Ludovici titles this chapter Morality as the Enemy of Nature.

    Question: How does working with an impulse differ from trying to eradicate it?

    Why it belongs: The contrast between spiritualizing passions and extirpating them supplies a direct test for competing forms of self-discipline. Yet §3 also values internal opposition and rejects an easy ideal of settled peace. Giving a life form need not mean eliminating its tensions or reconciling every drive with every other.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions; The Gay Science — §§304–305.

    Online edition: Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49

    Goethe and disciplined wholeness

    Ludovici titles this chapter Skirmishes in a War with the Age.

    Question: What kind of unity can preserve a person's range of capacities?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche's Goethe organizes many capacities without treating sensuality as an enemy. The portrait joins self-discipline to breadth, strength, and affirmation, making a useful comparison with the cultivated style of The Gay Science. Read it as Nietzsche's interpretive image of Goethe, rather than evidence that every person should reproduce this type.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §290; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Those Who Are Sublime.

    Online edition: Skirmishes §49 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Twilight of the Idols What I Owe to the Ancients §1

    Learning form from Sallust and Horace

    Ludovici titles this chapter Things I Owe to the Ancients.

    Question: How might apprenticeship in writing illuminate the formation of character?

    Why it belongs: The account of learning compression and organization from Roman writers offers a concrete artistic parallel to cultivated character. Its relevance is analogical: it concerns the formation of Nietzsche's prose, rather than a direct theory of the self. It works best after the central readings on discipline and style.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §299; Beyond Good and Evil — §188.

    Online edition: What I Owe to the Ancients §1 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo6 reading assignments

Nietzsche's retrospective self-portrait puts conscious effort, instinctive organization, illness, and affirmation into an unsettled relation. Read its claims as deliberate self-presentation, and ask where they challenge the expectations created by earlier readings.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo Preface §§1–4

    A self-portrait that resists discipleship

    Question: How can a writer present himself as exemplary while asking readers to become independent?

    Why it belongs: The preface stages Nietzsche's identity, audience, and task, ending with Zarathustra's warning against remaining merely a disciple. It frames becoming oneself as a problem for readers as well as the author. Treat the rhetoric of philosophical height and distinction as something to examine, rather than a measure already proved.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Gift-Giving Virtue, §§1–3; Untimely Meditations — III, Schopenhauer as Educator §1.

    Online edition: Preface §§1–4 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Wise §§1–2

    Changing perspectives through illness and recovery

    Question: How does Nietzsche turn changes in his condition into changes in evaluation?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents illness and recovery as occasions for reversing perspectives and selecting what sustains him. The account complicates a simple picture of uninterrupted strength, while also making strong claims about his exceptional constitution. Its interest lies in his interpretation of experience, not a promise that suffering necessarily produces growth.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §382 (Book V, 1887); Human, All Too Human — Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (1886).

    Online edition: Why I Am So Wise §§1–2 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Wise §6

    Restraint, resentment, and changing conditions

    Ludovici uses resentment for ressentiment in this passage.

    Question: Can the same refusal to react express different kinds of strength or need?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche gives different reasons for avoiding ressentiment during exhaustion and after recovery. At one point restraint preserves limited resources; at another, resentment no longer fits his sense of abundance. The passage complicates the assumption that self-overcoming must always look like increased effort, active resistance, or an immediate change of circumstances.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Redemption; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Those Who Are Sublime.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Wise §6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Clever §9

    Becoming without knowing the destination

    Question: How much of becoming oneself can be planned or known in advance?

    Why it belongs: This retrospective account gives detours and self-misunderstanding a role in development, while an organizing process works beneath consciousness. It directly complicates the earlier images of deliberate cultivation. Keep both possibilities in view: capacities can be shaped through practice without a transparent self already knowing the final form they will take.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (1886); Daybreak — §119.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Clever §9 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Clever §10

    Everyday conditions, play, and amor fati

    Question: How does changing a life relate to affirming its necessity?

    Why it belongs: The chapter closes by emphasizing ordinary conditions of living, presenting great work as play, and formulating amor fati. These moves resist a purely heroic account of self-transformation. Read them beside the claims about discipline: affirmation and effort are related problems here, rather than two interchangeable instructions for living.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §341; Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Clever §9.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Clever §10 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Ecce Homo Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§6, 8

    Affirmation, creative destruction, and the past

    Question: How can rejecting inherited forms belong to an affirmation of existence?

    Why it belongs: These reflections connect Zarathustra's opposing capacities, affirmation of existence, redemption of the past, and creative hardness. They revisit the figure and several speeches rather than specifically explaining On Self-Overcoming. They provide a demanding late comparison with the theme's earlier readings, especially where creation requires both attachment and destruction.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Redemption; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Self-Overcoming.

    Online edition: Thus Spoke Zarathustra §6 · Thus Spoke Zarathustra §8 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

Nine numbered entries in seven assignments develop four questions raised by the published works. Begin with the shorter starting route below, or choose the question you want to pursue.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche’s notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. These experiments can sharpen distinctions without supplying the final explanation of the published works.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions; the compilation also brings together notes from different dates.

Working with the passions2 assignments · 4 entries

What changes when overcoming a passion means finding a use for it rather than making it disappear?

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §383, §384, §385

    Transformation instead of eradication

    Question: Does moral restraint organize our strongest capacities, or express a fear of them?

    Why it belongs: These notebook remarks distinguish destroying passions from learning to employ them, and moral intolerance from the capacity to organize dangerous forces. They clarify the criticism of an ideal that treats diminished desire as improvement. Section 384 also considers the organization of passions at the level of communities and peoples, extending the question beyond personal self-command.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3; The Gay Science — §290.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §778

    Development or merely changing moods?

    Question: What distinguishes a developing character from a succession of disconnected states?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes a dominant passion coordinating activity, destructive internal conflict, and disconnected moods that change without developing. This gives the idea of composition more precise alternatives to consider. His diagnoses of health and illness remain evaluative and speculative; they should not be mistaken for medical criteria or a universal model of character.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §290; Daybreak — §109.

Who does the overcoming?2 assignments · 2 entries

Examine the self that is supposed to command, and the conditions under which its commands can become effective.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §490

    The subject as a plurality

    Question: Must self-overcoming have a single, unified self directing it?

    Why it belongs: This note tests a plurality of interacting subjects in place of an assumed single subject. It challenges the picture of an independent self standing outside its drives and arranging them. The analogy with cooperating and competing cells is an exploratory hypothesis, not an established biological explanation of agency.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §§12, 19; Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Despisers of the Body.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §668

    Commanding does not guarantee execution

    Question: What makes a command effective when we try to change ourselves?

    Why it belongs: Willing is distinguished from wishing through the experience of command, but issuing a command does not establish that it will be carried out. The note challenges the assumption that self-creation follows directly from a sovereign will. It asks us to explain effective agency rather than assuming that conscious intention controls the whole person.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil — §§12, 19; Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Clever §9.

Restraint and strength2 assignments · 2 entries

Distinguish disciplined strength from diminished capacity, and ask how much internal difference a character can sustain.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §870

    The pleasure of measure

    Question: Can restraint express enjoyment of one's powers rather than fear or exhaustion?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes moderation imposed by weakness from restraint exercised over powerful capacities, emphasizing pleasure in measure. His polemic against inherited moral judgments also asks why creative people become ashamed of their strengths. The passage gives discipline an aesthetic dimension while complicating any assumption that either indulgence or visible severity proves strength.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On Those Who Are Sublime; Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §966

    Organizing a multiplicity of drives

    Question: Does a coherent character require less conflict, or a greater capacity to organize it?

    Why it belongs: The note presents moralities as limited hierarchies among drives and imagines exceptional strength as the capacity to sustain and organize powerful opposing impulses. Coherence need not mean emotional uniformity or a simplified personality. Read alongside section 778, it makes the relation between internal conflict, development, and achieved form a problem to examine.

    Read alongside: Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions; Twilight of the Idols — Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §49.

Testing the result1 assignment · 1 entry

An achieved goal or persuasive account of oneself does not settle whether self-overcoming has occurred.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1009

    Abundance, reaction, and resistance

    Question: Does an apparent achievement express organized strength, reactive need, or avoidance?

    Why it belongs: These questions distinguish abundance from need, initiative from reaction, and compositional power from impoverishment, while asking whether a person seeks resistance or evades it. They make a useful concluding interrogation of apparent achievement. The entry is neither a neutral self-assessment checklist nor an authoritative diagnosis; its closing claims also include a restrictive stereotype about women.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science — §335; Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Clever §9.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • Affirming what one could not choose. Read The Gay Science §341, On Redemption, and Ecce Homo, Why I Am So Clever §10. How can a life be affirmed when its past cannot be revised?

A question to carry forward

What would count as evidence that someone had transformed their way of valuing—and how would we distinguish that from adopting a more flattering story about themselves?
