Critique of Metaphysics and "the True World"

A "true world" is the world a philosophy promises behind, above, or after this one. Nietzsche's charge is not only that there is no such world, but that the machinery used to build one — opposite values, substances, subjects, causes — is still running after the world it was built for has been given up.

The target is specific, and keeping it specific is what makes the theme tractable. Nietzsche attacks a family of constructions: the division of reality into a real world and an apparent one; the assumption that something of high value cannot have grown out of something of low value; the thing that stands behind and underneath its properties; the doer behind the deed; the cause that precedes and explains an effect; the unity called "the I." Each of these is treated as a piece of equipment rather than a discovery, and the question asked of each is what produced it and what it is for.

The objection is also evaluative, and Nietzsche does not hide it. To call this world merely apparent is already to have passed sentence on it, and he thinks the sentence came first and the metaphysics afterwards. That is why the argument in Twilight of the Idols runs through a history rather than a refutation: six short paragraphs take the true world from Plato through Christianity to Kant and then to its abolition, and the last stage says that when the true world goes, the apparent world goes with it, because "apparent" was only ever the other half of a pair.

Two things this page tries not to do. It does not treat the critique as finished once God has been given up — The Gay Science §344 is here precisely because Nietzsche thinks a metaphysical commitment survives in the sciences that believe they have left metaphysics behind. And it does not slide into being a page about perspectivism. The two themes meet, but the questions are different, and the section below marks the border.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves from the measured early treatment of the thing in itself, through the two late compressions in Twilight, to the book-length attack on the prejudices of philosophers and the residue that survives all of it. Continue through the fuller route to test each construction separately, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook readings are a separate optional investigation and are clearly marked as unpublished material.

Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order. The sequence begins with the cautious middle-period treatment so that the late polemics can be measured against it, then takes the two compressed chapters of Twilight, then the long published attack on the prejudices of philosophers, then the errors about cause and effect, and ends with the passage that says the job is not done. Chronology matters here: 1878 and 1888 are ten years apart and the position hardens across them.

  1. Earlier formulation

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §16

    The thing in itself set aside rather than destroyed

    Zimmern: Phenomenon and Thing-in-itself.

    Question: Nietzsche does not here declare the thing in itself impossible. What does he say about it instead, and what is the difference?

    Why it belongs: The section treats the world as it appears to us as the product of a long history of erring and growing organisms, and concludes that a thing in itself, if there were one, would be a matter of complete indifference — knowledge of it would be the most useless of all knowledge. That is a weaker claim than the late dismissal of the notion as absurd, and starting here keeps the development visible. It also introduces the move the whole theme depends on: not refuting a metaphysical entity but asking what work the concept was doing.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Will to Power §556.

    Online edition: §16 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Twilight of the Idols "'Reason' in Philosophy"

    The prejudice for being, and the defence of the senses

    Ludovici: "'Reason' in Philosophy." Section numbers restart in each chapter.

    Question: Nietzsche says the senses do not lie and that reason is what falsifies their evidence. What has to be true about concepts for that inversion to work?

    Why it belongs: The chapter is the most compressed statement of the positive half of the critique. Philosophers are charged with an "Egypticism" that hates becoming and mummifies its objects; the senses are defended as showing becoming and passing away; the highest concepts are said to have been put first when they came last, so that the emptiest concept is placed at the beginning as cause. It closes by setting out in numbered form the theses about the true and the apparent world that the next chapter will narrate historically. Note what is being claimed and what is not: this is an account of how a certain kind of philosophy proceeds, not a theory of perception.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: "'Reason' in Philosophy" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Twilight of the Idols "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"

    Six stages, and what is left at the end of them

    The chapter is subtitled "The History of an Error." The free edition's contents omit it, although the text follows "'Reason' in Philosophy."

    Question: The fifth stage removes the true world and the sixth removes the apparent one. Why does the second removal follow from the first?

    Why it belongs: One page, six numbered stages, from the true world attainable by the wise, through the promise deferred to the beyond, to the Kantian version that is unattainable but still obliging, to the one nobody has any use for, to its abolition — and then to the point the chapter exists for: with the true world we have also abolished the apparent one. Read slowly. The argument is that "appearance" was a comparative term, so that removing its opposite leaves no contrast to make. The closing line — noon, the shortest shadow, incipit Zarathustra — is the place where the critique hands over to whatever is supposed to follow it, and the page is honest that what follows is announced rather than argued.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §9; Death of God.

    Online edition: "How the True World Finally Became a Fable" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil Part I, §§1–23

    The prejudices of philosophers, taken one at a time

    Part I: "On the Prejudices of Philosophers." Section numbers run continuously across the book's nine parts.

    Question: §2 attacks the faith in opposite values. Which of the later sections in this part are applications of that single objection, and which are separate arguments?

    Why it belongs: The longest sustained published treatment. §1 turns the will to truth into a question; §2 identifies the metaphysicians' faith in opposite values and proposes instead that valued things may have grown out of their apparent opposites; §12 proposes getting rid of "soul atomism" while keeping room for refined hypotheses about the soul; §§16–17 attack the immediate certainty of the "I think" and argue that a thought comes when it wishes rather than when "I" will it; §19 breaks willing into a complex of sensations, commanding and obeying; §21 treats cause and effect as conventional fictions useful for description. Read the part as a whole and slowly. Note that §22 concedes, in an aside, that Nietzsche's own counter-reading is also only interpretation — the concession belongs to the argument rather than escaping it.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §54; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    Twilight of the Idols "The Four Great Errors"

    Cause, effect, and the invention of a doer

    Ludovici: "The Four Great Errors."

    Question: Which of the four errors is a mistake about the world, and which is a mistake about ourselves? Does Nietzsche keep them apart?

    Why it belongs: This is the chapter where the four errors actually are — confusing cause and consequence, false causality, imaginary causes, and the error of free will — and where the critique of substances and causes does most of its work. The second error is the important one for this theme: Nietzsche argues that the inner world of will, motive and ego, from which we took our concept of cause, contains no causes at all, and that having projected that model outward we then find agents everywhere. The fourth error extends the point to responsibility, and the chapter ends by saying that removing the doer is what restores innocence to becoming. The historical psychology in the third error is assertion rather than demonstration, and should be read as such.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §13; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: "The Four Great Errors" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  6. Complication · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §344

    The commitment that survives the demolition

    Kaufmann: How we, too, are still pious. Common: To what Extent even We are still Pious.

    Question: If the unconditional will to truth is itself a metaphysical faith, what happens to the arguments that used it to dismantle metaphysics?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that science rests on a conviction that truth is needed above all else, that this unconditional preference cannot be established by science, and that its origin is the same faith in a divine order that the sciences believe they have escaped. The section is the reason this theme does not end with Twilight: the metaphysical residue is not a leftover doctrine but a valuation, and it is carried by exactly the people who think they are free of it. It also puts the whole critique under pressure, since the demolition was itself carried out in the name of truthfulness. Belongs to Book V, added in 1887.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §27; Nihilism.

    Online edition: §344 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Five investigations to take after the first encounter. Each construction Nietzsche attacks gets its own group, because the arguments against them are not the same argument repeated. Assignments open their full explanations and reading links in the work survey below.

1. The two-world structure itself

Start with the target. Follow the claim that a higher reality is always accompanied by a verdict on this one, and ask whether the verdict could be separated from the metaphysics.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"
    Six stages, ending with the apparent world abolished too.
  2. Human, All Too Human — §9
    A metaphysical world granted as possible and then declared useless.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Afterworldly"
    The other world diagnosed as a product of suffering and weariness.
  4. The Antichrist — §15
    A world of purely imaginary causes and effects, described as a whole system.

2. Opposite values

The faith that something valuable cannot come from its opposite is the assumption Nietzsche says underlies the rest. Test whether the alternative he proposes is an argument or a preference.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — Part I, §§1–23
    §2 on the metaphysicians' faith in opposite values; §1 on the will to truth as a question.
  2. On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense
    The earliest version: truth and lie treated as products of a social requirement.
  3. Human, All Too Human — §1
    The programme: a chemistry of concepts, and the refusal of miraculous origins.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §13
    Strength and its expression separated by grammar alone.

3. Substances, subjects, and the grammar that carries them

If the doer is added to the deed, the addition needs an explanation. These readings give one, and the explanation is linguistic before it is metaphysical.

  1. Human, All Too Human — §11
    Language taken for a science it never was, and the reification of words.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §§16–17, §19
    Immediate certainty examined; a thought that comes when it wishes; willing broken into parts.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §54
    Modern philosophy described as a long attack on the old concept of the soul.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Despisers of the Body"
    The soul redescribed as a word for something about the body.

4. Causes, and the innocence of becoming

The attack on causality is the part of the theme with the most at stake for moral responsibility. Follow it into the places where Nietzsche draws that consequence.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — "The Four Great Errors"
    Cause and consequence confused; imaginary causes; the error of free will.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §13
    The lightning and its flash: one event, two words.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §21
    Cause and effect as conventional fictions for describing, not explaining.
  4. The Gay Science — §109
    The warning against putting law, purpose or machinery back into the whole.

5. What survives the critique

The hardest group. If the will to truth is the last metaphysical commitment, the critique has to say something about its own footing.

  1. The Gay Science — §344
    Science resting on a faith it cannot establish.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §27
    Christian truthfulness turning against Christian morality, and what that pattern implies next.
  3. The Antichrist — §§10–12
    German philosophy charged with keeping theology's structure after dropping its content.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §211
    The philosopher redescribed as a legislator of values rather than a discoverer of facts.

Keep these distinctions in view

Denying a world and refusing a contrast
The sixth stage of the Twilight history does not replace the true world with the apparent one. It says that "apparent" had meaning only against "true," so that both terms go together. A reading that leaves Nietzsche with the apparent world as his answer has stopped at stage five.
The metaphysical claim and the verdict it carries
Nietzsche attacks both the construction and the evaluation packed inside it. These are separable questions, and someone could accept the psychological diagnosis of why two-world theories are attractive while still thinking one of them true. Keeping them apart is what makes the diagnosis testable.
Thing in itself, middle and late
Human, All Too Human §16 calls it a matter of indifference. The late work and the notebooks call the notion absurd. That is a change of position and not a change of emphasis, and using the later formulation to read the earlier one reverses the chronology.
Soul atomism and the soul
Beyond Good and Evil §12 expels the soul as an indivisible, eternal atom, and in the same breath invites new hypotheses — mortal soul, soul as subjective multiplicity, soul as a social structure of drives and affects. What is being refused is a particular metaphysical model, not the topic.
Attacking causality and denying regularity
The argument is that cause and effect are concepts we project from an inner experience that does not contain them, and that they serve description rather than explanation. That is compatible with the world being orderly. It is not a claim that anything may follow anything.
Metaphysics and Christianity
Nietzsche treats them as one inheritance — "Christianity is Platonism for the people," as the Beyond Good and Evil Preface puts it. The identification is a polemical thesis with real work behind it, not an established historical result, and the critique of Christianity page carries the religious argument.
Published attack and notebook proposal
The published books dismantle. The notebooks also contain sketches for a positive counter-account of the world. Those sketches were not published, they are not consistent with each other, and they should not be read back into the books as the doctrine the critique was clearing ground for. See the notebook readings.

Where this theme stops

This page and the perspectivism page share passages, and it is easy to let one absorb the other. They ask different questions.

The question here is what happens to a set of constructions — true worlds, opposite values, substances, subjects, causes — when they are asked where they came from and what they are for. It is a question about the furniture of metaphysics, and it can be answered without saying anything general about knowledge. Twilight's six stages, the four errors, and the attack on soul atomism are all answers of that kind.

The question on the perspectivism page is what knowing is, given that there is no view from nowhere: whether perspectives can be ranked, what objectivity could mean afterwards, and whether the position can be stated without undermining itself. That is a question about the knower.

The overlap is real, and The Gay Science §344 and Genealogy III belong to both. But the difference shows up in what would count as a reply. An objection to the critique of metaphysics would defend one of the constructions — argue that substances are needed, or that causes are not projections. An objection to perspectivism would be about whether interpretation can be constrained at all. When a paragraph on this page starts sounding like a general theory of interpretation, it has crossed the line, and the crossing usually means the specific argument has been replaced by a slogan.

Explore by work

Twenty-four assignments across seven published works and one early unpublished essay, including the six starting readings. Core identifies the first encounter; development adds a mechanism or setting; complication tests an apparent answer; extension follows a related question. The survey follows publication chronology, the 1873 essay is marked as unpublished, and The Gay Science Book V is marked as the 1887 addition.

Three figures recur as targets, and it helps to know which is which. Plato is the origin of the true world in the Twilight history and the named error in the Beyond Good and Evil Preface. Kant is the figure whose unattainable-but-obliging version occupies the third stage, and whose thing in itself is the object of the middle-period sections in Human, All Too Human. Schopenhauer, Nietzsche's early teacher, is the metaphysician he had been closest to and does not treat at length in the passages assigned here; the Wagner and decadence page carries more of that break. As for what shaped Nietzsche's own position, F. A. Lange's History of Materialism is frequently named in the scholarship as a formative influence on his early thinking about knowledge and the thing in itself. That is a claim about intellectual history, argued about rather than settled, and this page neither relies on it nor disputes it.

The early essays1 reading assignment

One piece from 1873, which Nietzsche did not publish. It is the earliest place the question is visible, and its register is considerably more sweeping than anything he later released.

  • Earlier formulation

    On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense 1873, unpublished

    Concepts as worn metaphors

    Mügge: On Truth and Falsity in their Ultramoral Sense.

    Question: If a concept is made by dropping what is individual about cases, what kind of object is it?

    Why it belongs: The essay describes truth as a mobile army of metaphors and metonymies, worn smooth by long use until they are taken for the things themselves, and traces the duty of truthfulness to the requirements of living together. For this theme the useful part is the account of concept formation: the essay proposes that abstraction produces the very things metaphysics then treats as most real. Two cautions. It is unpublished, so it cannot stand as Nietzsche's settled 1873 position; and its scope is much wider than the specific attacks of 1886–88, which is why it is placed here as background rather than in the first encounter.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §11; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Read the essay (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

Human, All Too Human4 reading assignments

The 1878 book's opening chapter, "Of First and Last Things," is Nietzsche's first sustained anti-metaphysical programme. The voice is analytic rather than polemical, and the positions are more careful than the late ones. Preferred edition: Hollingdale.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §1

    A chemistry of concepts and sensations

    Zimmern: Chemistry of Ideas and Sensations.

    Question: What does it change to ask how a concept arose rather than whether it applies?

    Why it belongs: The opening section asks what would happen if the most valued things turned out to be related to and entangled with their apparent opposites, and proposes a chemistry of moral, religious and aesthetic ideas. It is the programme statement for the whole theme, and it names the target — the metaphysical demand for a miraculous origin — before any of the late polemics exist. Read it beside Beyond Good and Evil §2, which is the same objection eight years later and much less patient.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §2; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: §§1–2 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §9

    A metaphysical world conceded, and then made irrelevant

    Question: Nietzsche grants that a metaphysical world is possible. Why is granting it the stronger move?

    Why it belongs: The section allows that the existence of a metaphysical world can hardly be disputed, and then argues that nothing whatever could be said about it and that knowledge of it would be the most inconsequential of all knowledge. This is the strategy that distinguishes the middle period: not refutation but the removal of any use. It also gives a precise sense to what the six stages later narrate, since a world that makes no difference is already on its way to being abandoned.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"; §16.

    Online edition: "Of First and Last Things" (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §11

    Language mistaken for knowledge of the world

    Zimmern: Language As a Presumptive Science.

    Question: If words were once believed to state what things are, what did people end up owning instead?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that in believing they had knowledge of the world in language people acquired an enormous quantity of error, and that the belief in found truths has done lasting damage through the treatment of words as things. It is the mechanism behind the later claim that metaphysics is carried by grammar, stated a decade before the polemical version in Twilight and without the rhetoric.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Aphorism and style.

    Online edition: §11 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Earlier formulation

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §16

    The thing in itself set aside rather than destroyed

    Question: What is the difference between calling something indifferent and calling it absurd?

    Why it belongs: The measured early treatment of the appearance/thing-in-itself pair. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Will to Power §556; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §16 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

Book III (1882) supplies the warning about re-divinizing the world once God has been removed; Book V (1887) supplies the analysis of the commitment that outlives metaphysics. The five years between them are interpretively live and are marked below.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §109

    Neither machine nor organism nor law

    Common: Let us be on our Guard.

    Question: Nietzsche warns against calling the universe a machine, and also against calling it chaos. What is wrong with each?

    Why it belongs: The section is a list of the ways the removed God gets smuggled back — as design, as law, as organism, as machine, as purpose — and it insists that even aesthetic and moral vocabulary drags the old picture along with it. For this theme it is the constructive test case: having abolished the true world, what exactly is one allowed to say about this one? Nietzsche's answer, that we must beware even of the word "chaos," shows how narrow he thinks the remaining room is.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The Four Great Errors"; Death of God.

    Online edition: §109 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §344

    The commitment that survives the demolition

    Question: What can science test, and what must it presuppose?

    Why it belongs: The place where the critique turns on its own instrument. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §27; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §344 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §354

    Consciousness as a late and shallow instrument

    Kaufmann: On the "genius of the species." Common: The "Genius of the Species."

    Question: If what becomes conscious is what can be communicated, what has happened to everything else?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche proposes that consciousness developed under the pressure of the need to communicate, is therefore bound to what is common and averageable, and generalizes what it registers. The relevance to this theme is that it supplies a naturalistic story for why the categories metaphysics trusts most — unities, substances, the "I" — would be exactly the ones consciousness produces. It is offered as a conjecture, and Nietzsche says so; read it as a proposed mechanism rather than a result.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§16–17; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: §354 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra2 reading assignments

Two speeches from Part I, placed consecutively in the book and best read that way. They give the refusal of two-world theories in dramatic form, and they do so by way of a psychological diagnosis rather than an argument.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Afterworldly"

    The other world traced to the state of its inventors

    The German title is Von den Hinterweltlern, a coinage English versions render variously; locate the chapter by position rather than by title. It directly precedes "On the Despisers of the Body."

    Question: Zarathustra says he himself once cast his fancy beyond humanity. What does putting the diagnosis in the first person do to it?

    Why it belongs: The speech treats the other world as a projection of suffering and of an incapacity, made by those weary of the earth, and says that the creator of such worlds was once himself. It is the psychological complement to the historical argument in Twilight: the six stages say how the true world was built, this says why. Note that a diagnosis of a belief's origin is not a refutation of it, and that Human, All Too Human §1 had already made that distinction; the speech is more forceful and less careful.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Open the free edition (trans. Thomas Common; use the contents for the Part I chapter immediately before "The Despisers of the Body") · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Despisers of the Body"

    Soul as a word for something about the body

    Common: The Despisers of the Body.

    Question: The speech replaces one unity with another. What kind of unity is the body supposed to be?

    Why it belongs: The most concentrated refusal of the soul/body division in the corpus: body entirely and nothing else, soul as only a word for something about the body, the body as a great reason and a multiplicity with one sense. It matters for this theme as the replacement move — the place where the dismantling of substances is offered an alternative rather than left as a negation. What it does not do is explain how the alternative avoids the objections just made to unities, and the body and physiology page takes that question up.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §54; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Despisers of the Body" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil4 reading assignments

The book's first part is the canonical site, and the Preface sets the anti-dogmatic frame. Two later sections make specific moves the general attack does not. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296 across all nine parts.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil Preface

    Dogmatism as a clumsy approach

    Question: The Preface calls Plato's invention of pure spirit and the good in itself the worst and most persistent error. Is that a philosophical claim or a diagnosis of a temperament?

    Why it belongs: The Preface frames the whole book: dogmatizing philosophers have handled truth badly, Plato's error has stood the longest, and Christianity is Platonism for the people. It is where the theme's polemical identification of metaphysics with religion is announced. Read it as a statement of the book's programme rather than as an argument, and note that the gendered image it opens with is doing rhetorical work that the aphorism and style and women, marriage, and gender pages discuss.

    Read alongside: Critique of Christianity; The Antichrist §§10–12.

    Online edition: Preface (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil Part I, §§1–23

    The prejudices of philosophers, taken one at a time

    Question: Which sections apply the objection to opposite values, and which make separate arguments?

    Why it belongs: The longest sustained published treatment of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §1; Twilight, "The Four Great Errors".

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §54

    Modern philosophy as an attack on the soul

    Part III, "The Religious Nature" (Zimmern); Kaufmann: "The Religious Mood."

    Question: If modern philosophy has been an assassination attempt on the old soul concept, why does Nietzsche treat that as anti-Christian rather than as merely technical?

    Why it belongs: The section reads the whole modern epistemological tradition as a long attack on the subject-and-predicate model that underwrote the soul, and treats that attack as an assault on the presupposition of Christian doctrine rather than as a neutral development. It shows the theme's two halves joined: a technical dispute about substance is also a move in the religious argument. Read it with §12, where Nietzsche keeps the topic while discarding the atom.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §12; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Part III (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §211

    Philosophers who legislate rather than find

    Part VI, "We Scholars."

    Question: If genuine philosophers create values, what is left of the idea that philosophy describes how things are?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes philosophical labourers, who fix and formalize existing valuations, from philosophers proper, whose knowing is creating and whose willing to truth is willing to power. The section is where the critique of metaphysics turns into a positive job description, and it is also where the theme becomes most exposed: if philosophy commands rather than discovers, the earlier objections to the metaphysicians look less like errors of method than like a dispute over who gets to legislate.

    Read alongside: Revaluation; Free spirits.

    Online edition: Part VI (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals3 reading assignments

The Genealogy's contribution is the grammar argument in the first essay and the account, in the third, of how the will to truth destroys the ideal that produced it. The free edition numbers the Preface and each essay separately.

  • Core of the argument about substances

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §13

    The lightning and its flash

    Question: Nietzsche says the deed is everything and the doer is a fiction added by language. Does that claim need the metaphysics it attacks in order to be stated?

    Why it belongs: The section argues that popular morality separates strength from its expression as if there were an indifferent substratum free to express strength or not, and that this is the same error as saying the lightning flashed — doubling the deed into a doer and a doing. It is the most concentrated statement of the attack on substance in the published work, and it is the point at which that attack has moral consequences, since without a doer behind the deed the practice of holding responsible loses its object. Nietzsche is aware of the cost and, here, welcomes it.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The Four Great Errors"; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each essay are numbered separately) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §24

    Science as the ideal's most recent form

    Question: Nietzsche calls the will to truth the kernel of the ascetic ideal rather than its opponent. What would refute that identification?

    Why it belongs: The section denies that science is the counter-movement to the ascetic ideal and argues that both rest on an overvaluation of truth, so that the free spirits who think themselves emancipated are the ideal's most recent believers. Placed on this page, it explains why dismantling the true world does not finish the job: what has to be examined next is a valuation, and valuations are not removed by showing that a doctrine was false.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §344; Free spirits.

    Online edition: Essay III §24 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §27

    A doctrine destroyed by its own truthfulness

    Question: If Christian truthfulness dissolved Christian dogma, what is the next thing it should dissolve, and who will be doing the dissolving?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that Christianity as a dogma perished at the hands of its own morality, and that in the same way Christianity as a morality must now perish — and that this is the law of self-overcoming applied to itself. For this theme it is the most important structural claim on the page: the critique of metaphysics is presented not as an external attack but as the completion of a movement already inside the tradition. That is an elegant story, and it is worth asking how much of it is argument and how much is the shape Nietzsche wants history to have.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §344; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Essay III §27 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

    Twilight of the Idols "Maxims and Arrows" §26

    The will to a system distrusted

    Ludovici: "Maxims and Missiles."

    Question: Is distrust of systems a philosophical position or a refusal to take one?

    Why it belongs: One line: Nietzsche distrusts all systematizers and avoids them, and calls the will to a system a lack of integrity. It is the aphoristic form of the theme, and it states the cost as well as the claim — a critique that treats systematic completeness as a symptom cannot then present itself as a finished alternative. Read it beside the six stages, which are as close to a system as this material gets.

The Antichrist3 reading assignments

The polemical extension of the theme into the philosophy of religion. The free translation is by H. L. Mencken and omits parts of §§29, 35 and 38; the course follows Kaufmann.

  • Extension

    The Antichrist §§10–12

    Philosophy accused of keeping theology's furniture

    Question: The charge is about inheritance rather than about belief. What evidence would establish it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that German philosophy is theology in disguise, names Kant's success as the deepest form of the problem, and treats the instinct that preserved theology's structure after its content was given up as the real object of attack. It is the sharpest form of the claim that metaphysics and religion are one inheritance. The claim is polemical and specific to a national tradition, and the passage asserts far more than it shows; read it for the shape of the accusation rather than for its historical support.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil, Preface; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: §§10–12 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §15

    A complete world of imaginary causes

    Question: Nietzsche lists God, soul, spirit, free will as imaginary causes and sin, redemption, grace as imaginary effects. What makes the list a system rather than a collection of errors?

    Why it belongs: The section sets out a whole fictitious world of causes, beings, psychology and teleology, and argues that the difference from dreaming is that this one is believed. It gives the clearest demonstration of what the attack on causes does when applied to a religion: the individual concepts are not examined one by one but shown to hold each other up. Read it as the application of "The Four Great Errors" rather than as independent evidence for it.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The Four Great Errors"; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: §15 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §54

    Convictions as prisons

    Question: If convictions are more dangerous to truth than lies, where does that leave a book as convinced as this one?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that the man of faith is not free to have a conscience about questions of true and false, and that great intellects are sceptical because strength makes freedom from conviction possible. It belongs on this page as the self-directed test: the critique of metaphysics is carried out at enormous rhetorical pressure, and this section supplies the standard by which that pressure can be judged. Whether The Antichrist meets its own standard is a fair question to put to it.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §344; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: §§50–55 (trans. H. L. Mencken; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

Five numbered entries. They are included because this is a theme where the notebooks go further than the books and are quoted as if they did not — and the distance between the two is itself worth reading. Treat them as experiments that sharpen a question, never as the settled version of what the published works say more carefully.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Where an entry and a published passage diverge, the published text governs. On this page the divergence is the point: Human, All Too Human §16 and Beyond Good and Evil §22 are more cautious than the entries below, and Nietzsche had the opportunity to publish the bolder formulations and did not.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different times.

Preferred edition: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Kaufmann. The numbered links open Anthony M. Ludovici's translation at a nearby printed page. Edition guidance.

The true world, and what replaces it3 assignments · 3 entries

The entries where Nietzsche states the position without the published qualifications, and one where he sketches a replacement he never published in that form.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §12

    Aim, unity, and a world behind this one

    Question: The entry treats the collapse of the true world as a loss rather than a liberation. Which of the two does the published work say?

    Why it belongs: The entry sets out the categories through which value had been read into events — aim, unity, and a true world beyond becoming — and argues that once they are seen to be things we put in, the world looks valueless. It connects this theme directly to nihilism: the dismantling has consequences, and they are not automatically cheerful. Note how differently the same event is described in Twilight, where the abolition of the true world is a bright noon.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "How the True World Finally Became a Fable"; Nihilism.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §481

    The line that is quoted instead of the books

    Question: Is the claim that there are no facts, or that facts are not available apart from interpretation?

    Why it belongs: Written against positivism, this is the source of the formulation about there being no facts, only interpretations, and of the claim that our needs interpret the world. It is the most cited sentence in the vicinity of this theme and it is an unpublished note whose published counterparts are narrower. Included here as a caution rather than as evidence: a reader who arrives at the critique of metaphysics through this entry will expect a much more sweeping position than the books defend. The perspectivism page treats the entry at length.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §22; Perspectivism.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1067

    A world of forces offered in place of the one removed

    Question: Is this a description of the world, or the description of what a world would have to look like for the critique to have been worth making?

    Why it belongs: The closing entry of the compilation describes a world of force without beginning or end, a household of energy that does not grow or spend itself but only changes, and ends with the line that this world is the will to power and nothing besides. It is the nearest thing in the notebooks to a positive metaphysics replacing the one dismantled, and it is exactly the kind of totalizing claim the published work treats with suspicion — Beyond Good and Evil §22 offers the same idea and immediately concedes that it too is only interpretation. Its position as the last entry is the compilation editors' arrangement and not Nietzsche's, and readers should not take the placement as evidence that he regarded it as his conclusion.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §22; Will to power.

Things, causes, and errors worth having2 assignments · 2 entries

Two entries on the constructions this theme is about. Both go past the corresponding published formulations, and the gap is instructive.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §493

    Truth as the kind of error we cannot live without

    Question: If an error is indispensable to a form of life, in what sense is calling it an error still doing any work?

    Why it belongs: A single sentence: truth is the kind of error without which a certain species of living being could not live. It is the compact form of the position The Gay Science §§110–111 builds more slowly and §121 then guards against, and it is worth setting beside that guard — "life is no argument" — because the entry on its own reads as the pragmatism the published book refuses. The link opens at a nearby printed page; use Find for the entry number.

    Read alongside: Perspectivism; The Gay Science §354.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §556

    A thing in itself called absurd

    Question: If a thing's properties are its effects on other things, what has been denied — an entity, or a way of speaking?

    Why it belongs: The entry argues that a thing in itself is as absurd as a sense in itself, since the properties of a thing are effects on other things, and that removing all relations leaves nothing. This is the theme's sharpest anti-substance formulation, and it is considerably more decisive than Human, All Too Human §16, where Nietzsche was content to call the thing in itself a matter of indifference. The distance between the two is a good measure of how much the notebooks claim that the books do not.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §16; Genealogy I §13.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • Ten years of hardening. Read Human, All Too Human §9 and §16, then Twilight's six stages and Will to Power §556. Is the late position better argued, or only more confident?
  • What the doer costs. Follow Genealogy I §13 into Twilight, "The Four Great Errors". If there is no agent behind the act, what happens to the rest of the Genealogy's moral argument?
  • The replacement problem. Compare Zarathustra I, "On the Despisers of the Body" with Will to Power §1067. Both offer something in place of what was removed. Which one is Nietzsche prepared to publish, and why might that be?
  • The critique applied to itself. Read The Gay Science §344, then The Antichrist §54 and Beyond Good and Evil §211. Does Nietzsche have a position left, or a practice?

A question to carry forward

If "apparent" only had meaning against "true," what should we now call the world we are left with — and does any available word avoid dragging one half of the old pair back in?
