If a value has a history, what follows — and what does not follow — about whether it should still command us?

Genealogy is the historical and psychological investigation of how a value, a concept, or an institution came to be, and of what its present form conceals about that coming-to-be. The method has a polemical edge: showing that morality has a history, and a contingent and sometimes ugly one, removes the appearance of timeless self-evidence. But removing an appearance is not the same as refuting a claim, and Nietzsche's own best statements of the method are careful about the difference.

Two confusions are worth naming at the start. The first is the genetic fallacy: the inference from a value's disreputable origin to its present falsity or worthlessness. Daybreak §44 states the problem directly, and the site treats Nietzsche as raising the question of a value rather than settling it by tracing where it came from. The second is the assumption that genealogy uncovers a hidden real meaning. It does not. It treats values as historical objects that have served particular psychological and social functions, and asks whether those functions still hold and at what cost.

This page is about the method. For a complete reading of the book in which it is most fully deployed, follow the twelve-lesson course on On the Genealogy of Morals, covering the Preface and all three essays in order.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter follows the method from its announcement in the middle works, through its central methodological caution, to one worked example and the demand for a comparative science of morals. Continue through the fuller route to test the method against its own standards, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook material is a separate, optional, and explicitly secondary investigation.

Essential first encounter

Six short assignments, read in order. The sequence moves from the announcement of a historical philosophy, through the caution that keeps it from becoming debunking, to a programme, a demand, one worked example, and the call for a comparative science. The three essays of the Genealogy are deliberately not assigned here as a single block; the opening of Essay I stands as the worked example, and the rest is available through the work survey and the book course.

  1. Core

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§1–2

    Historical philosophy announced

    Zimmern: Chemistry of Ideas and Sensations; Inherited Faults of Philosophers.

    Question: What does Nietzsche think philosophers get wrong when they reason about human beings without history?

    Why it belongs: The opening two sections set the programme: a chemistry of moral, religious and aesthetic conceptions, and a diagnosis of philosophy's habit of treating the recent European person as a fixed type. The claim is that concepts have emerged and can be understood through their emergence. Note that §1 raises the possibility that the results of such a chemistry might be uncomfortable, which already ties the method to an evaluative stake.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Volume I, Preface (1886); Daybreak §44.

    Online edition: §§1–2 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Daybreak §44

    Origin and significance are not the same

    Kennedy: Origin and Meaning.

    Question: If knowing where a practice came from does not tell us what it now means, what is the historical inquiry for?

    Why it belongs: This is the caution that separates genealogy from debunking, stated by Nietzsche himself before the mature method exists. Tracing an origin does not by itself establish present significance, because a practice can acquire new functions and shed old ones. Assigned second on purpose: a reader who takes this seriously will not read the later worked examples as automatic refutations, which is the most common misreading of the Genealogy.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Preface §§5–6; Daybreak §95.

    Online edition: §44 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    The Gay Science §7

    What a genealogical programme would actually contain

    Kaufmann: Something for the industrious. Common: Something for the Laborious.

    Question: Nietzsche lists histories nobody has written. What kind of evidence would each of them require?

    Why it belongs: The section names a research programme rather than a polemic: histories of love, of avarice, of conscience, of pity, of cruelty, and of the division of the day and the consequences of work and festival. It is the most concrete statement in the published work of what genealogical inquiry would consist in, and it makes the method look like collaborative scholarship rather than private unmasking. It is also a useful check on the later books: very little of this programme was carried out.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §345; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

    Online edition: §7 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§5–6

    The value of the values themselves

    Question: What would count as evidence for or against the value of a morality, as opposed to evidence about its history?

    Why it belongs: The methodological centrepiece of the late work. Nietzsche moves from the question of the origin of moral judgments to the demand that the value of these values be called into question, and insists that this requires knowledge of the conditions under which they grew. The two sections together show the method's structure: history is the instrument, evaluation is the aim, and the second does not follow automatically from the first. Read them against Daybreak §44 and the relation between the two becomes the page's central problem.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §44; On the Genealogy of Morals — Preface §§7–8.

    Online edition: Preface §§5–6 (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface is numbered separately from the essays) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§1–3

    The method shown at work on a small target

    Question: Nietzsche objects that his opponents' history is unhistorical. What would a better history have to do differently?

    Why it belongs: One worked example, kept short, rather than the whole book. In these opening sections Nietzsche takes the English psychologists' account of the origin of "good" — utility, forgotten and then felt as unconditional — and objects that it is psychologically and historically implausible. The passage shows the moves that constitute the method: an existing explanation is identified, its assumptions are located, and a counter-explanation is proposed that is supposed to fit the evidence better. Whether his counter-explanation does fit is exactly what the rest of the essay has to earn.

    Read alongside: On the Genealogy of Morals — Preface §§7–8; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§1–3 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §186

    A science of morals that does not yet exist

    Question: What is the difference between justifying a morality and collecting the material for a comparative study of moralities?

    Why it belongs: The opening section of Part V argues that what has been called the science of morals has really been an attempt to give a foundation to one morality, and that the preparatory work of description, comparison and classification has not been done. It names the method's project as comparative rather than legislative. The polemic against Schopenhauer here is sharp, and Nietzsche's charge that moral philosophers lacked historical sense is itself a historical claim open to examination.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §7; Beyond Good and Evil §32.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Continue with these five investigations after the first encounter. Each assignment below opens its explanation and reading links in the work survey. Read the assignments within a group in order; the question above them explains what the comparison is meant to test.

1. Does tracing an origin discredit a value?

The method's most common misuse and Nietzsche's own defences against it. Ask in each case whether an explanation is being offered, or a verdict.

    Origin and significance held apart.
  2. Human, All Too Human — §39

  3. Human, All Too Human — §99
    Separating an act from the evaluation of it.
  4. Human, All Too Human — §107
    What a thoroughgoing naturalism would cost moral judgment.

2. What does a genealogy actually explain?

The middle works practise the method patiently, before it becomes polemical. Watch what kind of claim each passage is making.

  1. Daybreak — §95

  2. Daybreak — §103
    A moral feeling examined as a composite.
  3. Daybreak — §202
    Moral categories applied to what medicine had begun to reclassify.
  4. The Gay Science — §345

3. The method applied to itself

Genealogy is not exempt from genealogy. These passages ask what the genealogist's own commitment to truth is a form of.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §6
    Philosophy read as the confession of its author.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §12
    Objectivity redefined as more eyes, not none.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§24–28
    The will to truth as the latest form of the ascetic ideal.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §227
    Honesty as the virtue that must question itself.

4. Worked examples at speed

The 1888 books apply the method rapidly to large targets. Read them for technique, and note where rhetoric outruns evidence.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — The Problem of Socrates
    A founding figure read as a symptom.
  2. Twilight of the Idols — The Four Great Errors
    The conceptual apparatus of moral judgment itself.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — The 'Improvers' of Mankind
    Comparative anthropology, with a historical claim that needs checking.
  4. The Antichrist — §57
    Christian moral law set beside the Law of Manu.

5. Periodization and the long task

The method implies a history with stages and a programme that outlives any one book. What would completing it require?

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §32
    Pre-moral, moral, and extra-moral periods.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §187
    Moralities read as means rather than as arguments.
  3. Beyond Good and Evil — §188
    What long constraint is said to have produced.
  4. Beyond Good and Evil — §230

Keep these distinctions in view

Origin and present function
Daybreak §44 states it and the Genealogy relies on it: a practice can be taken over, reinterpreted and put to new use, so its beginning does not fix its meaning. A genealogy that ignores this has become the genetic fallacy it was meant to avoid.
Raising a question and settling it
The Preface demands that the value of moral values be called into question. Calling into question is an opening move. Where the late books move straight from history to verdict, the move is worth marking rather than adopting.
Historical claim and psychological claim
Many passages make both at once: something happened, and it happened because of a particular kind of motive. These need separate evidence. Nietzsche's psychological readings are often more developed than the historical narratives they rest on.
Nietzsche's polemic and independent history
Claims about priestly castes, about the Law of Manu, or about the actual practices of ancient peoples are Nietzsche's assertions. Some rest on the scholarship available to him, some on sources now regarded as unreliable. Read them as his claims and check them elsewhere.
Description and endorsement
Showing what a morality does for the type of person who holds it is not approval of that type or that morality. The method is equally capable of describing what Nietzsche admires and what he detests.
The method and its results
One can accept that values have histories worth investigating without accepting the particular genealogy of "good and evil" that Essay I proposes. This page is about the first; master and slave morality is about the second.
Practising the method and dramatizing its consequences
Zarathustra is absent from this page's survey on purpose. It stages what follows once values are seen as made rather than found, but it does not conduct historical or psychological investigation, and folding it in would blur the distinction the page exists to keep.
Published method and notebook methodology
The most quotable methodological formulations — including the line about facts and interpretations — are notebook entries in a posthumous compilation. Their published counterparts exist and say something more careful.

A note on Foucault

Foucault's "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) is an influential modern essay on the method, and Foucault's own historical work is partly modelled on it. He sharpens some features — the role of contingency, the discontinuity of historical formations, the refusal of a single origin — at the cost of softening others, in particular Nietzsche's continuing interest in evaluating, and not only describing, what a genealogy turns up. Worth reading; not the only available framing, and not a substitute for the passages above. Readers who come to Nietzsche through Foucault should check the emphasis on discontinuity against Genealogy Preface §§5–6, where the point of the history is explicitly to make an evaluative question askable.

The early essays1 reading assignment

An early, unpublished piece in which Nietzsche performs the operation before he has a name for it. Its status matters: he did not publish it, and it should not be treated as a statement of a settled position from 1873.

  • Earlier formulation

    On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense 1873, unpublished

    Asking how truth came to be valued

    Mügge: On Truth and Falsity in their Ultramoral Sense.

    Question: The essay asks about the value of truth rather than its nature. What does that shift of question make visible?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche treats truthfulness as a social requirement with an origin, and describes concepts as the residue of forgotten metaphor. He is not investigating truth's metaphysical credentials but the history of a valuation and the kind of creature that needs it. That is the genealogical move, almost a decade before Human, All Too Human announces it. The essay's own rhetorical extravagance is a reason to read it as an experiment rather than a doctrine.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §§1–2; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

    Online edition: Read the essay (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

Human, All Too Human6 reading assignments

The 1878 book in which moral concepts are first systematically treated as historical products. The opening sections carry the programme; the rest is the workshop. The 1886 preface was added eight years later and is marked as retrospective.

  • Core

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§1–2

    Historical philosophy announced

    Question: What does philosophy lose by reasoning about a human being without a history?

    Why it belongs: The programme stated. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Volume I, Preface (1886).

    Online edition: §§1–2 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §39

    How moral responsibility gets constructed

    Zimmern: The Fable of Intelligible Freedom.

    Question: Nietzsche describes a sequence of stages. Is the sequence historical, conceptual, or both?

    Why it belongs: The section reconstructs how acts came to be called good or bad, how the error was transferred to the person, and then to the motive, and then to the whole being. It is an early model of what a genealogy of a moral concept looks like when carried out step by step. The conclusion — that nobody is responsible — is a strong claim; note how much of the argument's weight rests on the naturalism rather than on the history.

    Read alongside: §107; Twilight of the Idols — The Four Great Errors.

    Online edition: §39 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §96

    Morality as obedience to custom

    Zimmern: Custom and Morality.

    Question: If morality is obedience to inherited custom, what happens to the distinction between moral and merely conventional?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche identifies being moral with obedience to a long-established law or tradition, and notes that the tradition's content is a separate matter from the obedience. It is the hinge that lets the later work treat different moralities as comparable objects rather than as competing candidates for the truth. Daybreak will develop this into the morality of custom at length.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §9; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

    Online edition: §96 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §99

    Separating an act from the evaluation of it

    Zimmern: The Innocent Side of So-called Evil Actions.

    Question: What remains of an action once the moral colouring is set aside — and is anything lost?

    Why it belongs: The genealogist's first practical operation: prising an act apart from the moral evaluation attached to it, so that the evaluation becomes a separate object of study. Nietzsche's claim that so-called evil actions aim at self-preservation is itself a psychological hypothesis, and a reductive one; the technique it illustrates does not stand or fall with it.

    Read alongside: §39; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §99 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §107

    What a thoroughgoing naturalism would cost moral judgment

    Zimmern: Irresponsibility and Innocence.

    Question: Nietzsche calls the conclusion bitter. Does the method require accepting it?

    Why it belongs: The section pushes the naturalist argument to complete irresponsibility, and then acknowledges how hard that is to live with. It marks the point at which genealogy stops being a historical exercise and starts making demands. It also shows Nietzsche at his least polemical about a conclusion he finds uncomfortable, which is a useful corrective to the late rhetoric.

    Read alongside: §39; Twilight of the Idols — The Four Great Errors.

    Online edition: §107 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · retrospective (1886)

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, Preface §§1–7 (1886)

    The method described eight years later

    Question: How much of this account of the book's purpose is visible in the book itself?

    Why it belongs: Written in 1886, the preface narrates the book as a campaign of liberation and a discipline of suspicion against inherited valuations. It is the clearest statement of what Nietzsche later took the middle-period method to have been for. Because it is retrospective, it is evidence about the later Nietzsche's self-understanding, and it should be checked against the 1878 text rather than substituted for it.

    Read alongside: §§1–2; Daybreak — Preface §§3–4 (1886).

    Online edition: Preface (trans. Helen Zimmern; the Preface is numbered separately from the body) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak6 reading assignments

The 1881 book is the most patient practice of the method in the corpus: moral judgments read symptomatically, without the polemical compression of the late work. The 1886 preface is a later addition and is marked as such.

  • Development

    Daybreak §9

    The morality of custom

    Kennedy: Conception of the Morality of Custom.

    Question: If morality is obedience to custom, what makes a custom feel like a moral requirement rather than a habit?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche treats morality as obedience to customs and describes the pressure that tradition exerts as itself the source of moral feeling. The section supplies the historical picture that the later accounts of conscience and guilt build on. His account of primitive societies draws on the ethnographic sources of his day and is best read as a hypothesis about mechanism rather than as established history.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §96; §103.

    Online edition: §9 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Daybreak §44

    Origin and significance are not the same

    Kennedy: Origin and Meaning.

    Question: What is the historical inquiry for, if it does not settle present meaning?

    Why it belongs: The caution that separates genealogy from debunking. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§5–6.

    Online edition: §44 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §95

    Historical refutation as the definitive refutation

    Kennedy: The Historical Refutation as the Decisive One.

    Question: Does this section contradict §44, or specify the narrow case in which history does refute?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that formerly one tried to disprove the existence of God, and that now it is enough to show how the belief arose, at which point the belief becomes superfluous. Set beside §44 this is the page's sharpest internal tension: there, origin does not fix significance; here, an account of origin is said to be decisive. The resolution most readers reach — that explanation undercuts a belief only when the belief's claim to authority rested on its not having such an explanation — is an interpretation, and the text does not state it.

    Read alongside: §44; Death of God.

    Online edition: §95 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §103

    Two different things that denying morality can mean

    Kennedy: There are Two Classes of People who deny Morality.

    Question: Which of the two denials does a genealogy support, and does it support the other?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche separates denying that moral motives actually drive people's conduct from denying that moral judgments rest on truths, and is precise about which he is committed to and in what sense. It is the clearest middle-period statement of what the method claims and what it does not, and a useful check on readings that make Daybreak straightforwardly anti-moral — he is explicit that criticizing the reasons offered for a way of acting is not the same as recommending against it.

    Read alongside: §202; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: §103 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §202

    Moral categories and what medicine had begun to reclassify

    Kennedy: The Care of the Health.

    Question: What follows for a moral vocabulary if conditions it names are reclassified as illness?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche compares the treatment of the criminal with the earlier treatment of the sick, and argues that moral categories lag behind changes in how conditions are understood. It shows the method making a claim about the present rather than the past. The analogy assumes a medical framing that is itself historically situated, and Nietzsche's confidence in it should not be read as a settled result.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — The Problem of Socrates; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: §202 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · retrospective (1886)

    Daybreak Preface §§3–4 (1886)

    Working beneath the moral surface

    Question: The image is of digging underneath. What does it imply about what is found there?

    Why it belongs: Written five years after the book, the preface describes Nietzsche as working underground beneath European morality, undermining an ancient confidence. It is the earliest place he explicitly names the project the method serves. Like the Human, All Too Human preface, it is a retrospective account and belongs to 1886 rather than to 1881.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human — Preface (1886); Genealogy — Preface §§5–6.

    Online edition: Preface (trans. J. M. Kennedy; the Preface is numbered separately from the body) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

The 1882 book states the research programme and, in the 1887 Book V, sharpens it into the question of morality as such. Book V is a later addition and is marked as such.

  • Core

    The Gay Science §7

    What a genealogical programme would actually contain

    Kaufmann: Something for the industrious. Common: Something for the Laborious.

    Question: What evidence would each of the listed histories require?

    Why it belongs: The programme in its most concrete form. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §345; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

    Online edition: §7 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §335

    The moral psychologist as his own object

    Kaufmann: Long live physics! Common: Cheers for Physics!

    Question: If self-knowledge is unreliable, how can the genealogist trust his own account of his motives?

    Why it belongs: The section attacks the confidence of moral self-judgment, argues that we know our own actions far less well than we assume, and calls for the study of the world's laws and necessities as the basis for creating values. It brings the method's epistemological problem home: the investigator is the same kind of creature as the investigated.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §6; Genealogy III §12.

    Online edition: §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §345

    Morality itself made the problem

    Common: Morality as a Problem.

    Question: What is the difference between doubting a particular moral claim and treating morality as a problem?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes the scholar who collects moral facts from the thinker who asks what morality is worth, and complains that the second question has scarcely been posed. Published in 1887, the same year as the Genealogy, it is the closest published statement of the programme that book executes. Its placement in the later Book V matters: this is the sharpened, late formulation, not the 1882 one.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§5–6; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: §345 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil7 reading assignments

Part V, "Natural History of Morals" (§§186–203), is the method's home in this book; the hermeneutical principle that licenses symptomatic reading is stated much earlier, in Part I. The free Zimmern edition has no per-section anchors, so the links open the relevant part; use Find for the section number. Zimmern's part titles differ from Kaufmann's in Parts III, IV and VIII.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §6

    Philosophy read as the confession of its author

    Question: If every philosophy is a confession, what is the status of this claim about philosophies?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche proposes that a philosophy is the involuntary memoir of its author and that the decisive question is which morality the work is aiming at. This is the hermeneutical principle on which symptomatic reading depends, and it is the point at which the method becomes reflexively awkward: the principle applies to Nietzsche too, as he elsewhere concedes.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§24–28; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §32

    Pre-moral, moral, and extra-moral

    Question: Is this periodization a historical finding, a conceptual scheme, or a proposal?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche divides the history of moral judgment into a period in which acts were judged by consequences, one in which they are judged by intention, and a coming one in which intention would be seen as surface. It is the clearest periodization he offers, and it shows the method producing large-scale historical structure. The stages are asserted with very little supporting evidence, which is worth noting rather than passing over.

    Read alongside: §186; §187.

    Online edition: Part II (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §186

    A science of morals that does not yet exist

    Question: What would the preparatory work actually involve?

    Why it belongs: The method's project named as comparative rather than legislative. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §7.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §187

    Moralities read as means rather than as arguments

    Question: What does asking what a morality is for leave out that asking whether it is true would capture?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche treats a moral system's foundations as a symptom of what its author needed, and asks what such demands reveal about those who make them. It is the functional half of the method, and it is also where the method is most vulnerable: explaining why someone holds a view is compatible with the view being correct.

    Read alongside: §6; Daybreak §44.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §188

    What long constraint is said to have produced

    Question: Can the claim that constraint produced European freedom of mind be tested, and how?

    Why it belongs: The section argues that every morality is a long compulsion, and that what is valuable in European thought and art grew under precisely such tyranny. It complicates any reading of the method as straightforwardly liberating, and it should be read alongside the passages endorsing rank and severity rather than separated from them. The historical claim is broad and Nietzsche offers little to support it beyond examples chosen to fit.

    Read alongside: §230; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §227

    Honesty as the virtue that must question itself

    Question: Nietzsche calls honesty the one virtue left to free spirits. What does he say must be done to it?

    Why it belongs: The section urges working on honesty, warns that a virtue can become a source of vanity and stupidity, and asks that it be kept from turning into a new form of moralism. It gives the genealogist's own commitment a genealogy, which is the reflexive turn the third essay of the Genealogy completes.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§24–28; Free spirits.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §230

    Translating the human being back into nature

    Question: What would it take to describe a human being without the interpretations the method has removed?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche sets the task of translating the human back into nature, mastering the vanities that have overwritten the basic text, and making the person stand before the person. It is the method's programme at its most ambitious, and it raises an obvious difficulty: if all seeing is interpretation, the idea of a basic text underneath needs more defence than it receives here.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §12; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals7 reading assignments

The 1887 book is where the method reaches its mature form and turns on itself. This survey takes the methodological frame — the Preface, the opening of Essay I, the note appended to Essay I, and the epistemological sections of Essay III — rather than the substantive genealogies, which belong to master and slave morality and the psychology of morality. The Preface and each essay are numbered separately in every edition. For a complete reading of all three essays in order, use the twelve-lesson course.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§1–4

    Where the question came from

    Question: Nietzsche gives the question a personal history. Does that strengthen or weaken the case for it?

    Why it belongs: The opening sections describe knowers who are strangers to themselves, and then trace Nietzsche's own long preoccupation with the origin of good and evil back to a boyhood attempt. The autobiographical framing is deliberate: the book begins by applying its own principle that a philosophy carries its author's history. It also, conveniently, presents the inquiry as long-matured rather than opportunistic.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §6; Preface §§5–6.

    Online edition: Preface §§1–4 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§5–6

    The value of the values themselves

    Question: What would count as evidence about the value of a morality?

    Why it belongs: The methodological centrepiece of the late work. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §44; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: Preface §§5–6 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Preface §§7–8

    Real history, and the demand for slow reading

    Question: What distinguishes the history Nietzsche wants from the history he is attacking?

    Why it belongs: §7 sets documented, actual history against what Nietzsche calls the hypothesis-mongering of the English moral psychologists; §8 warns that the book will be unintelligible to readers who have not learned to read slowly and ruminate. Together they state the method's standard of evidence and its demand on the reader. The irony worth noticing is that Nietzsche's own historical claims frequently fall short of the standard §7 sets.

    Read alongside: Essay I §§1–3; Aphorism and style.

    Online edition: Preface §§7–8 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§1–3

    The method shown at work on a small target

    Question: What would a better history have to do differently?

    Why it belongs: One worked example rather than the whole book. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Essay I §17 and note; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§1–3 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §17 and the appended Note

    Genealogy proposed as collective research

    Question: What kind of discipline is Nietzsche asking for, and who would carry it out?

    Why it belongs: The Note appended after §17 invites philologists, historians, physiologists and physicians to take up the history of moral concepts, and suggests a university prize for the question. It is the strongest evidence that Nietzsche took genealogy to be a research programme rather than a private polemic, and it directly echoes The Gay Science §7. Nietzsche's closing Note is printed after Essay I §17 in the free edition, as the edition guidance records; make sure not to skip past it.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §7; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

    Online edition: Essay I §17 and Note (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §12

    Objectivity redefined as more eyes, not none

    Question: If there is no view from nowhere, what makes one genealogy better than another?

    Why it belongs: The section rejects the ideal of a knowing without interest or perspective and substitutes a discipline of bringing more perspectives and more affects to bear. This is the epistemology the method needs: the genealogist does not stand outside history, and the claim to objectivity is reconstructed rather than abandoned. The passage is treated at length on Perspectivism and the will to truth; here it is the licence for the method's own standing.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §230; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

    Online edition: Essay III §12 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§24–28

    The method turned on the genealogist

    Question: If the will to truth is the ascetic ideal's latest form, what happens to the book making that claim?

    Why it belongs: The closing sections argue that science is not the opposite of the ascetic ideal but its most recent expression, and that the unconditional will to truth is faith in the ideal itself. Applied to the Genealogy, this makes the book's own probity part of what it is diagnosing. Nietzsche accepts the consequence rather than evading it, and the resulting instability is a feature of the method, not a flaw to be tidied away.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §227; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Essay III §§24–28 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols4 reading assignments

The 1888 book is a portfolio of demonstrations: short chapters each applying the method to a different target. Section numbers restart in each chapter, and Ludovici's chapter titles differ from Kaufmann's; the free edition's contents list also omits two chapters that are present in the text. See the edition guidance.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols The Problem of Socrates

    A founding figure read as a symptom

    Question: Nietzsche treats Socrates' rationalism as a symptom. Of what, and on what evidence?

    Why it belongs: The chapter reads the equation of reason, virtue and happiness as a sign of decline rather than as philosophy's self-evident beginning, and reads Socrates' physiognomy and plebeian origins as part of the diagnosis. It is a compact demonstration of symptomatic reading. It is also the clearest case where the method shades into ad hominem, and the physiognomic material in particular rests on assumptions that no longer stand.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §202; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: The Problem of Socrates (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols 'Reason' in Philosophy

    Metaphysics given a history

    Question: What is the genealogical claim here, as distinct from the philosophical objection?

    Why it belongs: The chapter treats the philosophers' hatred of becoming and their faith in a true world as expressions of a way of life rather than as conclusions, and locates the error partly in language and its grammatical habits. It shows the method applied to metaphysics rather than to morality. The appeal to grammar as the source of metaphysical error is suggestive and frequently quoted; it is also a hypothesis rather than a demonstration.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §230; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: 'Reason' in Philosophy (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols The Four Great Errors §§1–8

    The conceptual apparatus of moral judgment itself

    Question: If the will is not a cause, what becomes of praise, blame and responsibility?

    Why it belongs: The chapter takes apart the confusion of cause and consequence, false causality, imaginary causes, and the error of free will, and argues that the doctrine of the will was invented for the purpose of punishing. It is genealogy applied not to a particular value but to the machinery that makes moral evaluation possible, and it makes explicit the practical interest Nietzsche thinks the machinery serves.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: The Four Great Errors (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols The 'Improvers' of Mankind

    Comparative anthropology, with claims that need checking

    Question: The chapter sets two moral systems side by side. What is being compared, and what is being assumed?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche places Christian moral training beside the Law of Manu, arguing that both are systems of taming or breeding and that neither should be read as improvement. It is the most direct example of the comparative method in the late work. It is also where the historical material is weakest: Nietzsche relied on a French translation of the Manu text and read it through his own scheme, and his account of the caste system and of the outcast groups it describes cannot be taken as a reliable description. The chapter contains some of his most brutal formulations, and they are his, stated plainly here rather than softened.

    Read alongside: The Antichrist §57; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: The 'Improvers' of Mankind (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Antichrist2 reading assignments

The method at full polemical intensity on its largest target. The substantive argument belongs to the critique of Christianity; what is taken here is the technique. The free Mencken text omits portions of §§29, 35 and 38 and the separate Law Against Christianity; see the edition guidance.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §15

    Symptomatic reading in compact form

    Question: Nietzsche calls a whole vocabulary imaginary. What is the argument, as opposed to the assertion?

    Why it belongs: The section lists the categories of Christian moral psychology — God, soul, spirit, free will, sin, redemption, grace — as an imaginary world of causes and effects, and reads the whole scheme as the product of a particular condition. It is symptomatic reading at its most compressed, and a good place to see how much of the late method is carried by classification and tone rather than by demonstration.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — The Four Great Errors; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: §15 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §57

    Christian moral law beside the Law of Manu

    Question: What work is the comparison doing, and does the evidence support it?

    Why it belongs: The most explicit comparative-anthropological moment in the late work: Nietzsche sets an order of castes against Christian morality and argues that a natural order of rank is being described rather than imposed. The passage endorses a hierarchy of human types in plain terms, and that endorsement is not incidental to the method here. As with the Twilight chapter, the historical basis is a mediated reading of the Manu material and does not support the use made of it.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols — The 'Improvers' of Mankind; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: §57 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

Notebook material

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Its entries can show what he was working out in private. They do not establish what the published works claim, and the published counterparts of the famous formulations below exist and are more careful.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. These are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different dates. Edition guidance.

The notebooks of the late 1880s contain concentrated methodological remarks, gathered by the compilation's editors mainly into Book Three. Four entries are given here, each identified individually rather than as a range. No short starting route is offered: the relevant material is a scattering of working notes about procedure rather than a developed notebook treatment of the method, and presenting seven entries in sequence would imply an organization the notes do not have.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §253

    Investigating a morality without being charmed by it

    Question: Is detachment from a morality a methodological requirement, or an achievement the method presupposes?

    Why it belongs: The entry describes the attempt to examine morality without falling under its spell and without assuming its evaluations. It states the stance the published method requires, and it states it as an attempt rather than an accomplishment, which is more cautious than the tone of the late books.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§5–6; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §466

    Method as the century's real achievement

    Question: Why would method rather than result be the thing worth claiming?

    Why it belongs: The entry that opens Book Three in the compilation's arrangement holds that the nineteenth century's distinction is the triumph of scientific method over science. It is the clearest statement of why Nietzsche thought the procedure mattered more than any particular finding, and it sits behind the demand for real history in the Genealogy Preface.

    Read alongside: Genealogy — Preface §§7–8.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §481

    A frequently quoted notebook line, and its published counterparts

    Question: Does the entry claim that no facts exist, or that facts are not given independently of interpretation?

    Why it belongs: This is the source of the formulation about there being no facts, only interpretations, written against positivism. It is frequently quoted in discussions of the subject, and it is an unpublished note. Its published counterparts — Beyond Good and Evil §22 and Genealogy III §12 — say something narrower and more defensible, and a reader who meets the slogan first will usually have to unlearn it. Treat the entry as a working formulation, not as a doctrine Nietzsche released.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §12; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §492

    Taking the body as the starting point

    Question: What does beginning from the body offer a genealogy that beginning from consciousness does not?

    Why it belongs: The entry proposes the body and physiology as the methodological starting point, on the grounds that they give a richer and more observable phenomenon than consciousness. It shows the naturalistic commitment underlying the published demand to translate the human being back into nature. The physiological confidence here runs well ahead of anything Nietzsche could have established, and the entry is a proposal rather than a result.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §230; Body and physiology.

Drafts and outlines for the projected Revaluation of All Values are sometimes cited as evidence of what the method was finally for. They show intentions, not a completed project, and the plans changed repeatedly. For the original notebook arrangement and manuscript references rather than the compilation's editorial numbering, consult Nietzsche Source's critical edition.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The method's sharpest internal tension. Read Daybreak §44 against Daybreak §95. When, if ever, does an account of a belief's origin make the belief superfluous?
  • What the programme promised. Compare The Gay Science §7 and the Note after Genealogy I §17 with what the late books actually deliver. Which of the proposed histories were written?
  • The reflexive turn. Follow Beyond Good and Evil §6 into §227 and then Genealogy III §§24–28. Does the method survive being applied to itself, and in what form?
  • Where the history is weakest. Read The 'Improvers' of Mankind beside The Antichrist §57. What would it take to assess these claims, and what remains of the comparison if the historical material does not hold?

A question to carry forward

Suppose a genealogy of one of your own convictions turned out to be accurate. What would have to be added to it before it counted as a reason to give the conviction up?
