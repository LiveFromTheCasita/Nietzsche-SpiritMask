Who gets to call something “good”? Master valuation begins by affirming itself and calls what differs from it “bad.” Slave valuation begins by condemning an enemy as “evil” and understands itself as “good” through that opposition.

Nietzsche connects these ways of valuing with ruling and subordinated groups, while allowing them to coexist within one person. His admiration for noble self-affirmation and his criticism of slave morality are part of the argument. His claims about aristocracy, Judaism, and Christianity require historical and philosophical scrutiny; describing types of valuation does not remove their social and political implications.

New to the theme? Start with the five sections below. For the sustained argument, continue to the fuller route. For a particular book, use the work-by-work survey.

Essential first encounter

Five numbered sections: a first orientation, not the whole case. Read them in order, then test their claims against the context supplied by the fuller route.

  1. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §260

    Two forms of moral valuation

    Part IX: “What Is Noble?”

    Question: Where does each form of valuation begin?

    Why it belongs: Sets out good/bad and good/evil, ties them to social relations, and allows their mixture within a person.

    Read alongside: §§257–259, 265, and 287 for the tensions within nobility.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §2

    Who first calls something good?

    Question: Does “good” begin with those who benefit from an action or those who name their own worth?

    Why it belongs: Introduces Nietzsche’s competing account of moral origins. The origin he proposes still needs evidence.

    Read alongside: I §§3–6 in the complete First Essay for the etymological argument.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §7

    Priestly rivalry and revaluation

    Question: How can a subordinated position become a source of moral authority?

    Why it belongs: Connects the inversion of values to priestly rivalry and Nietzsche’s polemical reconstruction of Jewish moral history.

    Read alongside: I §6 for priestly nobility; III §15 for a different priestly use of ressentiment.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §10

    When ressentiment creates values

    Question: How can opposition to an enemy become a source of values?

    Why it belongs: Develops the central contrast introduced in BGE §260. Noble people can also experience resentment; its duration and role in valuing matter.

    Read alongside: II §§10–11 on justice and revenge.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §13

    The doer, the deed, and blame

    Question: What does the image of birds of prey and lambs assume about responsibility?

    Why it belongs: Examines how strength and weakness become morally imputable. The argument does not automatically justify every exercise of power.

    Read alongside: I §§11–12 and 14–17 in the complete First Essay for violence, ideals, and revenge.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Full argument and its complications

Continue from BGE §260 through the complete First Essay, then the developments below. Together with the first encounter, this route covers 33 numbered sections: 28 additional sections, plus rereading the five already encountered. It includes distinct arguments about justice, guilt, priestly self-blame, and hierarchy.

  1. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §§1–17, including the closing Note

    The complete genealogy of good and evil

    Question: How well do the historical claims support the psychological distinction?

    Why it belongs: Restores the context around the first encounter: etymology, priestly revaluation, noble violence, the workshop of ideals, heavenly revenge, and the Rome–Judea opposition.

    Read alongside: I §15 and its optional Latin explanation.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  2. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§10–11

    Can strength restrain revenge?

    Question: When can power make leniency possible, and how does law limit vengeance?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects growing communal power with mercy and challenges an origin of justice in reactive feeling alone. His account also preserves the authority of stronger powers.

    Read alongside: Justice Without Revenge?, which distinguishes interpretation from a contemporary proposal.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  3. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§16–22

    Internalization, debt, and guilt

    Question: How does an imposed social order turn instincts inward, and how is debt moralized?

    Why it belongs: Develops bad conscience and its religious elaboration. This is a different explanatory task from the slave revolt in Essay I; §21 complicates the hope that unbelief alone dissolves guilt.

    Read alongside: III §15 for the priest’s subsequent redirection of blame.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  4. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals III §15

    Ressentiment redirected toward the sufferer

    Question: What changes when the sufferer is told to blame themselves?

    Why it belongs: The ascetic priest redirects the search for someone responsible for suffering into self-accusation. Distinguish this intervention from both I §10 and II’s account of internalization.

    Read alongside: III §§13–14 for the priest’s situation; the complete Genealogy course for all three essays.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259, 265, and 287

    Social hierarchy and self-reverence

    Question: Does inward nobility transform, supplement, or depend on aristocratic social relations?

    Why it belongs: Read all three of §§257–259: human elevation is connected with rank, sacrifice, and exploitation. §265 joins respect among equals to subordination; §287’s self-reverence does not simply cancel these commitments.

    Read alongside: Nobility After Aristocracy, an interpretation that confronts this tension.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Keep these distinctions in view

Master and slave morality
Contrasting ways of generating and ranking values, connected to unequal social relations. They can also mix within a person.
Herd morality
Valuation organized around the needs and preservation of a community. It overlaps with the later critique of slave morality but does not name precisely the same argument.
Ressentiment
A reactive relation to injury that can become enduring and value-creating. Feeling anger or objecting to harm does not by itself establish this diagnosis.
Bad conscience
In Essay II, instincts turn inward under the pressure of social confinement; the moralization of debt develops the account further.
The ascetic ideal
An interpretation that gives suffering meaning through self-denial. The ascetic priest’s redirection of ressentiment in III §15 is one mechanism within this wider inquiry.

DaybreakEarlier precursor

These selections question the authority, psychological origins, and truth claims of inherited morality. Nietzsche asks both what sustains a judgment and whether its assumptions are true.

  • Earlier precursor

    Daybreak §9

    The morality of custom

    Question: Why is obedience valued independently of what a custom demands?

    Why it belongs: Identifies the authority of tradition as a problem for the history of moral judgments.

    Online edition (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Earlier precursor

    Daybreak §103

    What does it mean to deny morality?

    Question: Are we doubting professed motives or the truth of moral judgments?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche adopts the second kind of denial without abandoning every practical distinction between actions to encourage and actions to resist.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Earlier precursor

    Daybreak §104

    How borrowed valuations become our own

    Question: How often do our judgments originate with us?

    Why it belongs: Fear and habituation make adopted evaluations feel like second nature; childhood judgments of other people shape whose authority we accept.

    Online edition (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Earlier precursor

    Daybreak §112

    Duty and right

    Question: How do duties and rights express relations of power?

  • Earlier precursor

    Daybreak §113

    The striving for distinction

    Question: Why might someone seek distinction even through self-denial?

The Gay ScienceDiagnosis and related extensions

The diagnostic readings concern power, communal valuation, and sin. The later readings ask about self-formation and affirmation; their relevance to this theme requires an interpretive bridge.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §13

    Benefiting, hurting, and power

    Question: How can assistance and injury each express power?

    Why it belongs: Examines dependence, generosity, risk, and differing temperaments without making help and harm identical in effect.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §§116–117

    Herd instinct and herd remorse

    Question: How does a community teach individuals what to value and regret?

    Why it belongs: Offers an account of social valuation. Herd morality should not simply be equated with the slave revolt of Genealogy I.

    Read alongside: §116 in the collection; continue to §117 in the online edition.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §135

    The origin of sin

    Question: What conception of divine injury gives sin its meaning?

    Why it belongs: Examines a religious form of valuation through Nietzsche’s claims about Jewish and Christian history, which remain claims to assess.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    The Gay Science §290

    Giving style to character

    Question: How might satisfaction with oneself interrupt revenge against others?

    Why it belongs: The ending explicitly links dissatisfaction to revenge, making self-formation a substantive connection. Nietzsche also allows resistance to a single imposed style.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    The Gay Science §335

    Self-examination and creating values

    Question: What do we really know about the origin of our moral judgments?

    Why it belongs: Joins criticism of moral certainty to a proposal for learning and self-creation. Read as a possible response to inherited valuation.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    The Gay Science §341

    The greatest weight

    Question: Could you affirm the return of this life, including its suffering?

    Why it belongs: The recurrence experiment can test a reader’s relation to their past. Its connection to overcoming ressentiment is an interpretation, not a definition of master morality.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke ZarathustraDramatic developments and extensions

Read these speeches as dramatic encounters. Zarathustra’s audience, vulnerability, and unfinished questions matter as much as his declarations.

  • Related extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Prologue §5

    The last man

    Question: Why does the crowd welcome the future Zarathustra finds diminished?

    Why it belongs: Comfort, conformity, and reduced aspiration suggest a connection to the theme. The passage does not itself identify the last man with slave morality.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, “On the Flies of the Marketplace”

    Public opinion and petty hostility

    Common’s title: “The Flies in the Market-Place.”

    Question: What does public recognition cost the person who seeks independence?

    Why it belongs: Connects reactive hostility with the marketplace and the pressures surrounding value creation.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part II, “On the Tarantulas”

    Revenge in the language of justice

    Common’s title: “The Tarantulas.”

    Question: How does the accusation of revenge turn back upon the speaker?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra attacks equality-preaching and explicitly defends inequality. After being bitten, he asks his friends to restrain his own revenge; the psychological diagnosis does not erase the political judgments.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part II, “On Self-Overcoming”

    Commanding, obeying, and transforming values

    Common’s title: “Self-Surpassing.”

    Question: How does the desire for mastery appear in servants as well as masters?

    Why it belongs: Complicates both sides of the moral distinction. It should not be treated as a settled metaphysical foundation exclusively for master morality.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part II, “On Redemption”

    The will and the unchangeable past

    Common’s title: “Redemption.”

    Question: What would it mean to overcome revenge against what has happened?

    Why it belongs: Extends revenge beyond relations with enemies to the will’s relation to time. The ending leaves the proposed redemption unsettled.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part III, “The Convalescent”

    Nausea and recurrence

    Question: Can affirmation include the return of what provokes disgust?

    Why it belongs: Deepens the problem of affirming existence. Attend to the difference between Zarathustra’s ordeal and his animals’ account of recurrence.

    Online edition (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and EvilCore and developments

§260 supplies the typology. The wider argument joins the psychology of valuation to explicit claims about aristocratic society, obedience, suffering, and unequal rank.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §260, Part IX: “What Is Noble?”

    Two forms of moral valuation

    Question: How do good/bad and good/evil differ?

    Why it belongs: The starting point of the essential route, including the possibility of mixed valuations.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §195

    The historical claim

    Question: What supports the proposed priestly inversion of values?

    Why it belongs: Anticipates Genealogy I through a polemical reconstruction of Jewish moral history. Its historical generalizations require scrutiny.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §199

    The inheritance of obedience

    Question: How does the habit of obeying shape those who command?

    Why it belongs: Examines the persistence of herd obedience and the need to justify command.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§201–202

    Fear and modern herd morality

    Question: Why do a community’s changing dangers alter its virtues?

    Why it belongs: Connects fear, moral ranking, and modern religious and political movements. These are developments of the inquiry, not interchangeable names for every moral type.

    Read alongside: §201 and §202 in the collection.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §225

    Suffering and human formation

    Question: What does Nietzsche think the wish to abolish suffering overlooks?

    Why it belongs: Challenges moralities centered on pity by attributing formative power to suffering. That evaluation needs argument, not an automatic endorsement of imposed suffering.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259

    Social hierarchy and human elevation

    Question: What social costs does Nietzsche associate with higher forms of life?

    Why it belongs: Links aristocratic rank with inward distance, the sacrifice of many to a few, and exploitation. Include §258 before turning to §287.

    Read alongside: §257 in the collection; read §§258–259 in the online edition.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §265

    Nobility, egoism, and unequal relations

    Question: How does the noble soul’s justice depend on rank?

    Why it belongs: Joins reciprocal respect among equals to the expectation of others’ subordination. This is more demanding than an endorsement of healthy self-respect.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §287

    Nobility as self-reverence

    Question: Does inward certainty alter or retain the dependence on social hierarchy?

    Why it belongs: Describes nobility through belief about oneself without decisively detaching it from the relations defended in §§257–259.

    Read alongside: §§257–259 and §265, above.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of MoralsCore and developments

The indispensable center of this theme. Essay I develops the distinction; Essays II and III introduce related but distinct mechanisms.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §§1–17

    Good/evil and good/bad

    Question: How does the argument move between language, history, and psychology?

    Why it belongs: Supplies the sustained case behind the selected core sections. Include noble violence and the closing questions, not only the account of ressentiment.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§10–11

    Mercy, law, and revenge

    Question: Can justice restrain reactive feeling rather than originate in it?

    Why it belongs: Complicates the equation of justice with vengeance and strength with relentless punishment.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§16–22

    Bad conscience and moralized debt

    Question: How do internalization and religious guilt become connected?

    Why it belongs: Develops a separate account that must not be collapsed into the slave revolt.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals III §15

    The priest’s redirection of blame

    Question: Why is the sufferer made responsible for their own suffering?

    Why it belongs: Bridges ressentiment, the interpretation of suffering, and self-accusation within the inquiry into the ascetic ideal.

    Read alongside: I §10; II §§16–18; III §§13–14.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the IdolsLate developments and an extension

These late arguments ask what kind of life speaks through a morality. Nietzsche’s physiological explanations and judgments about enhancement or decline remain claims to examine.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols “Morality as Anti-Nature” §§1–6

    Condemning and transforming the passions

    Ludovici’s title: “Morality as the Enemy of Nature.”

    Question: What distinguishes transforming a drive from trying to extirpate it?

    Why it belongs: Develops the criticism of moralities that condemn natural drives, then questions judgments of life from an imagined outside standpoint.

    Read alongside: §5 and §6 in the collection.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols “The ‘Improvers’ of Mankind” §§1–5

    What moral improvement does

    Question: Does “improvement” describe cultivation, domestication, or domination?

    Why it belongs: Examines the effects and means of moral reform. Its discussions of caste and breeding retain difficult hierarchical and historical commitments.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols “Skirmishes” §§37–38

    Modern morality and freedom

    Ludovici’s title: “Skirmishes in a War with the Age.”

    Question: What does freedom demand in Nietzsche’s polemic against modern ideals?

    Why it belongs: Extends questions of moral inheritance, struggle, and rank rather than supplying a neutral account of liberty.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    Twilight of the Idols “What I Owe to the Ancients” §§4–5

    Dionysian affirmation and suffering

    Ludovici’s title: “Things I Owe to the Ancients.”

    Question: Can affirmation include becoming, pain, and destruction?

    Why it belongs: Interprets Dionysian religion and tragedy through procreation and the affirmation of becoming. This is a related response to life-denying valuation, not an account of Roman Stoicism or another definition of the moral typology.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

A late polemical development. Each selection contributes a different claim; distinguish the diagnosis of pity, religious interpretations of reality, priestly revaluation, and spiritual equality.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §2

    Goodness, power, and the higher type

    Question: What ethical commitments follow from the proposed measure of power?

    Why it belongs: States a forceful late valuation and includes prescriptions whose implications should not disappear behind a general language of strength.

    The higher type, decadence, and pity

    Question: How does the attack on pity depend on Nietzsche’s conception of flourishing?

Ecce HomoRetrospective developments and extensions

A retrospective self-presentation to examine. Nietzsche portrays his responses to illness, resentment, and his own work; the text does not independently establish that he achieved freedom from ressentiment.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo “Why I Am So Wise” §6

    Nietzsche’s practice against resentment

    Question: How does his response change between exhaustion and recovered strength?

    Why it belongs: Makes the danger of ressentiment personal and complicates an exclusively outward accusation against moral opponents.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Related extension

    Ecce Homo “Why I Am So Clever” §§9–10

    Becoming oneself and amor fati

    Question: What would it mean to affirm necessity without wishing the past otherwise?

    Why it belongs: Offers an autobiographical perspective on self-formation and affirmation. Its relation to overcoming ressentiment is a connection to explore.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Ecce Homo The section on “On the Genealogy of Morals”

    Nietzsche rereads his argument

    Question: What does this retrospective summary emphasize or leave out?

    Why it belongs: Lets readers compare Nietzsche’s later account of the three essays with the arguments they have read.

    Online edition (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

    Ecce Homo “Why I Am a Destiny” §§7–8

A question to carry forward

How do we distinguish a justified objection to mistreatment from a valuation organized around revenge—and who has the authority to make that distinction?

Test the diagnosis against evidence and against the interpreter’s own motives. An objection to harm cannot be dismissed merely by calling its speaker resentful.
