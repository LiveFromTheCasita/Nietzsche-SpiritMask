Who gets to call something “good”? Master valuation begins by affirming itself and calls what differs from it “bad.” Slave valuation begins by condemning an enemy as “evil” and understands itself as “good” through that opposition.

Nietzsche connects these ways of valuing with ruling and subordinated groups, while allowing them to coexist within one person. His admiration for noble self-affirmation and his criticism of slave morality are part of the argument. His claims about aristocracy, Judaism, and Christianity require historical and philosophical scrutiny; describing types of valuation does not remove their social and political implications.

New to the theme? Start with the five sections below. For the sustained argument, continue to the fuller route. For a particular book, use the work-by-work survey. For the posthumous notes, explore The Will to Power reading guide.

Essential first encounter

Five numbered sections: a first orientation, not the whole case. Read them in order, then test their claims against the context supplied by the fuller route.

  1. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §260

    Two forms of moral valuation

    Part IX: “What Is Noble?”

    Question: Where does each form of valuation begin?

    Why it belongs: Sets out good/bad and good/evil, ties them to social relations, and allows their mixture within a person.

    Read alongside: §§257–259, 265, and 287 for the tensions within nobility.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §2

    Who first calls something good?

    Question: Does “good” begin with those who benefit from an action or those who name their own worth?

    Why it belongs: Introduces Nietzsche’s competing account of moral origins. The origin he proposes still needs evidence.

    Read alongside: I §§3–6 in the complete First Essay for the etymological argument.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §7

    Priestly rivalry and revaluation

    Question: How can a subordinated position become a source of moral authority?

    Why it belongs: Connects the inversion of values to priestly rivalry and Nietzsche’s polemical reconstruction of Jewish moral history.

    Read alongside: I §6 for priestly nobility; III §15 for a different priestly use of ressentiment.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §10

    When ressentiment creates values

    Question: How can opposition to an enemy become a source of values?

    Why it belongs: Develops the central contrast introduced in BGE §260. Noble people can also experience resentment; its duration and role in valuing matter.

    Read alongside: II §§10–11 on justice and revenge.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §13

    The doer, the deed, and blame

    Question: What does the image of birds of prey and lambs assume about responsibility?

    Why it belongs: Examines how strength and weakness become morally imputable. The argument does not automatically justify every exercise of power.

    Read alongside: I §§11–12 and 14–17 in the complete First Essay for violence, ideals, and revenge.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Full argument and its complications

Continue from BGE §260 through the complete First Essay, then the developments below. Together with the first encounter, this route covers 33 numbered sections: 28 additional sections, plus rereading the five already encountered. It includes distinct arguments about justice, guilt, priestly self-blame, and hierarchy.

  1. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals I §§1–17, including the closing Note

    The complete First Essay

    Question: How well do the historical claims support the psychological distinction?

    Why it belongs: Restores the context around the first encounter: etymology, priestly revaluation, noble violence, the workshop of ideals, heavenly revenge, and the Rome–Judea opposition.

    Read alongside: I §15 and its optional Latin explanation.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  2. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§10–11

    Can strength restrain revenge?

    Question: When can power make leniency possible, and how does law limit vengeance?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche connects growing communal power with mercy and challenges an origin of justice in reactive feeling alone. His account also preserves the authority of stronger powers.

    Read alongside: Justice Without Revenge?, which distinguishes interpretation from a contemporary proposal.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  3. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals II §§16–22

    Internalization, debt, and guilt

    Question: How does an imposed social order turn instincts inward, and how is debt moralized?

    Why it belongs: Develops bad conscience and its religious elaboration. This is a different explanatory task from the slave revolt in Essay I; §21 complicates the hope that unbelief alone dissolves guilt.

    Read alongside: III §15 for the priest’s subsequent redirection of blame.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  4. Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals III §15

    Ressentiment redirected toward the sufferer

    Question: What changes when the sufferer is told to blame themselves?

    Why it belongs: The ascetic priest redirects the search for someone responsible for suffering into self-accusation. Distinguish this intervention from both I §10 and II’s account of internalization.

    Read alongside: III §§13–14 for the priest’s situation; the complete Genealogy course for all three essays.

    Online edition (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259, 265, and 287

    Social hierarchy and self-reverence

    Question: Does inward nobility transform, supplement, or depend on aristocratic social relations?

    Why it belongs: Read all three of §§257–259: human elevation is connected with rank, sacrifice, and exploitation. §265 joins respect among equals to subordination; §287’s self-reverence does not simply cancel these commitments.

    Read alongside: Nobility After Aristocracy, an interpretation that confronts this tension.

    Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Keep these distinctions in view

Master and slave morality
Contrasting ways of generating and ranking values, connected to unequal social relations. They can also mix within a person.
Herd morality
Valuation organized around the needs and preservation of a community. It overlaps with the later critique of slave morality but does not name precisely the same argument.
Ressentiment
A reactive relation to injury that can become enduring and value-creating. Feeling anger or objecting to harm does not by itself establish this diagnosis.
Bad conscience
In Essay II, instincts turn inward under the pressure of social confinement; the moralization of debt develops the account further.
The ascetic ideal
An interpretation that gives suffering meaning through self-denial. The ascetic priest’s redirection of ressentiment in III §15 is one mechanism within this wider inquiry.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §287

    Nobility as self-reverence

    Question: Does inward certainty alter or retain the dependence on social hierarchy?

    Read alongside: §§257–259 and §265, above.

    Read this selection in the collection · Online edition (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Moral values as forms of power6 assignments · 14 entries

Ask where valuations come from, what they do, and whose lives they preserve.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §4, §55

    Morality preserves life—and exercises power

    Question: What does morality give the oppressed, and what changes if their moral judgments also express will to power?

    Why it belongs: These notes credit Christian morality with giving people worth and protecting them against despair. Section 55 then asks whether moral opposition to domination itself expresses will to power, making preservation and valuation part of the same difficult inquiry.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§10–13; Beyond Good and Evil §260.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §254, §256, §258, §259

    From the origin of values to their value

    Question: Does uncovering a morality’s origin settle whether it is valuable?

    Why it belongs: §254 explicitly separates explaining an origin from evaluating its consequences: for whom, and for what form of life, does a morality work? §§256, 258, and 259 develop valuations as interpretations tied to conditions of life, including conflicting valuations within one person. This supplies a method for assessing the master/slave distinction without treating an unflattering genealogy as sufficient refutation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy, Preface §§3–6; Beyond Good and Evil §260.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §266

    Morality’s costs—and its protective work

    Question: What can morality preserve even when Nietzsche criticizes it?

    Why it belongs: This outline lists both morality’s damage to life and its benefits: social cohesion, restraint of dangerous passions, relief from bitterness, and protection of the lowly against the mighty. Its explicit counter-account complicates any reading in which slave or herd values have no positive function.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§6–7; III §13.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §274, §400, §401

    Whose will to power is morality?

    Question: Which different interests can converge in a moral ideal?

    Why it belongs: §274 distinguishes herd resistance to independence, suffering people’s opposition to the fortunate, and mediocrity’s opposition to exceptions; §400 restates their struggle to make their values prevail. §401 then asks whether the victory of the weak might preserve life and whether a world without their subtlety and consideration would be desirable. These are overlapping diagnoses and self-questioning notes, with speculative biological assumptions, rather than one settled equation of weakness with worthlessness.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§7–10; Beyond Good and Evil §§199–202.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §720, §721

    Powerlessness and its moral disguises

    Question: When does renouncing power become an indirect way of seeking it?

    Why it belongs: These notes connect moral education with the restraint of individual ambition, then examine obedience, duty, resignation, asceticism, and indignation as possible compensations for powerlessness. They extend the analysis of reactive valuation while leaving a difficult question open: how could we distinguish these motives from forms of restraint that express strength?

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook extension · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1009, §1026

    Who judges the strength of a valuation?

    Question: How can we distinguish spontaneous abundance from reaction when powerful people name their own condition virtue?

    Why it belongs: Read together, the questions about abundance and reaction meet a case where powerful people call their own happiness virtuous and impose new ideals on others. The discussion of Napoleon also allows that the means of prevailing can corrupt character, keeping worldly success from settling the inquiry. This further test of valuation asks the reader to compare motives, interpretations, and effects.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §§2, 10–11.

Herd values, obedience, and exceptions8 assignments · 18 entries

Distinguish communal usefulness from the authority to impose one standard on everyone.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §132, §134

    Different moralities for rulers and the herd

    Question: Who is permitted to move beyond herd morality, and who is expected to continue obeying it?

    Why it belongs: These programmatic notes connect moral valuations to competition for power and propose preserving herd morality for those expected to obey. They expose the unequal distribution of moral freedom in Nietzsche’s projected order, while leaving its historical and evaluative claims to be argued for.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §§7–10.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §275, §276, §277, §278

    The authority of the herd

    Question: Who speaks through the moral demand to be dependable and dutiful?

    Why it belongs: §§275–276 locate commanding moral authority in the herd and describe the resulting suspicion of exceptional qualities. §§277–278 connect truthfulness and trust with the community’s need to make individuals recognizable and predictable, while distinguishing relations among equals from those beyond the group.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§199–202; Genealogy II §§1–3.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §280, §281, §284, §285

    A community’s virtues and its exceptions

    Question: Why can a group praise conduct abroad that it condemns at home?

    Why it belongs: These notes connect herd values with security and the preservation of an average type. They distinguish restraint within the group from aggression outside it, and examine how communities fear or recruit exceptional individuals. Communal virtue therefore does not imply an absence of domination or cruelty.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §11; Beyond Good and Evil §§201, 260.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §286, §287

    Different moralities for different ranks

    Question: Is Nietzsche proposing one morality of individualism for everyone?

    Why it belongs: §286 identifies the demand for unselfishness with herd formation. §287 explicitly rejects an individualistic morality as the description of Nietzsche’s project: it proposes a new order of rank, allowing herd morality within the herd while assigning different valuations to leaders and independent people. The inequality is part of the argument.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257, 260; Genealogy I §17.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §685, §766

    When the herd prevails

    Question: Does prevailing over others prove the superiority of a person or a set of values?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that organized numbers can defeat exceptional individuals and disputes making the collective the highest end of human development. This separates historical success from his valuation of strength, but section 685's speculative and often untenable biological claims should not be treated as established evolutionary science.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §717

    Obedience and organized aggression

    Question: How can dutiful obedience enable conduct an individual would hesitate to undertake alone?

    Why it belongs: This note examines how the state distributes responsibility through command, execution, duty, and patriotic attachment. It extends the theme into institutions: obedient conduct can participate in collective aggression, so submission and the exercise of power are not straightforward opposites.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy II §11.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §726, §730

    Whose survival does morality serve?

    Question: What happens to exceptional individuals when moral obligations preserve a community across generations?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche considers how communities sanctify useful dispositions, give exceptions a bad conscience, and bind successive generations to enduring institutions. The change of perspective between those who command and those who sacrifice reveals why the same morality can receive sharply different valuations.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §2.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §901

    Different virtues and an unequal foundation

    Question: Can virtues be appropriate to different forms of life without making some people mere means to others?

    Why it belongs: Traits suited to a higher type may ruin an average person, while industry, regularity, and moderation can perfect the latter’s way of life. Nietzsche joins this plurality of virtues to an explicitly unequal social picture in which the lives of many provide the foundation for the higher type.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§258, 260.

Priestly authority and the victory of ideals5 assignments · 13 entries

Compare the mechanisms through which religious and moral claims acquire power.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §139, §140

    How priests acquire the authority to value

    Question: How can a type without direct military power claim the highest authority?

    Why it belongs: These notes examine the priest’s claim to privileged knowledge, divine mediation, and superior virtue, and the philosopher’s inheritance of that authority. Their accounts of caste and religion are polemical historical constructions; use them to examine the proposed mechanism of rule rather than as established comparative history.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§6–7; Beyond Good and Evil §6.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §174, §179

    Two notebook accounts of Christian resentment

    Question: Is resentment presented as Christianity’s origin or as a later transformation?

    Why it belongs: §174 describes an initially non-resentful Christian life altered by persecution; §179 instead emphasizes resentment and revolt as driving forces concealed beneath professions of peace. Read these accounts against each other. Their difference matters when comparing the notebooks with the published genealogy of the slave revolt.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§7–8; The Antichrist §§33–42.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §215, §216

    How the same morality serves different interests

    Question: Why might both subjects and rulers value obedience?

    Why it belongs: §215 traces Christian herd values into modern democratic ideals, distinguishing the interests of the oppressed, the mediocre, and the sick. §216 asks why rulers adopt values that make subjects governable, while admiring exceptional strength in religious figures. The same outward virtue can serve different motives and positions of power.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§199–202; Genealogy I §16.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §282, §283

    Herd life and priestly guilt are distinct

    Question: Is belonging to a herd already a sign of sickness?

    Why it belongs: §282 calls the herd valuable and not inherently morbid, then distinguishes it from the priest’s infection of conscience. §283 turns to hostility toward the fortunate and the priest’s use of that hostility. Read the distinction carefully while questioning the notes’ sweeping diagnoses of illness and human worth.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§13–15.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §304, §305, §306, §310, §311

    The political work of making virtue supreme

    Question: By what means does a moral ideal acquire authority?

    Why it belongs: These notes describe virtue’s public success through force, concealment, historical legitimation, promises, and attacks on rival ideals. The comparison with a political party exposes a tension between a doctrine’s moral demands and the means used to establish it. Keep §254’s distinction in view: exposing those means does not by itself settle the ideal’s value.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§7–9; Beyond Good and Evil §186.

Ressentiment, justice, and bad conscience9 assignments · 17 entries

Keep reactive valuation, punishment, and the inward turn of aggression in view as distinct problems.

  • Notebook reading · Book I

    The Will to Power §100

    When revenge calls itself justice

    Question: What evidence could distinguish a response to injustice from a wish to condemn those who possess power?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche portrays Rousseau’s moral and political ideals as an elevation of resentment into justice. Read this as a polemical diagnosis whose claims need examination: the presence of anger does not by itself establish that an argument about injustice is false.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §11; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §255, §375

    Justice, revenge, and the interpretation of drives

    Question: Can the same drives receive opposite moral names?

    Why it belongs: §255 calls justice a development of revenge, while §375 connects both praised and condemned drives with will to power. The first claim stands in tension with Genealogy II §11, which expressly resists deriving justice from reactive feeling. Comparing the texts is more illuminating than treating the notebook claim as the published account’s missing conclusion.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§10–11; Beyond Good and Evil §23.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §294, §295, §296

    Conscience, self-accusation, and a reversal of guilt

    Question: Does a feeling of guilt establish that a person has done wrong?

    Why it belongs: §294 treats conscience as the after-effect of inherited judgments, not an independent proof of moral value. §§295–296 connect moralized suffering with self-torment and explore turning bad conscience against ideals hostile to earthly life. This is a provocative redirection of guilt, not simply its disappearance.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–22; III §15.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §348, §350

    An ideal that begins by opposing an enemy

    Question: Does affirmation or negation supply an ideal’s first impulse?

    Why it belongs: §348 describes combatants imagining their opponents as their exact moral opposite and reserving goodness for their own side. §350 identifies hatred and contempt as the initiating feelings of ideals born from resentment. Together they sharpen the distinction between reactive valuation and a merely forceful disagreement.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§10–11.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §376

    When blocked aggression turns inward

    Question: Where do cruel and vengeful impulses go when outward action is restricted?

    Why it belongs: The note describes drives redirected into the self, knowledge, and art under conditions of social peace. It helps distinguish the internalization involved in bad conscience from the creation of good/evil values in the slave revolt, while leaving room to investigate their connections.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §738, §740, §741, §742

    Punishment, shame, and the possibility of return

    Question: Can punishment protect a community without teaching contempt for the person punished?

    Why it belongs: These notes connect fearful prohibition with guilt, investigate punishment's association with despised social groups, and contrast reconciliation with continued exclusion. Section 740 rejects punitive contempt while also proposing preventive castration of a supposed criminal type; this disturbing contradiction must remain visible rather than presenting the passage as straightforward humane reform.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §11; Genealogy III §15.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §765

    Revenge, responsibility, and innocence

    Question: Can we confront suffering and injustice without needing someone to be ultimately guilty?

    Why it belongs: This substantial note connects resentful blame, scapegoating, free will, and punitive responsibility before proposing the recovery of innocence in becoming. Its sweeping treatment of social grievance needs scrutiny: the question is whether Nietzsche distinguishes revenge from justified criticism adequately. His list of scapegoats includes Jews among the targets people blame; it does not endorse blaming them.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §10; Genealogy II §11; Genealogy III §15.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §775

    The power to praise and repay

    Question: How can gratitude affirm another person while also asserting our own standing?

    Why it belongs: Praise claims the authority to judge and confer honor, while gratitude can repay a benefit and restore a proud sense of equality. The note brings reciprocity into the theme and asks us to distinguish forms of repayment from the enduring hostility characteristic of ressentiment.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10; Genealogy II §8.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §925, §926

    Reciprocity, equality, and justice

    Question: What must be true about two people before their actions can count as equivalent?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche challenges the Golden Rule and reciprocal exchange by questioning whether different individuals can perform equivalent actions. His restriction of reciprocity to genuine equals makes these notes a direct test of the relation between justice and rank; the example of vendetta also unsettles morality based simply on avoiding harmful returns.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §11; Beyond Good and Evil §260.

Selflessness, pity, and noble generosity8 assignments · 21 entries

Test whether the same outward virtue can express dependence, abundance, or the wish to rule.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §351, §352, §353, §354

    The ideal of an exclusively good person

    Question: What is lost when benevolence is isolated from the capacity to resist or harm?

    Why it belongs: These notes question splitting human powers into wholly good and wholly evil halves and making one useful type into a universal ideal. Nietzsche links helping and harming to capacities that can serve different ends. His resistance to uniform standards also contains an explicit attack on equal rights; it should not be recast as a straightforward liberal defense of diversity.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §13.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §356, §358, §361

    Selflessness, obedience, and competing ideals

    Question: What distinguishes choosing one’s ends from being useful to someone else’s?

    Why it belongs: §§356 and 358 connect prescribed modesty and unselfishness with the formation of people who serve purposes they do not set. §361 opposes the exclusive rule of the Christian ideal while retaining it as a strong rival to other ideals. The alternative is a contest over authority, not an impartial acceptance of every way of life.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§199, 260.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §362, §364, §365, §388

    Which self gives—and from what abundance?

    Question: Can love and generosity express a strong sense of self?

    Why it belongs: §§362 and 364 question the automatic discrediting of egoism and ask which self is at stake; §365 resists reducing complex actions to a simple contrast between self and other. §388 treats love, pity, justice, and generosity as possible expressions of personal richness, while retaining claims to mastery. Helping others is not automatically classified as slave morality.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§260, 265; Genealogy II §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §366, §367, §368

    The dispute over pity

    Question: Whose suffering or frustrated possibility receives compassion?

    Why it belongs: §366 rejects Schopenhauer’s reduction of morality to pity. §367 redirects concern toward wasted higher possibilities, while §368 interprets pity as potentially spreading distress or relieving the spectator’s discomfort. These are contested diagnoses of motives and values, best tested alongside the notes that allow compassion to express strength.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §225; Genealogy, Preface §§5–6.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §774, §776

    Freedom, justice, and love as forms of power

    Question: Can similar acts of service or compassion express different relationships to power?

    Why it belongs: These notes examine independence, useful subordination, conscience, justice, love, and sacrifice as different expressions of the desire for power. Section 776 associates love and pity with the strongest type as well as discussing the demands of the oppressed, so outwardly compassionate behavior cannot simply be assigned to slave morality.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §785, §786

    How selflessness becomes an ideal

    Question: What must we believe about ourselves before self-denial appears to be the measure of goodness?

    Why it belongs: These notes connect dependence on others for self-worth with a longer account of how egoism and selflessness became moral opposites. Section 786 brings together herd valuation, priestly ideals, self-contempt, and the rehabilitation of condemned drives; its argument challenges the categories through which motives are judged, rather than supplying a simple instruction to act selfishly.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §13; Genealogy III §15.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §935, §939

    Generosity and hospitality from abundance

    Question: How does giving change when it neither seeks repayment nor establishes the giver’s moral superiority?

    Why it belongs: Genuine goodness appears here as expenditure from inner wealth, and noble hospitality can welcome beggar, cripple, and king. The pair gives the theme a positive account of generosity while preserving the giver’s distance and the unequal social world in which the gesture occurs.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook extension · Book IV

    The Will to Power §983

    Commanding strength and the capacity for pity

    Question: What could Nietzsche mean by the composite ideal of a Roman Caesar with Christ’s soul?

    Why it belongs: This brief note asks for virtues that make a person master of benevolence and pity, then combines commanding power with Christ’s soul. As a further question beyond the basic distinction, it tests whether familiar virtues can be reorganized within a different structure of character.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

Self-command and the formation of strength6 assignments · 9 entries

Explore how drives are organized and why visible dominance does not settle the question of strength.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §345

    When rare strength looks like weakness

    Question: Can refinement make a strong person appear defenseless?

    Why it belongs: The note attacks attempts to weaken exceptional people, but also observes that highly refined forms may relinquish coarse weapons and resemble weakness. This complicates any test of noble strength based only on visible dominance or immediate success.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §287; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §383, §384, §385

    Organizing passions instead of eradicating them

    Question: Is self-command the destruction of powerful drives or the capacity to employ them?

    Why it belongs: These entries contrast the extirpation of passions with learning to master and use them, and interpret intolerance as fear of what one cannot control. The argument concerns organized strength rather than indiscriminate indulgence. §384 also considers this discipline at the level of communities and peoples, so its scope exceeds personal self-command.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols, Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3; Beyond Good and Evil §260.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §773

    The collective beginnings of noble self-esteem

    Question: Could belonging to a proud community prepare someone for personal independence?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche explores how collective pride and responsibility for a whole can train individuals in self-respect, foresight, and command. This complicates any simple identification of noble morality with solitary individualism: personal sovereignty can inherit capacities first developed through membership and service.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §2.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §778

    Commanding rather than fearing the passions

    Question: How does organizing one's passions differ from either suppressing or indulging them?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes fear of irresistible impulses from the ability to coordinate passions around a dominant task. His account of inner conflict and organization gives the theme a more precise conception of self-command: strength requires a relationship among drives, rather than permission to act on every impulse.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy II §2.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §870, §874

    When strong people condemn their own strengths

    Question: How can commanding people become ashamed of the qualities through which they act and create?

    Why it belongs: Section 870 distinguishes the pleasurable restraint of powerful passions from moderation imposed by weakness, and describes creative people adopting a condemning moral conscience. Section 874 connects the victory of slave values to the degeneration of rulers and their adoption of those values, complicating any direct equation between political office and noble valuation.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §§10–13.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §966

    An order of rank within the person

    Question: Does strength require fewer conflicting drives, or a greater capacity to organize them?

    Why it belongs: Moralities appear as local hierarchies among competing drives, while the strongest person can organize a greater multiplicity of impulses. This extends the theme into the formation of character and invites comparison with the social orders discussed in the other notes.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy II §2.

Rank, social hierarchy, and their costs7 assignments · 14 entries

Read psychological distinctions together with Nietzsche’s proposals about social order and exclusion.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §397, §398

    Taming and the cultivation of a type

    Question: What does moral improvement produce, and by whose measure?

    Why it belongs: §397 distinguishes making people less dangerous from making them stronger, describing priestly improvement as taming. §398 then imagines the cultivation of a type across generations. The language of heredity and breeding gives this argument a social and political scope beyond personal development.

    Read alongside: Twilight of the Idols, The Improvers of Mankind §§1–5; Beyond Good and Evil §§257–258.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §758, §764

    Labor, command, and unequal moral demands

    Question: How far does Nietzsche's distinction between moralities depend on an unequal social order?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche criticizes modern servitude, questions the glorification of work, and imagines different requirements for a ruling caste and those who obey. These notes preserve the social and political stakes of the theme, including explicit coercion, and prevent its reduction to a contrast between personality styles.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §257; Beyond Good and Evil §260.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §783, §784

    Equality, recognition, and emerging ranks

    Question: Does the demand for equal standing end struggles for distinction, or change their form?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche investigates the coexistence of modern individualism and equal rights, then describes emancipation and equality giving way to new associations and hierarchies. These are contentious interpretations of equality's motives, useful for examining whether demands for recognition express dependence, independence, or both.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §257; Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §859, §957

    The political cost of cultivating a higher type

    Question: What happens when the cultivation of exceptional people is made a justification for other people’s subordination?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche asks whether restrictions of freedom, even enslavement, could support a higher type, and sketches a future ruling caste opposed to the aims of herd morality. These proposals give rank a concrete political meaning and require readers to confront whose lives bear the cost of the projected achievement.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257–259; Genealogy I §11.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §886, §887

    Moral types do not simply follow social class

    Question: How should we read the claim that exceptional people may emerge among the lowest social ranks?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche gives solitary and gregarious types different standards and says both are necessary, while insisting that the distance between them should increase. He also locates exceptional personalities in the lowest ranks and allows that democratic order can help their development, creating tensions within a straightforward political reading of his hierarchy.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §260; Genealogy I §10.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §936, §951

    Aristocratic freedom and the demand for hardness

    Question: Can the generosity of a noble type be reconciled with Nietzsche’s insistence on domination and brutality?

    Why it belongs: Section 936 defends aristocratic freedom through differences of rank, conflict, and the will to dominate. Section 951 insists that nobility also includes hardness and a proximity to brutality, so these notes belong beside the passages on generosity as a tension the reader must examine.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257–260; Genealogy I §11.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §942, §943

    Inheritance, nobility, and duties to equals

    Question: Which features of this noble ideal depend on disciplined character, and which depend on inherited or social privilege?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche ties nobility to inheritance and includes an anti-Jewish stereotype in §942; §943 combines self-control, masks, and courtesy with inherited rank and obligations restricted to equals. Read the psychological and social claims together, including the claim that justice applies among equals, to assess the exclusions built into this ideal.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §§257, 260; Genealogy I §§2, 10–11.

Further questions: innocence and affirmation2 assignments · 3 entries

These closing extensions concern the interpretation of existence and suffering, beyond the basic master/slave distinction.

  • Notebook extension · Book III

    The Will to Power §788, §789

    A good conscience beyond cosmic judgment

    Question: What would it mean to recover self-respect without treating moral judgments as laws of the universe?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche asks whether his project restores a good conscience to the strong person condemned as evil, then rejects an intrinsic cosmic basis for reward, punishment, good, and evil. The pairing returns moral judgments to the lives and interests of particular human types without itself establishing that every action by a strong person is justified.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §13; Genealogy II §11.

  • Notebook extension · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1052

    Two meanings of suffering

    Question: What changes when suffering becomes an objection to life, or something an affirmation of life must include?

    Why it belongs: The figures of Dionysus and the Crucified give suffering opposing meanings: condemnation of life and affirmation through destruction and renewal. Use this as a closing extension of the struggle over interpretation, with the distinction between master and slave morality still to be examined on its own terms.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §10; Genealogy III §28.

A question to carry forward

How do we distinguish a justified objection to mistreatment from a valuation organized around revenge—and who has the authority to make that distinction?

Test the diagnosis against evidence and against the interpreter’s own motives. An objection to harm cannot be dismissed merely by calling its speaker resentful.
