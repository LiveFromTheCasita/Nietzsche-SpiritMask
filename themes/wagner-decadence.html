In sixteen years Nietzsche published a book dedicated to Wagner, an essay in praise of him, a book that takes apart the theory of the artist he had praised him with, a preface narrating a separation, and two short books against him. The order is the argument. This page is built to keep it.

The convenient version runs: admiration, betrayal, hatred. Nietzsche supplies a version of it himself in 1888, which is part of why it is convenient. But it compresses documents written across sixteen years, in at least four different modes, into a single emotional arc — and once compressed, the interesting questions disappear. Which judgments actually changed? Which were revised in print and which were quietly dropped? When is he arguing, and when is he telling the story of having argued? Four things Nietzsche is doing in these texts sets out the distinction the rest of the page relies on.

Two limits are worth stating at the outset. This is not a page about Wagner's music, which Nietzsche's polemics describe but do not assess in any way a musician would recognise. And it is not a page about either man's psychology. The texts support claims about what Nietzsche wrote and when; they do not license conclusions about what he privately felt in 1876, or about what Wagner intended. Claims of that kind appear here only where Nietzsche is making them, in a polemic, and they are marked as his.

The second half of the title needs its own warning. Decadence is not a word from the early writing; it arrives as a term of art in the books of 1888, and it carries a physiological claim, an aesthetic claim and a cultural diagnosis at once without ever quite separating them. What "decadence" is doing takes it up on its own, including the places where the site does not follow Nietzsche.

Begin with seven readings. One more than the usual six, because the sequence has seven stations and dropping any of them produces the flattened story this page exists to avoid. The first encounter is strictly chronological. Continue through the fuller route, or use the work survey for a particular book. Two sections do specific work: four things Nietzsche is doing and what "decadence" is doing.

Use the preferred translation where possible. Each assignment gives a precise section or chapter and links to a named online translation. Essential first encounter

Seven assignments in date order: 1872, 1876, 1886, 1887, 1887, 1888, 1888. Read them in that order even if you already know how it ends. At each step, note what Nietzsche is claiming, whom he is claiming it to, and whether he is arguing or recounting.

  1. Core · 1872

    The Birth of Tragedy Foreword to Richard Wagner; §§16–19

    What was promised, in print, while it was still current

    Free edition trans. William A. Haussmann. The second link opens §16; §§17–19 follow. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: The closing chapters argue that a living art form will produce a cultural transformation. What would have counted as the argument failing?

    Why it belongs: Start with the promise rather than the retraction, because the retraction is unreadable without it. The dedication calls art the highest task and the truly metaphysical activity of this life, and addresses that claim to a living composer with a project under way. §§16–19 then argue that German music — Wagner's above all — is reviving the tragic capacity the book has spent fifteen chapters describing as lost. Read it as what it is: advocacy, published while the collaboration was current, for something that had not yet happened. Notice how little of it is argument in the sense the earlier chapters are. The material on the Apollonian and Dionysian belongs to that page, and the aesthetic formula to tragedy and aesthetic justification; here the question is only what is being predicted and on what basis.

    Read alongside: "Attempt at a Self-Criticism" §§6–7; Untimely Meditations IV.

    Online edition: Foreword to Richard Wagner · §16 (trans. William A. Haussmann; §§17–19 follow) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core · 1876

    Untimely Meditations IV, "Richard Wagner in Bayreuth" §§1, 4–5, 9

    The festival essay, written for the occasion

    Free edition: Thoughts Out of Season, Part I, trans. Anthony M. Ludovici, which contains the Strauss and Wagner essays. The Collins volume (Part II) contains the other two. Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: §1 makes the audience part of the problem. Does the rest of the essay take its own warning seriously?

    Why it belongs: The last of the four meditations, published in the year of the first Bayreuth festival. It is the most extended thing Nietzsche wrote in Wagner's favour, and it is more interesting than its reputation. §1 argues that an achievement becomes culturally consequential only through the quality of its reception, and turns that immediately on Bayreuth's own public: can these people receive this? §4 claims that tragic art can renew the capacity to face struggle and suffering, and explicitly denies that it supplies instructions for conduct. §5 is on how conventional words lose contact with what people actually need to say, and on music as a recovery of expressive contact. §9 describes how myth thinks — through events, deeds and suffering rather than through an idea in decorative dress. Read the four together and ask how much of the essay is praise of Wagner and how much is a set of demands that Wagner is being asked to meet.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations III §6; Human, All Too Human, 1886 Preface.

    Online edition: "Richard Wagner in Bayreuth" §1 · §4 · §5 · §9 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  3. Development · 1886

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, 1886 Preface

    The separation, narrated ten years later

    Written in 1886 for a book first published in 1878. Free edition trans. Helen Zimmern; the three section links open the printed pages for §§3, 4 and 6. Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: The preface never names Wagner. Does anything in it require him?

    Why it belongs: The most quoted account of the break, and it is worth noticing what it actually does. Nietzsche describes a separation from revered duties and attachments that arrives with excitement, aggression and disorientation, and warns that inverting every valuation can become compulsive in its turn — so that the rebellion is a stage with its own dangers, not the achievement of freedom. §4 is on convalescence: the freedom worth having is the one that can meet an opposed way of thinking without being captured by it. §6 asks that convictions become capacities one commands rather than masters one serves. Read it as retrospective narration rather than as evidence. It is written by the person the story happened to, fourteen years after the dedication and two years before the polemics, and it is shaped by what he then wanted the episode to mean. The free-spirit material is treated at length on free spirits.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human I §§162–163, 222; Ecce Homo.

    Online edition: Volume I, 1886 Preface · §3 · §4 · §6 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core · 1887

    The Gay Science §370 · Book V

    The distinction the whole later attack runs on

    Book V was added in 1887 to a book first published in 1882. Common's free edition heads the section "What is Romanticism?" Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: If the same work can come from abundance or from poverty, what evidence could settle which it came from?

    Why it belongs: The hinge, and the one place where Nietzsche gives the criterion the polemics will apply. Revisiting his own attachment to Wagner and Schopenhauer, he separates two things that look identical from outside: suffering that comes from an overflow of life and suffering that comes from its impoverishment. Either can produce destruction; either can produce a craving for permanence and rest. What differs is the need underneath, and the act alone does not disclose it. What he calls Dionysian pessimism affirms difficulty without treating every expression of pain or destructiveness as a sign of strength. Take the criterion seriously and the later Wagner writings become harder rather than easier to accept, because they assert the diagnosis far more often than they demonstrate it. This section is chosen over the more obvious 1887 candidates precisely because it is the argument rather than the verdict.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§3–5; Nietzsche Contra Wagner.

    Online edition: §370 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core · 1887

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §§3–5

    Wagner as a case inside somebody else's argument

    Free edition trans. Horace B. Samuel; the link opens the third essay and you will need Find for the section numbers. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: The essay is about ascetic ideals, not about Wagner. What does using him as the opening example commit the argument to?

    Why it belongs: The third essay opens by asking what ascetic ideals mean for different kinds of people — artists, philosophers, priests — and takes Wagner as its first worked example, turning on his late treatment of chastity and on the philosophical authority he claimed. This is the most analytically serious thing Nietzsche wrote about him, and the reason is structural: Wagner is not the subject here, he is evidence in an argument about something larger, which forces the diagnosis to do work rather than simply land. Read §§3–5 as one movement, and then read §6, where the essay turns from the artist to Kant's and Schopenhauer's accounts of the beautiful — that section is examined on tragedy and aesthetic justification. Note the limit Nietzsche does not always observe: showing what a position did for the person holding it is not yet showing the position to be false.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §370; The Case of Wagner.

    Online edition: Third Essay (trans. Horace B. Samuel; use Find for §§3–5) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core · 1888

    The Case of Wagner §§1–12

    The polemic, written for the occasion

    Free edition trans. Anthony M. Ludovici, in a volume that also contains Nietzsche Contra Wagner — different works, printed together. The section links open printed pages; the first link opens the volume. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: How much of this is diagnosis and how much is performance? Does Nietzsche think the difference matters here?

    Why it belongs: The short book that gave the theme its name, and the only one of the seven readings written about Wagner from start to finish. Three sections repay particular attention. §6 stages Wagner's success as imaginary advice to young artists — manufacture overwhelming effects, exalted feelings, promises of salvation — which is the polemic's method in miniature: a recipe offered as a diagnosis. §7 separates the power to persuade a crowd from Wagner's real gift, which Nietzsche locates in the small musical detail, and the question of what then happens to the whole is the seed of everything he means by decadence. §11 connects mass success with theatrical display while crediting Wagner with raising what performers demanded of themselves; its praise of discipline sits directly beside its attack on obedience and on the German Empire. Read the whole thing — it is short — and keep asking which claims could be checked.

    Read alongside: The Case of Wagner, Postscript; what "decadence" is doing.

    Online edition: The Case of Wagner · §6 · §7 · §11 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  7. Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Clever" §§5–6; "Why I Write Such Good Books — The Case of Wagner" §§1–4

    The break as its author wants it remembered

    Written in 1888, published in 1908. Ludovici's free edition titles the chapters "Why I Am So Clever" and "Why I Write Such Excellent Books"; the review of The Case of Wagner comes last within the second. Use the contents or Find for the section numbers.

    Question: A person's own account of why they broke with someone is evidence of something. Of what?

    Why it belongs: The last word, and the least neutral. "Why I Am So Clever" is Nietzsche's account of what he counted as nourishing — climate, diet, reading, music — and Wagner enters it there, in the register of a condition of life rather than of an argument. The chapter on The Case of Wagner then explains what the polemic was for. Both are written in his final productive months, in a book designed as a self-presentation, and neither is a record of 1876. That is the reason to read them here rather than first: having gone through the sequence in order, you can see exactly which parts of the story he keeps, which he smooths, and which he silently drops. Treat the disagreement between this account and the 1886 preface as the most useful thing on offer.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, 1886 Preface; "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Clever" (use Find for §§5–6) · "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" (the chapter on The Case of Wagner comes last) (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Four things Nietzsche is doing in these texts

Every document on this page belongs to one of four kinds, and the kind determines what it can be used as evidence for. Establish which one you are reading before deciding what it shows.

Writing contemporary praise
The 1872 dedication and Untimely Meditations IV are written while the collaboration is current, for a public, about something that has not yet happened. They are advocacy, and they should be read as advocacy is read: for what is being claimed, for whom it is being claimed to, and for what the claim would have cost if it failed. Neither is a private document and neither is a report.
Revising an earlier judgment
Human, All Too Human (1878) takes apart the theory of the artist the earlier praise was built on, largely without naming the case. The Gay Science §370 supplies the criterion. Genealogy III uses Wagner as evidence in an argument about ascetic ideals. The Case of Wagner attacks him directly. These are the places where something is actually being argued, and they are also the places where the argument can be checked.
Compiling older passages into a new book
Nietzsche Contra Wagner (1888) is assembled by Nietzsche from passages written across the preceding decade, selected and rearranged. The book is late; most of the sentences are not. Dating a line to 1888 because it appears in an 1888 book is the commonest error made with this material, and the compilation is the place it is easiest to make.
Retrospectively narrating his own break
The 1886 prefaces and Ecce Homo tell the story of a change after it has happened, by the person it happened to, with later purposes in view. They are excellent evidence of how Nietzsche in 1886 and 1888 wanted the episode understood. They are weak evidence of what he thought in 1876, which the 1876 essay is much better evidence of — and the two do not fully agree.

The practical consequence is a habit rather than a rule: before quoting any sentence from this material, establish when it was written, not when it was printed, and whether Nietzsche is arguing with someone or telling a story about himself. Both are worth reading. They are not worth confusing.

Keep these distinctions in view

When a sentence was written and when its book appeared
The prefaces of 1886 sit in front of books from 1878 and 1882; Nietzsche Contra Wagner collects passages from across a decade; Ecce Homo was written in 1888 and published in 1908. On this theme, dating by the book will produce a false chronology almost immediately.
A judgment revised and a friendship ended
These are separate events with separate evidence, and only the first is documented in the published work. The texts show when the theory of the artist changed and when the verdict was printed. What happened between two people is not something this page can settle, and Nietzsche's own accounts of it are the accounts of one party.
Symptom and cause
Nietzsche's late claim is that Wagner exhibits a condition rather than creating it — which is a weaker and more interesting claim than the one the rhetoric usually suggests. When a passage reads as an accusation, check whether the argument underneath it is diagnostic. Often it is; sometimes the accusation is doing all the work.
Decadence as diagnosis and decadence as insult
In its technical use the word names a relation between parts and a whole, applied to a style, a body or a culture. In its polemical use it is simply a term of abuse. Both are present in 1888, sometimes in the same paragraph, and the first is worth something only where it can be kept apart from the second.
The music and the case
Nietzsche's late writing describes musical effects — on audiences, on performers, on himself — but it does not analyse the scores, and it was never intended to. Nothing on this page settles a question about Wagner's music, and treating the polemic as musical criticism will misjudge both.
Advocacy and argument
The 1872 close and the 1876 essay are written to persuade a public towards a project. That is not a defect, but it is a genre, and its claims are not supported in the way the earlier chapters of The Birth of Tragedy support theirs. Noticing the difference is what makes the later retraction legible.
Nietzsche's account of the break and the break
Nearly everything a reader knows about the shape of this episode comes from Nietzsche's own retrospective telling of it. That telling is a primary text about him in 1886 and 1888. It is not a neutral chronicle, and this page does not treat it as one.

What "decadence" is doing

The word does not appear in the early writing and is not the name of a doctrine Nietzsche worked out over a career. It arrives as a term of art in 1888 and does several jobs at once. In The Case of Wagner its aesthetic sense is clearest: §7 sets Wagner's real gift, which Nietzsche places in the small musical detail, against the question of what becomes of the whole that the details are supposed to serve. A style in which the part has stopped serving the whole is the model, and the same relation is then applied outward — to a body whose drives no longer compose into anything, and to a culture whose members no longer belong to a common form.

Two things follow for a reader. The first is that the term is genuinely useful at the aesthetic level, where it names something one can look for in a work and argue about. The second is that its extension to bodies and cultures is where it stops being checkable. When Nietzsche moves from a claim about musical form to a claim about physiological decline, the evidence does not travel with him, and the confidence of the prose does not change to mark the difference.

The site does not follow him everywhere this term goes. Twilight, "Skirmishes" §§35–37 applies the standard of ascending and descending life to modern morality, and in §36 it endorses a role for physicians in eliminating lives Nietzsche classifies as degenerate. That is not a metaphor for personal growth and it should not be read as one; the passage is assigned here because the theme cannot be understood without it, and it is something to assess rather than adopt. The aesthetic sections carry a related problem: where "Skirmishes" §§19–20 ties ugliness to perceived degeneration and bodily impairment, the argument is not established biology, and a reaction in an observer would not establish a defect in the person observed.

Ecce Homo adds the last complication. Nietzsche applies the diagnosis to himself, presenting his own constitution as a case of the condition and his ability to recognise it as evidence of something else. Whatever one makes of that, it rules out the simplest reading of the Wagner polemic, in which decadence is what other people have.

    The Birth of Tragedy Foreword to Richard Wagner; §§16–19

  • Complication · 1886

    The Birth of Tragedy "Attempt at a Self-Criticism" §§6–7

    The prediction withdrawn, in the same book

    The 1886 preface, printed in front of the 1872 text. Haussmann's free edition heads it "An Attempt at Self-Criticism".

    Question: He disowns the hope. Does he disown the argument that produced it?

    Why it belongs: §6 is the retraction in Nietzsche's own words: he says he spoiled the Greek problem by mixing in the most modern things and entertained hopes where nothing was to be hoped for, and that he has since come to regard the contemporary German music he once celebrated as romanticism through and through, and the most un-Grecian of all possible forms of art. That is the 1872 close repudiated by name, fourteen years later, in a preface bound into the same volume. §7 replaces the book's offer of metaphysical comfort with laughter. It is the cleanest example on this page of a judgment revised in public, and the useful exercise is to compare it with the version Ecce Homo gives two years later.

    Read alongside: Foreword; §§16–19; Ecce Homo.

    Online edition: §6 · §7 (trans. William A. Haussmann) · Preferred edition.

Untimely Meditations3 reading assignments

Four essays published between 1873 and 1876; the fourth is the Wagner essay. The free editions divide them across two volumes under the title Thoughts Out of Season: the Strauss and Wagner essays are in Part I, translated by Anthony M. Ludovici, and the essays on history and Schopenhauer are in Part II, translated by Adrian Collins. Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale.

  • Development · 1873

    Untimely Meditations I, "David Strauss, the Confessor and the Writer" §§1–2

    The contrast type, named early

    Question: The test proposed here is aimed at Strauss. What does it do to Bayreuth?

    Why it belongs: The opening sections introduce the cultivated philistine — the figure who treats accepted taste as proof of achievement, and an empire's success as evidence about its culture. Everything the early Nietzsche wants from Wagner is defined against this type, which makes the essay useful here in a way its author did not intend: the standard it sets up is one the Bayreuth public of 1876 has to be measured by too, and Untimely Meditations IV §1 half admits it. Note also the method, which Nietzsche never abandons: a reputation is replaced by a vivid image, and the image does the work an argument would otherwise have to do.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations IV §1; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: "David Strauss" §1 · §2 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1874

    Untimely Meditations III, "Schopenhauer as Educator" §6

    Who pays for culture, and what the payment does to it

    Free edition: Thoughts Out of Season, Part II, trans. Adrian Collins, where the essay appears under the same title.

    Question: If the powers that fund a culture bend it towards their own interests, what should one expect of a festival with patrons?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche makes the production of exceptional individuals the central task and then examines the powers that support education while directing it towards themselves — commerce, the state, social polish, scholarship. Two years before Bayreuth, that is an argument with obvious application to an undertaking dependent on subscribers, a court and a public, and it is the sharpest internal check on the essay that follows. Note the cost the argument accepts: many people are asked to sustain achievements that will never be theirs, which is an elitism this site treats as something to examine rather than to adopt. The essay's wider work is on self-overcoming and free spirits.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations IV §§1, 4–5, 9; The Case of Wagner, Postscript.

    Online edition: "Schopenhauer as Educator" §6 (trans. Adrian Collins) · Preferred edition.

Human, All Too Human2 reading assignments

The 1878 book, with a preface added in 1886. Wagner is barely named in Volume I, which is exactly what makes it important here: the theory the praise depended on is dismantled before the verdict is announced. Volume II (1879–80) continues the analysis in two further instalments; see the edition guidance for the separate free volume. Free edition of Volume I trans. Helen Zimmern, whose section headings are given below. Preferred translation: R. J. Hollingdale.

  • Core · 1878

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §§162–163, 222

    The theory taken apart without the case being named

    Zimmern heads them "The Cult of Genius For the Sake of Vanity.", "The Earnestness of Handicraft." and "What Remains of Art."

    Question: Which of the 1872 claims about Wagner could survive these three sections?

    Why it belongs: Three short sections that do more damage to the early position than any of the polemics. §162 argues that calling an achievement miraculous puts it beyond comparison and thereby protects the vanity of everyone who did not achieve it. §163 states the alternative flatly — not gifts or inborn talents but small exercises, close observation and long practice, with the training of a writer of fiction as the example. §222 then asks what is left of art once the metaphysical claims it used to carry stop being believable, and answers that the capacities art taught may outlive the beliefs. Nothing here mentions Bayreuth. Everything here removes the ground the 1872 prediction stood on, which is why the 1886 preface can describe the change as a liberation rather than as a quarrel.

    Read alongside: Volume I, 1886 Preface; Tragedy and aesthetic justification.

Beyond Good and Evil1 reading assignment

One assignment, from Part VIII, "Peoples and Fatherlands" (§§240–256), where Wagner appears inside an argument about Germany and Europe rather than about art. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296; the free edition, translated by Helen Zimmern, is divided by part, so the link opens the part and you will need Find. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

  • Extension · 1886

    Beyond Good and Evil §§240, 256 · Part VIII

    Wagner placed as a German question inside a European one

    The first and last sections of Part VIII. Use Find for the numbers.

    Question: What changes when Wagner is treated as a national symptom rather than as an artist?

    Why it belongs: The part opens with Nietzsche listening again to the overture to Die Meistersinger and closes with an argument that Europe is moving towards unity while its loudest voices insist on national difference. Wagner sits inside both. This is the coolest of the Wagner writings and the one least driven by the break: the question is what kind of thing a German artist was in the 1870s and 1880s, and the answer is given as cultural politics. Read it before the 1888 polemics to see which of their charges were already formulated without heat. The nationalism material is treated at length on the herd and the last man.

    Read alongside: The Case of Wagner, Postscript; The Gay Science §370.

    Online edition: Part VIII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for §§240 and 256) · Preferred edition.

The Case of Wagner and Nietzsche Contra Wagner3 reading assignments

Two short books from 1888, printed together in the free volume translated by Anthony M. Ludovici and frequently confused with each other. They are different works and were assembled in different ways: The Case of Wagner was written as a polemic, while Nietzsche Contra Wagner gathers passages Nietzsche had published across the preceding decade. The preferred editions separate them too — The Case of Wagner is in Kaufmann's Basic Writings, Nietzsche Contra Wagner in The Portable Nietzsche. See the edition guidance.

  • Core · 1888

    The Case of Wagner §§1–12

    The polemic, written for the occasion

    Question: Which of these claims could be checked, and how?

    Why it belongs: The only one of the readings written about Wagner from start to finish. See the first encounter for the full explanation, including §6, §7 and §11.

    Read alongside: Postscript; Nietzsche Contra Wagner.

    Online edition: The Case of Wagner · §6 · §7 · §11 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888

    The Case of Wagner Postscript

    From one composer to an institution

    The book carries closing material after §12; the link opens the printed page where the Postscript begins. Further closing pieces follow it in the volume.

    Question: Is the objection to Wagner or to the arrangement that made him unanswerable?

    Why it belongs: The polemic widens here from a musician to the apparatus around him: organised worship, and the theatre's claim to rule over questions of taste. This is where the book stops being about one artist and becomes the cultural argument the theme is named for, and it connects directly back to the 1874 question about who funds a culture and what the funding does to it. Read the closing material rather than stopping at §12; the part of the book people quote is not the part that carries its thesis.

    Read alongside: Untimely Meditations III §6; what "decadence" is doing.

    Online edition: Postscript (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888, published 1889

    Nietzsche Contra Wagner whole, short

    A late book made mostly of earlier writing

    Printed in the same free volume as The Case of Wagner; this page has no verified section anchor for it, so the link opens the volume and you should use its contents. Preferred edition: Kaufmann's The Portable Nietzsche.

    Question: Does a passage mean the same thing in the book it was written for and in the book it was moved to?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche assembled this one himself in his last working weeks, from material he had already published — much of it years earlier — selected, retitled and arranged to make a case. That makes it the most interesting object on the page and the easiest to misread. A line in it may date from 1878 or 1882 or 1887; the arrangement is what is new. Read it last, after the sources it draws on, and watch what the selection does: what gets included, what gets dropped, and how a passage written as analysis reads once it has been placed in a book with this title. The habit it teaches is the one this whole page is built around.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §370; four things Nietzsche is doing.

    Online edition: The volume containing Nietzsche Contra Wagner (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use the contents) · Edition guidance.

Twilight of the Idols2 reading assignments

Two assignments from "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man", where the late vocabulary is at its most explicit and at its most objectionable. Section numbers restart in each chapter; Ludovici's free edition titles it "Skirmishes in a War with the Age". Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann. The chapter's account of intoxication and of art as a stimulant is treated on Apollonian and Dionysian.

  • Complication · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes" §§19–20

    Beauty conferred, and ugliness read as decline

    The link opens the chapter; use Find for the section numbers.

    Question: Does a reaction in an observer tell you anything about the thing observed?

    Why it belongs: §19 asks whether beauty is found in things or conferred by human standards, and treats aesthetic judgment as a species affirming its own kind — an argument worth taking seriously, and the aesthetic half of what "decadence" later claims. §20 then makes the far stronger move, tying ugliness to perceived degeneration, ageing and bodily impairment and presenting aversion as a physiological reaction. That is not established biology, it stigmatises forms of embodiment by treating them as signs of diminished life, and a reaction in the observer would not establish a defect in the observed. The two sections are assigned together because the second is what the first becomes when the restraint is dropped, and because this is where the late aesthetic vocabulary does real harm.

    Read alongside: "Skirmishes" §§35–37; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use Find for §§19–20) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes" §§35–37

    Ascending and descending life, and what the standard licenses

    The link opens the printed page where §35 begins; §37 begins on a later page.

    Question: Whose lives stay protected when vitality replaces humanitarian concern as the standard?

    Why it belongs: The place where the late standard is applied to morality itself. Nietzsche attacks altruism and disputes that gentler modern conduct shows moral progress; §36 assigns physicians a role in eliminating lives he classifies as degenerate and praises chosen death. The sequence shows plainly what affirming ascending life can be used to license, which is contempt for vulnerable people and a rejection of equal standing. It is assigned because the theme is unintelligible without seeing where the vocabulary goes, and it is to be assessed rather than adopted. Its wider setting is on revaluation.

    Read alongside: "Skirmishes" §§19–20; what "decadence" is doing.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes" §§35–37 · §37 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Ecce Homo3 reading assignments

Written in 1888, published in 1908. Ludovici's free edition titles the chapters "Why I Am So Wise", "Why I Am So Clever", "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" and "Why I Am a Fatality"; the reviews of individual books sit inside the third. Everything here is self-presentation, which is the reason to read it and the reason not to trust it. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

  • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §§1–2

    The diagnosis turned on the diagnostician

    The link opens the chapter; use Find for the section numbers.

    Question: If recognising decadence requires having been decadent, what happens to the argument's independence?

    Why it belongs: The opening of the book applies the late vocabulary to Nietzsche's own constitution: he presents himself as a case of the condition he diagnoses and treats the capacity to see it as belonging to something other than the condition. Whatever one makes of the claim, it rules out the simplest reading of the Wagner polemic, in which decadence is a property of other people. It also shows the vocabulary doing what it does most often in 1888 — moving between a physiological assertion and a cultural verdict without marking the crossing. Renderings of these sections differ considerably between translations; compare two before quoting.

    Read alongside: what "decadence" is doing; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Wise" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; use Find for §§1–2) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Clever" §§5–6; "Why I Write Such Good Books — The Case of Wagner" §§1–4

    The break as its author wants it remembered

    Question: A person's own account of why they broke with someone is evidence of what?

    Why it belongs: The last and least neutral telling. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human, 1886 Preface; "Attempt at a Self-Criticism" §§6–7.

    Online edition: "Why I Am So Clever" (use Find for §§5–6) · "Why I Write Such Excellent Books" (the chapter on The Case of Wagner comes last) (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Am a Destiny" §1

    The polemicist's account of what a polemicist is

    Ludovici: "Why I Am a Fatality." The declaration that he is not a man but dynamite appears in this section — §1, not later in the chapter; renderings differ between translations.

    Question: Does this description fit the author of The Case of Wagner?

    Why it belongs: The chapter opens by predicting that his name will be attached to a crisis without precedent and describing himself in terms of explosive force. It belongs here as the self-understanding the 1888 polemics were written out of, and as a limit case for the whole theme: a polemic whose author claims this much for himself is not easy to read as diagnosis. Compare it with Genealogy III §§3–5, where the same writer works far more carefully on the same subject, one year earlier. The agonistic material is treated on greatness and the agon.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§3–5; The Case of Wagner §§1–12.

    Online edition: "Why I Am a Fatality" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

A note on the notebooks

This page carries no notebook route, and on this theme the reason is unusually strong. The whole argument here is about dating: which judgment was held when, and which text was written for which occasion. Notebook entries are the worst available evidence for that question, because the posthumous arrangements that most readers encounter group them by subject rather than by date and assign them numbers no author chose. Using such material to establish a chronology would undo the one thing this page is for.

The late notebooks do contain a great deal on decadence, and the posthumous compilation gathers some of it. The published statements are in the books, and they are specific enough to work with: The Case of Wagner and its closing material, "Skirmishes" §§19–20 and §§35–37, and the opening of Ecce Homo. Anything in the notes should be read against those, not in place of them.

The sitewide caution applies if you follow that material: The Will to Power is a posthumous arrangement of notebook entries, its numbering is editorial rather than Nietzsche's, and it brings together notes written at different dates. See the edition guidance, and for the notebooks as written rather than as arranged, Nietzsche Source's critical edition. Note also that Nietzsche Contra Wagner, though assembled from earlier writing, is not Nachlass at all: Nietzsche selected and arranged it himself from work he had already published.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The same book, twice. Read the Foreword and §§16–19, then "Attempt at a Self-Criticism" §§6–7, which are bound into the same volume fourteen years apart. Then read what Ecce Homo says about the episode. Where do the two retractions differ?
  • Before the name is used. Take Human, All Too Human I §§162–163, 222 and ask which of the claims in Untimely Meditations IV survive them. The verdict changes years after the theory does.
  • Argument against verdict. Put The Gay Science §370 beside The Case of Wagner. The first gives the criterion, the second delivers the judgment. How often is the criterion actually applied?
  • Who pays. Read Untimely Meditations III §6 and then The Case of Wagner's closing material. Fourteen years apart, the same question about institutions and taste — and the later one is aimed at something the earlier Nietzsche helped promote.

A question to carry forward

Take a figure whose work you once admired and no longer do. Write, separately, what you would have said then and what you would say now. Then ask which of the two the change actually happened in — and whether you could tell from the documents alone.
