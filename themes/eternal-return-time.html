Take away a stable being underneath things, and take away a purpose the world is moving towards. Time stops being a container events pass through on their way somewhere, and becomes the whole of what there is. That is the problem this page follows, and it is a different problem from the one the demon poses.

The difference in labour is worth stating at the outset, because the two pages share texts. Eternal recurrence takes the thought as something addressed to a reader: The Gay Science §341 and the heaviest weight, Zarathustra's confrontation with the thought and what it costs him, the demand that a life be wanted again in full. That is the existential register, and this page does not rebuild it. What is left over is substantial: what time looks like once purpose and permanence have gone, why the will finds the past unbearable, what the gateway image is actually a picture of, and how far the unpublished cosmological attempts extend any of it.

The order used here is published texts first. That is not a formality on this theme. The most confident statements about the world as a cycle of force are in notebooks Nietzsche did not publish, and it is easy to read the published books as illustrations of them. Read the other way round, the published texts turn out to be doing something more limited and more interesting — dismantling the metaphysics that makes becoming look like a defect, and then asking what a will can do about a past it cannot alter.

One further caution. Nietzsche's early writing on Heraclitus is genuinely part of this story, and it is not evidence that he had already arrived at recurrence in 1873. The Heraclitean background handles that separately, and keeps three categories of unpublished writing apart: an early essay he finished and set aside, the later notebooks, and the posthumous compilation assembled from them.

Begin with five readings, all from published books. The first encounter runs in date order and keeps to time and becoming rather than to recurrence as a personal test. Continue through the fuller route, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook material is a separate and optional investigation, placed last for a reason.

Essential first encounter

Five sections or chapters: 1882, 1883, 1884, 1887, 1888. The sequence moves from a world without design, to the will's quarrel with what has already happened, to the image that compresses the thought, to the metaphysics that has to be cleared away, and finally to Nietzsche's own account of why philosophy preferred being to becoming.

  1. Core · 1882

    The Gay Science §109 · Book III

    A world that is not a machine, an organism, or a plan

    Free edition: Thomas Common's The Joyful Wisdom. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: Nietzsche lists what the world is not — living, dead, ordered, lawful, purposive. What positive claim, if any, is left standing at the end?

    Why it belongs: The section that sets the conditions for everything else on this page, and it does its work almost entirely by subtraction. Nietzsche warns against thinking the universe is a living thing, against thinking it a machine, against supposing that what looks like order is anything but an exception thrown up by chance, and against the astral shadows of God that keep returning in scientific dress. What follows is not an alternative cosmology but an instruction: when will we be done with the shadows, and when may nature be de-deified. Read it for the shape of the negative argument, and notice how much of what we ordinarily call time — direction, development, arrival — is being treated as one of the shadows.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: §109 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, "On Redemption"

    The will's quarrel with what has already happened

    Common: "Redemption"; the free edition is Thus Spake Zarathustra. The chapter's central phrase for transforming every "it was" is rendered by Kaufmann as willing it so and by Common as wishing to have had it so; the two are not interchangeable, and this page paraphrases rather than putting one translator's sentence under the other's link.

    Question: Zarathustra calls the will's inability to go backwards its loneliest sorrow. Is that a fact about willing, or about a particular way of willing?

    Why it belongs: The diagnosis the whole theme turns on, and the reason time is a problem rather than a topic. The will can act forwards and cannot act backwards; what has been is a stone it cannot roll away. Zarathustra's claim is that this incapacity is the source of a resentment directed at time itself — that the spirit of revenge is the will's ill-will towards what it cannot undo, and that the moral interpretations of existence which punish, which call existence a debt, grow out of it. The redemption he proposes is not a power to change the past but a transformation in how the past is willed. Note where the speech stops: Zarathustra breaks off, and the hunchback asks why he speaks differently to his pupils than to himself. The chapter poses the problem and does not solve it.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra III, "On Old and New Tables"; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part II, "Redemption" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core · 1884

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2

    The gateway, and the two roads nobody has yet followed to the end

    Common: "The Vision and the Enigma." The link opens the whole chapter; §2 is the second numbered part.

    Question: The dwarf answers that time is a circle. Zarathustra is angry at the answer. What is wrong with it?

    Why it belongs: The most concentrated image of time in the corpus, and it repays being read as an image rather than as a compressed proof. A gateway is named Moment; two roads meet at it, one running back for an eternity and one running forward for an eternity, and they contradict each other. Zarathustra asks whether everything that can run must already have run down the road behind, and whether this gateway too must already have been. The dwarf's reply — that all truth is crooked and time itself a circle — is the answer Zarathustra refuses, and the refusal is the point: he calls it making things too easy for oneself. What follows is not an argument but the shepherd and the snake, which the eternal recurrence page takes up as the existential confrontation. Here the question is narrower: what has actually been shown about time by the end of the section?

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §56; Eternal recurrence.

    Online edition: Part III, "The Vision and the Enigma" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  4. Complication · 1887

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I §13

    Whether anything stands behind what happens

    Free translation Horace B. Samuel; the link opens the first essay, so use Find for the section number. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale.

    Question: Separate the argument from grammar from the analogy with birds and lambs. Does the analogy do any work the grammar does not?

    Why it belongs: Assigned here as a complication about substance and event, not as a statement about recurrence — it says nothing about recurrence, and reading it as though it did is a common error. What it argues is that the subject who could have acted otherwise than its strength dictates is an artefact of language: behind the doing there is no separate doer, and the deed is everything. For this theme the relevance is that a world of pure becoming needs exactly this: if there were enduring substances behind events, becoming would be something that happens to things rather than what there is. Two limits. The grammatical argument is stronger than the animal analogy. And denying a wholly separate doer does not establish that restraint is impossible or that objecting to injury is unreasonable — a distinction the section itself does not always keep.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Will to power.

    Online edition: First Essay: locate §13 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–6

    Why philosophers preferred what does not change

    Ludovici's free edition titles the chapter "'Reason' in Philosophy". Section numbers restart in each chapter; the link opens the chapter. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann.

    Question: If the senses report change truthfully and reason is what falsifies, what happens to the standard usually used to correct the senses?

    Why it belongs: The clearest published statement of the position, and the right place to end the first encounter. Nietzsche charges philosophers with a hatred of the idea of becoming and with mummifying their concepts: what is, on their account, does not become, and what becomes is not. He then reverses the usual verdict on the senses, arguing that they do not lie about change and that reason is where the falsification enters, along with the conviction that the more abstract a concept is the closer it stands to reality. The last of the assigned sections traces the belief in being to a confidence in grammar. Read it as a diagnosis rather than a proof — Nietzsche explains why the preference for permanence arose, which is not the same as showing that nothing permanent exists — and notice that his own explanatory vocabulary would have to survive the same test.

    Read alongside: "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable"; Genealogy I §13.

    Online edition: "'Reason' in Philosophy" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Four investigations to take after the first encounter. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below.

1. What has to go before time becomes a problem

The negative work. None of it is about recurrence; all of it is what recurrence would presuppose.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–6
    The preference for permanence explained as a habit of reason and of grammar.
  2. Twilight of the Idols — "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable"
    Six stages, ending with the apparent world going too.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §13
    No doer behind the deed, and what that leaves.
  4. Twilight of the Idols — "The Four Great Errors" §§3, 8
    Imaginary causes, and a responsibility that needs a substrate.

2. The will and the past

The part of the theme that is about a person's relation to time rather than about time itself.

  1. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, "On Redemption"
    Revenge against time named as the will's ill-will towards what is done.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — III, "On Old and New Tables"
    The same demand restated for someone who has to make values.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — III, "Before Sunrise"
    Chance defended against purpose, in the form of a prayer.
  4. The Gay Science — §109
    The cosmological version of the same refusal of purpose.

3. The image of time, and what it is an image of

Four passages that give the thought a shape. Ask of each whether it argues, depicts, or celebrates.

  1. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — III, "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2
    The gateway, and the answer Zarathustra rejects.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §56
    The most compressed published formulation, reached through pessimism.
  3. The Gay Science — §112
    Cause and effect as pieces cut out of a continuum.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — IV, "The Drunken Song"
    Time affirmed in a song rather than in an argument.

4. The early background, and what it does not establish

Read these for what the young Nietzsche took from Heraclitus, and read the section below before drawing conclusions from them.

  1. Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks
    The Heraclitus chapters, in an essay Nietzsche finished and did not publish.
  2. The Birth of Tragedy — §16
    Becoming given a metaphysical role, in borrowed Schopenhauerian terms.
  3. The Birth of Tragedy — §24
    Dissonance and play, as the early answer to what becoming costs.
  4. Ecce Homo — on The Birth of Tragedy §3
    Nietzsche's own retrospective claim of a Heraclitean ancestry.

Keep these distinctions in view

Becoming and recurrence
That the world is process without underlying substance is one claim. That the process repeats is a second, much stronger one, and nothing in the first entails it. Most of the published material on this page supports the first. A reader can accept all of Twilight's "'Reason' in Philosophy" and still find recurrence unmotivated.
An image of time and a proof about time
The gateway in "On the Vision and the Riddle" is a picture, offered inside a narrated vision, and the section's own dramatic action is Zarathustra rejecting the dwarf's neat formulation of it. Reading the image as a compressed argument supplies premises the text does not.
The existential test and the cosmological claim
Whether a person could want their life again is a question about that person. Whether states of the world must repeat is a question about physics. Nietzsche moves between them; they have different evidence and different failure conditions, and the first does not need the second to be true. The existential side is developed on eternal recurrence.
Published and notebook
The published texts give a diagnosis of the preference for being, an account of the will's relation to the past, and one compressed formulation of recurrence. The extended cosmological attempts are in notebooks. Where the two pull apart, the published text governs; a notebook entry may sharpen, preview, complicate or overstate a published claim, and may not settle a question the books leave open.
Three kinds of unpublished writing
They are not one category. Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks is a finished early essay Nietzsche completed and set aside; it is not part of the posthumous compilation and should not be cited as though it were. The later notebooks are working material. The Will to Power is an arrangement of some of that material, made after his collapse, with editorial numbering. Collapsing the three makes the chronology unreadable.
Revenge against time and wanting a different past
Wishing a particular thing had gone otherwise is ordinary. What "On Redemption" describes is broader: a standing grievance against the fact that anything is finished at all, which then gets expressed as a moral interpretation of existence. The second is what Zarathustra attacks, and attacking it does not require approving of everything that has happened.
Abolishing the true world and abolishing the apparent one
The six-stage history in Twilight ends by removing both, not by promoting appearance to reality. A reading that keeps the apparent world as the one that is left has stopped at stage five, and has quietly retained the two-world structure the chapter is dismantling.

The Heraclitean background

Nietzsche wrote at length about Heraclitus as a young classicist, in Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks — an essay he completed in 1873 and did not publish. Its Heraclitus chapters present a world of coming-to-be and passing-away with no permanent being behind it, strife as the ordering principle rather than a disturbance of order, and the whole process as a kind of play, innocent of guilt and of purpose. The young Nietzsche's admiration is unmistakable.

Three things follow, and they need to be kept apart. The essay is genuine intellectual background: the mature attack on the preference for permanence is recognizably continuous with it, and Twilight names Heraclitus as the exception among the philosophers. It is also an interpretation of Heraclitus by a nineteenth-century philologist with a thesis, not a neutral report of what Heraclitus held, and readers who want the fragments themselves will have to go elsewhere. And it does not show that Nietzsche had formulated eternal recurrence in 1873. Affirming becoming and holding that states of the world repeat are different positions; the essay argues for something like the first and is silent about the second.

Nietzsche himself complicates this. In Ecce Homo he suggests that the doctrine of recurrence might already have been taught by Heraclitus, and credits the Stoics with an inheritance from him. That is a retrospective claim made in 1888 by an author constructing an ancestry for his own thought, and it is evidence about Nietzsche in 1888 rather than about Heraclitus or about 1873. Read it as such, and notice how convenient the ancestry is.

A practical note on sources. The essay is printed in a free collection of early writings translated by Maximilian A. Mügge, and it has its own link in the survey below. It is not part of The Birth of Tragedy, and it is not part of the posthumous compilation; earlier versions of this page pointed readers to the published book's online text for it, which sent them to the wrong work. The two are assigned separately here.

The early essays2 reading assignments

Two pieces from 1873 that Nietzsche completed and did not publish. They are unpublished writing of a quite different kind from the later notebooks, and neither belongs to the posthumous compilation. Both are printed in a free collection translated by Maximilian A. Mügge; there is no single matching Kaufmann or Hollingdale volume for the collection, so check individual titles in a modern anthology.

  • Development · 1873, unpublished

    Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks Heraclitus chapters

    Becoming, strife, and a world at play

    Mügge's free edition titles it "Philosophy during the Tragic Age of the Greeks". A distinct work from The Birth of Tragedy, with its own source link; use the essay's contents to reach the Heraclitus chapters.

    Question: How much of the position described here is Heraclitus's, and how much is what the young Nietzsche needed Heraclitus to have held?

    Why it belongs: The earliest sustained statement of the commitment this theme rests on: that coming-to-be and passing-away are all there is, that strife is what orders the world rather than what disturbs it, and that the whole is a play without guilt or aim. It is the background against which the late attack on the philosophers' Egypticism becomes legible. Read it with its status attached. It is an unpublished essay by a classicist in his twenties, arguing against the scholarship of his day; it is an interpretation of Heraclitus rather than a report of him; and it does not contain eternal recurrence. See the Heraclitean background.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Ecce Homo on The Birth of Tragedy §3.

    Online edition: Philosophy during the Tragic Age of the Greeks (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

  • Extension · 1873, unpublished

    On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense whole, short

    Concepts as the residue of forgotten metaphors

    Mügge's free edition titles it "On Truth and Falsity in their Ultramoral Sense".

    Question: If fixing a concept means ignoring the differences between the cases it covers, what does that imply about any account of a world in flux — including this one?

    Why it belongs: The companion essay, and the early form of a difficulty this theme cannot avoid. Nietzsche argues that concepts are formed by discarding what is individual and unequal, so that the stability of a concept records a forgetting rather than a discovery. For a page about becoming that is double-edged: it explains why a world of process gets described in terms of enduring things, and it makes the description of the process itself suspect. Treated as an epistemological argument on perspectivism.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §112; Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy".

    Online edition: On Truth and Falsity in their Ultramoral Sense (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

The Birth of Tragedy2 reading assignments

1872, and a published book — separate from the early essay above, with its own translation and its own link. Two sections bear on this theme; the book's main arguments are treated on Apollonian and Dionysian and tragedy and aesthetic justification. Free edition trans. William A. Haussmann; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

  • Extension · 1872

    The Birth of Tragedy §16

    Becoming given a metaphysical role, in borrowed language

    Question: How much of the position survives once the Schopenhauerian apparatus it is stated in is withdrawn?

    Why it belongs: The chapter in which music is treated as the direct expression of an underlying reality that images can only copy, and in which what is permanent and individuated is subordinate to a ceaseless process beneath it. The affirmation of becoming is unmistakably present, and it is stated in exactly the borrowed metaphysics Nietzsche would later regret — the 1886 preface names the Kantian and Schopenhauerian formulae as what most needs removing. Read it to see the commitment arriving before the vocabulary that could carry it, and do not treat the primordial unity here as an early version of the later cosmology; the later work removes precisely this kind of substrate.

    Read alongside: Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks; Apollonian and Dionysian.

    Online edition: §16 (trans. William A. Haussmann) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1872

    The Birth of Tragedy §24

    Destruction included in a game rather than excused

    Question: Does calling the world's self-destruction a game answer the objection to it, or restate the objection in a nicer tone?

    Why it belongs: The chapter that asks why we take pleasure in the ugly and the discordant on a stage, and answers with a world-artist at play — building and destroying without moral purpose. It belongs here for the picture of a process with no end-state, in which what is destroyed is not a failure of the process but part of how it goes. That is a genuine antecedent of the later refusal of teleology. It is also early and Schopenhauerian in its machinery, and the site treats its aesthetic claim on its own page; here the interest is strictly in the temporal picture. Do not run it together with eternal recurrence, which the book does not contain.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "What I Owe to the Ancients" §5; Tragedy and aesthetic justification.

    Online edition: §24 (trans. William A. Haussmann) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science2 reading assignments

1882, with Book V added in 1887. Book III contains a run of sections — roughly §§109–112 — in which the world is de-deified and the apparatus of enduring things, equal cases and causes is treated as a useful invention; two are assigned here and the others repay reading in sequence. The famous §341 belongs to eternal recurrence and is not duplicated. Free edition: Thomas Common's The Joyful Wisdom; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

  • Core · 1882

    The Gay Science §109 · Book III

    A world that is not a machine, an organism, or a plan

    Question: What positive claim is left standing at the end of the list of denials?

    Why it belongs: The conditions for the whole theme, established by subtraction. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §112; Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy".

    Online edition: §109 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1882

    The Gay Science §112 · Book III

    Two things cut out of something that has no joints

    Question: If cause and effect are pieces we isolate, what is the status of the continuum we are said to isolate them from?

    Why it belongs: The section that applies the argument to time's ordinary furniture. Nietzsche's claim is that describing an event as a cause followed by an effect imposes a division on something that does not come divided, and that the explanations natural science offers are descriptions laid over a process rather than accounts of why anything follows from anything. The point for this theme is that succession itself — one thing, then another — is being treated as part of the apparatus rather than as the bare fact underneath it. Note the same reflexive difficulty as elsewhere: a continuum is also a description.

    Read alongside: On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: §112 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra5 reading assignments

1883–85. The book carries most of what the published corpus has to say about the will and the past. Chapters that belong to the existential confrontation — "The Convalescent" above all — are treated on eternal recurrence rather than duplicated here. Free edition trans. Thomas Common, titled Thus Spake Zarathustra, with different chapter titles; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

  • Core · 1883

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, "On Redemption"

    The will's quarrel with what has already happened

    Common: "Redemption." The central phrase about transforming every "it was" differs between Kaufmann and Common; compare rather than quoting one as the other.

    Question: Is the will's inability to go backwards a fact about willing, or about a particular way of willing?

    Why it belongs: The diagnosis the theme turns on. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: III, "On Old and New Tables"; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part II, "Redemption" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Core · 1884

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2

    The gateway, and the two roads nobody has yet followed to the end

    Common: "The Vision and the Enigma." The link opens the chapter; §2 is the second numbered part.

    Question: Why is Zarathustra angry at the dwarf's answer that time is a circle?

    Why it belongs: The most concentrated image of time in the corpus. See the first encounter for the full explanation, and eternal recurrence for the shepherd and the snake.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §56; IV, "The Drunken Song".

    Online edition: Part III, "The Vision and the Enigma" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1884

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, "Before Sunrise"

    Chance defended against purpose

    Common: "Before Sunrise."

    Question: Zarathustra blesses accident and calls purpose a form of servitude. What would follow for someone trying to make plans?

    Why it belongs: The affirmative counterpart of The Gay Science §109, in a very different key. Zarathustra addresses the open sky and praises it for being free of eternal reason, eternal purpose and eternal will; what he calls the heaven of chance is offered as a liberation rather than a loss, and the alternative — a world in which everything has been intended — is named as the real servitude. It is the most attractive statement of the position on this page and therefore the one to press hardest. Blessing contingency in a prayer is not the same as showing that contingency is preferable, and the chapter does not attempt the second.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §109; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part III, "Before Sunrise" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1884

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra III, "On Old and New Tables"

    The demand about the past, restated for someone who must make values

    Common: "Old and New Tables." A long chapter in numbered parts; the demand about redeeming what has been returns in its later parts. Use the contents.

    Question: Does making new values require a different relation to the past than simply living does?

    Why it belongs: The chapter where the problem of "On Redemption" is picked up by someone with work to do. The demand is put as a condition on creating: whoever is to make new tables must be able to redeem what has been rather than merely inherit or reject it, because a value made in resentment of the past carries the resentment into whatever it builds. Read it against the earlier chapter and ask whether anything has been added beyond urgency. The chapter's material on creation and destruction is treated on revaluation of values and will to power.

    Read alongside: II, "On Redemption"; Revaluation of values.

    Online edition: Part III, "Old and New Tables" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1885

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra IV, "The Drunken Song"

    Time affirmed in a song rather than in an argument

    Common: "The Drunken Song." Part IV was first printed privately and its status in the work is debated.

    Question: The roundelay says joy wants eternity. Is that a claim about joy, or a wish expressed in the form of one?

    Why it belongs: The closing lyric, and the place where the theme is stated as something other than a thesis. The midnight bell is counted stroke by stroke and the argument, such as it is, runs through joy: that wanting anything back wants everything back with it, because nothing in a world of becoming comes separately. Assigned as an extension rather than a core reading, because its form is doing most of the work — and because that is worth noticing. A reader who finds it moving should be able to say what, if anything, has been established.

    Read alongside: III, "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2; Eternal recurrence.

    Online edition: Part IV, "The Drunken Song" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil1 reading assignment

1886. One assignment: the only compressed statement of recurrence Nietzsche put into print outside Zarathustra and The Gay Science. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296; the free edition is divided by part, so the link opens the part and you will need Find. Free translation Helen Zimmern; preferred translation Walter Kaufmann.

  • Development · Part III

    Beyond Good and Evil §56 · Part III

    Through pessimism and out the other side

    Part III, "The Religious Mood" (§§45–62). Use Find for the section number.

    Question: The section reaches its formulation by way of a pessimism it says must be thought through to the bottom. What does the route add to the destination?

    Why it belongs: The most compressed published formulation, and the one that shows what Nietzsche thought the thought was for. He describes someone who has looked into the world-denying way of thinking as deeply as possible and, without meaning to, opens their eyes to the opposite ideal — the most world-affirming human being, who wants what has been and is, again, and to all eternity. Two things are worth marking. It arrives as a portrait of a type rather than as a claim about physics, which matches the existential register rather than the cosmological one. And it is reached through pessimism rather than around it, which is the structural point the notebooks' arguments cannot supply.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra III, "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2; Eternal recurrence.

    Online edition: Part III: locate §56 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals2 reading assignments

1887. Neither assignment is about recurrence; both are about what a philosophy of becoming has to clear away and what it has to answer. Free translation Horace B. Samuel; the links open whole essays, so use Find for section numbers. Preferred translation: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale.

  • Complication · Essay I

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I §13

    Whether anything stands behind what happens

    Question: Does the animal analogy do work the argument from grammar does not?

    Why it belongs: A doer/deed complication, not a statement about recurrence. See the first encounter for the full explanation, including the limits of what it establishes.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "'Reason' in Philosophy"; Will to power.

    Online edition: First Essay: locate §13 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · Essay III

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III §28

    The alternative an affirmation of becoming has to answer

    Question: If the ascetic ideal succeeded because it gave suffering a meaning, what does a world without purpose have to offer in its place?

    Why it belongs: The closing section, and the sharpest statement of what is at stake in removing purpose from the world. Nietzsche credits the ascetic ideal with an achievement: it gave human suffering a meaning, and so preserved the capacity to will at all, at the cost of directing that will against the conditions of life. The famous closing formula — that one would rather will nothingness than not will — names the danger a philosophy of pure becoming runs into, since it removes the meaning without supplying a replacement. The Genealogy leaves the alternative unresolved, which is a fair description of where this theme stands.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §56; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Third Essay: locate §28 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

    Why philosophers preferred what does not change

    Why it belongs: The clearest published statement of the position. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

  • Core · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable"

    Six stages, and a fifth one it is easy to stop at

    Ludovici: "How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable." A single page in six numbered stages.

    Question: Stage six removes the apparent world along with the true one. What is the difference between that and stage five?

    Why it belongs: One page, and the most concentrated statement of the theme in the corpus. Nietzsche narrates the idea of a true world behind appearances through six stages — attainable to the wise, promised to the pious, unattainable but consoling, unknown and so no longer obliging, useless and therefore abolished — and then takes the last step: with the true world gone, the apparent world goes too, because it was only ever the shadow the other cast. That final move is what distinguishes this from ordinary scepticism. What remains is not appearance promoted to reality but a world that no longer divides that way, which is as close as the published corpus comes to saying what becoming is. Read the stages as a history of a philosophical habit rather than as a proof.

    Read alongside: "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–6; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: "How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "The Four Great Errors" §§3, 8

    Causes invented after the fact, and a responsibility with nothing to rest on

    Ludovici: "The Four Great Errors." The link opens the chapter; use Find for the section numbers.

    Question: If explanations are often invented to discharge an unpleasant feeling of unfamiliarity, how would one tell a good explanation from a comfortable one?

    Why it belongs: The late working-out of what The Gay Science §112 sketched. Nietzsche argues that causes are habitually supplied after an experience, chosen because they are familiar and relieving rather than because they are shown, and that the whole apparatus of the inner world — will, motive, ego — consists of such inventions. The eighth section then draws the consequence for responsibility: if no one is answerable for being as they are, because there is no cause of the whole to which they could be referred, the concepts of guilt and desert lose their ground. For this theme the point is that removing the substrate has costs and consequences; Nietzsche accepts both, and a reader should see clearly which ones.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §112; Genealogy I §13.

    Online edition: "The Four Great Errors": locate §§3, 8 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "What I Owe to the Ancients" §5

    Becoming affirmed, and traced back to the first book

    Ludovici: "Things I Owe to the Ancients."

    Question: Nietzsche presents this as a position he has held since 1872. Does the early book support the claim?

    Why it belongs: The late affirmative formulation, and Nietzsche's own account of its ancestry. He describes a joy in becoming that includes destruction within it, sets it against both Schopenhauer's conclusion and the discharge-of-emotion reading he attributes to Aristotle, and names The Birth of Tragedy as where his revaluation began. The continuity claim is worth testing rather than accepting: the early book states its position in a metaphysical language the late work rejects, and what persists is a question rather than a doctrine. Also central to eternal recurrence and amor fati.

    Read alongside: The Birth of Tragedy §24; Ecce Homo on The Birth of Tragedy §3.

Ecce Homo2 reading assignments

  • Complication · 1888

    Ecce Homo "Why I Write Such Good Books — The Birth of Tragedy" §3

    An ancestry claimed rather than shown

    Ludovici: "Why I Write Such Excellent Books"; the chapter on the first book sits within it and the link opens that chapter.

    Question: What would count as evidence for the claim that Heraclitus taught something like recurrence?

    Why it belongs: The passage in which Nietzsche suggests that the doctrine of recurrence might already have been taught by Heraclitus, and places his own thought in a line running back through the Stoics. It is assigned as a complication because it is so often quoted as though it settled the question of origins. It settles nothing: it is an author in his last productive months constructing an ancestry, the Heraclitus fragments do not obviously contain the doctrine, and the early essay Nietzsche himself wrote about Heraclitus does not attribute it to him either. See the Heraclitean background.

    Read alongside: Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks; Twilight, "What I Owe to the Ancients" §5.

    The author's gloss on his own dismantling

    Ludovici: "Why I Write Such Excellent Books"; the chapter on Twilight sits within it, and the link opens the containing chapter — use the contents to reach it.

    Question: Does the retrospect emphasize the same things the chapters themselves emphasize?

    Why it belongs: A short retrospect on the book that carries most of this theme's published weight, and the closest the corpus comes to an authorial commentary on it. Read it for what it selects. Notebook material

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Its entries can show what he was working out in private. They do not establish what the published works claim, and they should not be used to settle a question the published works leave open.

References below use the expanded 1,067-section compilation, followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and by the linked Ludovici translation. A manuscript reference is given only where it is securely known. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different dates. Edition guidance.

The notebooks contain Nietzsche's most extended attempts at a cosmological argument, and they are worth reading for that. They do not complete the published recurrence texts and they do not govern them. Three entries are given here as an optional secondary investigation, placed after the published route deliberately: a reader who meets them first will read the books as applications of a system, which is the mistake this page is arranged to prevent. For the published existential confrontation — the demon, the heaviest weight, Zarathustra's crisis — go to eternal recurrence rather than to these.

The first written formulations of the thought belong to notebooks kept around Sils Maria in August 1881. Those are dated notebook entries rather than numbered compilation sections, and this page does not assign compilation numbers to them.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    The Will to Power §1066

    An argument sketched, not completed

    WTP §1066 is an editorial number in the expanded posthumous compilation; it is not an original notebook number. No secure manuscript reference is given here for this entry. Free translation Anthony M. Ludovici.

    Question: What would the argument need in order to work, and does the entry supply it?

    Why it belongs: The fullest form the cosmological argument takes in the compilation: from a finite quantity of force and a finite number of possible states, distributed across a time without beginning or end, to the conclusion that every state must already have occurred and must recur. It is the entry a reader needs if they want to know what the argument actually is. It is also a sketch, and its two load-bearing assumptions — that the quantity of force is finite, and that a state can be counted as the same state twice — are asserted rather than argued. Nietzsche neither completed it nor published it, and the published texts nowhere assert its conclusion.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §56; Eternal recurrence.

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    KSA 11, 38[12] / The Will to Power §1067

    The world written as force without beginning or end

    KSA 11, 38[12] is the securely known manuscript reference for this entry; §1067 is an editorial number in the compilation, not Nietzsche's own. Free translation Anthony M. Ludovici.

    Question: The entry is composed as a crescendo. How much of its force is argument, and how much is arrangement?

    Why it belongs: The most extended picture of a world in pure becoming that Nietzsche wrote anywhere: a quantity of force with no beginning and no end, neither growing nor diminishing, eternally changing and eternally flooding back, a sea of forces that creates and destroys itself without aim. As a formulation of what this page has been calling becoming it is unmatched, and it is the reason the notebooks matter here at all. Its status is equally clear. It is an unpublished note; its position at the very end of the compilation, which makes it read like a conclusion, is an editorial decision rather than Nietzsche's; and the published texts that come nearest to it, Beyond Good and Evil §22 and §36, both mark themselves as interpretation or supposition. Treat it as one attempt to formulate the thought, not as the doctrine the books were leading up to.

    Read alongside: Will to power; Twilight, "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable".

  • Notebook reading · posthumous compilation

    The Will to Power §715

    What happens to value in a world with no fixed states

    An editorial compilation number; no secure manuscript reference is given here for this entry.

    Question: If valuing requires something stable to value, can a philosophy of becoming supply it?

    Why it belongs: One of the working notes on the consequence that most threatens the theme. If there are no enduring things and no end-state, then a value cannot be anchored in what something is or in what it is for; what is left is a point of view, held by something that is itself in process. The entry is useful because it shows Nietzsche pressing the difficulty rather than avoiding it. It is not a solution, and the published works do not supply one either — which is worth knowing before concluding that the theme has an ending.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §28; Perspectivism.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained above.

  • The step most readers miss. Read "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable" and stop at stage five. Then read stage six. Write down what changes, and whether you had been holding on to the apparent world without noticing.
  • Two pages on the same chapter. Read "On the Vision and the Riddle" §2 here, then read it again as the eternal recurrence page assigns it. The text does not change; the question does. Which reading does the chapter itself support better?
  • Fifteen years of one commitment. Put Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks beside "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–6. What has been added since 1873, and what has only changed its vocabulary?
  • The cost of removing purpose. Read Genealogy III §28 and then "Before Sunrise". One says the ascetic ideal gave suffering a meaning; the other blesses a sky with no intention in it. Can both be held at once?

A question to carry forward

"On Redemption" claims that a grievance against the past turns into a moral interpretation of existence. Test it on something specific: take a judgment you hold about how things ought to be, and ask whether any part of it is a complaint about something that cannot now be otherwise.
