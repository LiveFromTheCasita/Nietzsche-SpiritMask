Nietzsche writes about pain in several registers at once: as a historical fact about how societies made people memorable to themselves, as a psychological fact about what human beings have enjoyed, as an interpretive problem about what suffering means, and as an evaluative claim about what a culture organized around abolishing pain has cost. These are different claims and they need different evidence.

The page is organized around keeping them apart, because most of the misreading on this subject comes from running them together. A historical account of how the capacity to make promises was produced is not an endorsement of the methods. A psychological claim that people have taken pleasure in cruelty is not a recommendation to be cruel. An argument that the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning at great cost is not a claim that meaningless suffering would be better. And the evaluative claims — that pity is a danger, that comfort has produced a smaller kind of person — are the most contestable material on the page and are marked as such wherever they appear.

The centre of the theme is the second essay of the Genealogy, which gives the account of bad conscience: the proposal that when the instincts of an animal are prevented from discharging outward by the enclosure of society, they turn inward, and that this internalization is where the soul, and eventually guilt, came from. Read it alongside the third essay, which is a separate argument about a separate mechanism — the ascetic priest's interpretation of suffering, which supplies a culprit and so makes pain bearable by making the sufferer guilty. Nietzsche connects the two. He does not identify them, and neither should a reader.

One warning about the most quoted sentence on this subject. Beyond Good and Evil §229 says that almost everything called higher culture rests on the spiritualization and deepening of cruelty. In its chapter it is an argument about what has happened to an impulse when it is turned on the self and on one's own convictions — including the philosopher's willingness to be honest against his own preferences. Quoted alone it reads as a licence. The section is assigned here in full context for that reason.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves from the middle-period groundwork, through the analysis of pity, to the two central essays of the Genealogy, then to the evaluative claims and the late polemic. Continue through the fuller route, which is organized by the five separate claims this theme contains, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook readings are a separate optional investigation.

Essential first encounter

Six assignments, read in order. The sequence puts the descriptive and historical material first and the evaluative claims last, so that the arguments can be assessed before the verdicts that are built on them. The two Genealogy essays are the centre and are assigned separately, because they analyse different mechanisms.

  1. Earlier formulation · 1881

    Daybreak §§18, 215

    Suffering undertaken on purpose, and what it buys

    Kennedy titles §215 "The Morality of Victims." The free edition is The Dawn of Day.

    Question: In both sections someone accepts suffering voluntarily. What is each of them getting in exchange, and who is meant to notice?

    Why it belongs: §18 examines voluntary suffering within the morality of custom: self-inflicted pain as a way of making an impression, of acquiring standing, and of exercising power — over spectators, over gods, over oneself. §215 turns the same analysis on enthusiastic self-sacrifice, and reads it as satisfying a drive in the person performing it rather than as the pure abnegation it presents itself as. Together they establish the method the Genealogy will use at full strength: ask what a painful practice does for whoever undertakes it. Note that both readings are hard to confirm or disconfirm from outside, and that this is a real limitation of the psychology rather than a detail.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§1–15; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Book I (trans. J. M. Kennedy; §215 is in Book III — use the contents or Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  2. Development · Book I (1882)

    The Gay Science §338

    What the person who pities is actually responding to

    Kaufmann: The will to suffer and those who feel pity.

    Question: Nietzsche says the pitying person sees only the surface of another's distress. What would it take to know whether he is right about that?

    Why it belongs: The sharpest middle-period statement of the case against pity as a moral principle. Nietzsche argues that a person's path of suffering is their own and unknown to the onlooker, that pity responds to what is visible and wants it removed, and that removing it may take with it what the sufferer's own way required. The section is important here because it is the argument, not the polemic: the later one-line attacks in Twilight and The Antichrist presuppose it. It is also where the strongest objection can be seen most clearly — the claim requires that the pitying person is systematically mistaken about what the sufferer needs, and Nietzsche offers no way of checking that from outside either.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §13; Zarathustra II, "On the Pitying".

    Online edition: Book IV (trans. Thomas Common; opens nearby — use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§1–15

    How an animal was made able to promise, and what it cost

    Free edition (Samuel): the Preface and each of the three essays are numbered separately.

    Question: Nietzsche derives guilt from debt and punishment from exchange. Which steps in the derivation are philological, which are historical, and which are conjecture?

    Why it belongs: This assigned span traces promise-making and memory through painful training, connects the vocabulary of guilt to debt and creditor–debtor relations, and examines punishment and pleasure in suffering. The racial and sexist comparisons in §7 are part of the passage's difficulty and require scrutiny, not adoption as evidence. §§12–13 distinguish the origin of a practice from its later purposes; §§14–15 question whether punishment produces guilt. Internalization is a later hypothesis, developed in §§16–18, beyond this assignment; continue with those sections in the fuller route on bad conscience. Read §§1–15 in order and ask which steps rest on evidence and which on conjecture.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§11–22; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay II (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§11–22

    A meaning supplied for pain, and the price of the supply

    Question: The priest changes the direction of resentment rather than removing the suffering. Why does Nietzsche treat that as a genuine achievement?

    Why it belongs: A separate mechanism from the one in Essay II, and it needs to be read as separate. Nietzsche describes the ascetic priest as the physician of a sick herd who cannot cure the sickness but can make it endurable: by changing the direction of resentment, by prescribing regimens and small joys and mechanical activity, and above all by giving the sufferer a culprit — himself. What was unbearable was never pain as such but pain without meaning, and the priestly interpretation supplies one. Nietzsche's tone here is worth attending to: he calls the priest's work a triumph and treats him as the genuine saviour of the herd, while arguing that the cure deepened the illness. The mixture of admiration and diagnosis is not a lapse.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §28; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Essay III §§11 onwards (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  5. Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §§225, 229, 270

    The evaluative claims, stated at full strength

    §§225 and 229 are in Part VII, "Our Virtues"; §270 is in Part IX, "What Is Noble?" Section numbers run continuously across the book's nine parts.

    Question: §229 says the cruelty has been refined rather than abolished. Is that a claim about what people do, or about how to describe what they do?

    Why it belongs: These are the evaluative sections, and they are the ones this page treats as most contestable. §225 sets happiness against greatness and argues that what has made the human being deep is precisely what a morality organized around abolishing suffering would remove. §229 argues that cruelty has not disappeared from higher culture but been turned inward and refined, and includes the seeker after knowledge who forces his spirit to recognize what he would rather not — a reading that makes intellectual honesty itself a case of cruelty. §270 observes that deep suffering separates people and that cheerfulness can be a mask. Read them together and read the surrounding sections. Then notice what is not established: that the refinement of cruelty was necessary, that no other route to depth exists, or that a culture should therefore preserve the conditions that produced it. Nietzsche asserts these; the sections argue for none of them.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §188; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; §270 is in Part IX — use Find for the section numbers) · Preferred edition.

  6. Complication · 1888

    Twilight of the Idols "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §38; "Morality as Anti-Nature" §3

    The late compressions, and what compression costs

    Ludovici: "Skirmishes in a War with the Age" and "Morality as the Enemy of Nature." Section numbers restart in each chapter.

    Question: Both sections state in a paragraph what earlier books argued over many pages. What is lost in the compression, and is anything gained?

    Why it belongs: "Skirmishes" §38 is the late statement of the political and cultural version of the claim: Nietzsche's conception of freedom as something measured by the resistance overcome and by what it costs, against the modern institutions he thinks have made people comfortable and small. "Morality as Anti-Nature" §3 argues that a morality which attacks the passions at their root, because it cannot discipline them, is the response of organisms too weak for moderation. Both are worth reading as the late voice at its most quotable and its least argued. The instruction this page gives for them is the one it gives for all the evaluative material: read what the earlier books actually establish, then read these, and keep the difference in view.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The 'Improvers' of Mankind"; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: "Skirmishes" §38 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; for "Morality as the Enemy of Nature" use the contents) · Preferred edition.

Five claims, kept apart

The single most useful thing a reader can do with this material is to keep track of which claim is being made. Nietzsche moves between them quickly and the transitions are often unmarked.

1. Suffering as discipline (a claim about what produced something)
That the capacity to make promises, to sustain a long project, to keep a commitment across time, was produced by harsh training and is not natural to the animal. The evidence is historical and philological, and the argument is in Genealogy II §§1–3.
2. Cruelty as psychology (a claim about what people have enjoyed)
That watching and inflicting suffering has been a source of pleasure, that this is not an aberration, and that the pleasure did not disappear but changed form. Genealogy II §§5–7 and Beyond Good and Evil §229. This is descriptive, and it is the claim most often quoted as if it were a recommendation.
3. Bad conscience (a claim about a mechanism)
That instincts prevented from discharging outward turn inward, and that this internalization is the origin of inner depth and eventually of guilt. Genealogy II §§16–18. Note that Nietzsche gives bad conscience an origin in the enclosure of society, before and independently of the slave revolt; the two are connected later, not identified.
4. The ascetic interpretation (a claim about meaning)
That the priest made suffering bearable by giving it a cause in the sufferer's own guilt, and that this interpretation succeeded because meaningless suffering was the real problem. Genealogy III §§11–22 and §28. This is an interpretive-historical claim about an institution, distinct from all three above.
5. The evaluative claims (what Nietzsche wants said about all this)
That pity elevated to a principle is a danger; that a culture organized around the abolition of pain produces a diminished kind of person; that what is valuable in human beings was bought at a price a modern reader would refuse to pay. Beyond Good and Evil §225, Twilight, The Antichrist §7. These do not follow from claims 1–4. Someone could accept every descriptive claim on this page and reject every evaluative one.

Two things that are not on the list, because Nietzsche does not argue for them. He does not argue that suffering is good for people in general, or that an individual should seek out pain; the disciplinary claim is about what has historically produced certain capacities, not a prescription for a life. And he does not argue that one should be cruel to others: the passages about the refinement of cruelty are about what has happened to an impulse, and the ones he most admires are about what a person does to their own convictions. Reading the first four claims as advice is the standard way this theme goes wrong, and it goes wrong in a direction the texts do not support.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Five investigations, one for each of the claims above. Each assignment opens its full explanation and reading links in the work survey below. Read the assignments within a group in order.

1. Discipline: what was produced, and how

Follow the claim that certain capacities were made rather than given, and ask at each step what would count as evidence.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§1–3
    Breeding an animal with the right to make promises; memory burned in with pain.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §188
    The long constraint that Nietzsche says produced everything free on earth, stated as a general thesis.
  3. Twilight of the Idols — "The 'Improvers' of Mankind"
    The same claim turned against moral improvement: taming described as weakening.
  4. Human, All Too Human — §107
    The naturalist argument pushed to complete irresponsibility, and Nietzsche calling the conclusion bitter.

2. Cruelty as a psychological fact

The descriptive claim, and the places where it is most likely to be misread as something else.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§5–7
    Festival cruelty, and the argument that the sight of suffering was once simply enjoyed.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §229
    The refinement of cruelty, including the knower's cruelty toward his own preferences.
  3. Human, All Too Human — §99
    Separating what is done from the evaluation attached to it, as a method rather than a verdict.
  4. Homer's Contest
    An institution described as channelling destructive energy rather than removing it.

3. Bad conscience: the mechanism

The most consequential single piece of moral psychology in the corpus. Test whether the account can stand on its own.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§16–18
    Instincts turned inward under enclosure; the soul as the space that opens.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§19–22
    Debt to ancestors becoming debt to a god, and guilt made irredeemable.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§24–25
    The passage where Nietzsche imagines redemption from it — and then breaks off.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, "On the Pale Criminal"
    The same mechanism dramatized, and the deed misread by the doer.

4. The ascetic interpretation of suffering

A separate argument with a separate object. Follow the priest's solution and then the cost Nietzsche says it carried.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§11–22
    The physician who changes the direction of resentment and supplies a culprit.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §28
    Meaningless suffering named as the real problem, and the will to nothingness as the solution.
  3. The Antichrist — §22
    The same analysis applied to a specific institution's organization of guilt.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§1–5
    The ascetic ideal outside religion, in artists and elsewhere, before the priestly case.

5. The evaluative claims, examined

The contestable material. Read the strongest statements and then ask what in the earlier essays actually supports them.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §225
    Happiness against greatness, and pity for the creature set against pity for the creator.
  2. The Gay Science — §338
    The argued version, on which the later one-line attacks depend.
  3. The Antichrist — §7
    Pity called the practice of nihilism: the claim at its most compressed and least defended.
  4. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, "On the Pitying"
    The dramatic version, and the complication Part IV adds to it.

Keep these distinctions in view

Bad conscience and the slave revolt
The first essay describes a revaluation carried out by the powerless; the second describes instincts turning inward under social enclosure. Nietzsche gives them different origins and different mechanisms. Keep three further steps distinct: internalization in II §§16–18, the development and moralization of religious debt in II §§19–22, and the ascetic priest's redirection of suffering toward guilt in III §§15 and 20. Their relations need to be traced; the slave revolt is not a single explanation for all of them.
Pain and meaningless pain
Genealogy III §28 is explicit that what the human being objected to was not suffering but suffering without a reason. Every part of the theme depends on this: the priest's success, the ascetic ideal's durability, and whatever Nietzsche thinks might replace them.
Describing an enjoyment and endorsing it
The claim that watching suffering has been pleasurable is a claim about human beings, offered with historical examples. Nietzsche thinks the denial of it is dishonest. Neither the claim nor the charge of dishonesty tells anyone to do anything.
Cruelty toward others and cruelty toward oneself
Beyond Good and Evil §229 includes cruelty turned inward through asceticism and the discipline of knowing, as well as pleasure in others' suffering. That distinction does not confine Nietzsche's approvals to self-discipline: §§257–259 make harsh claims about hierarchy, exploitation, and the costs imposed on others. Ask in each passage whether he is describing a practice, valuing it, or defending it.
Pity as a feeling and pity as a principle
The target is the elevation of pity to the highest moral value, a position Nietzsche associates with Schopenhauer and with the moral culture of his century. Passages attacking that principle are not straightforwardly advice about how to treat a particular person in distress, and reading them that way makes the argument both stupider and easier to dismiss.
Suffering as condition and suffering as recommendation
That depth was historically produced by hardship does not by itself show that an individual should seek it. Yet the published work also makes evaluative demands: Beyond Good and Evil §225 opposes the abolition of suffering and calls for its increase in connection with higher possibilities. Read the demand alongside the historical account, without turning either into a universal prescription. See the notebook readings.
Nietzsche's explanation and Nietzsche's verdict
The Genealogy gives an account of how the moral world was made and then passes judgment on it. The account could be right and the judgment wrong, or the reverse. The page keeps the two apart wherever they appear in the same section, because in the strongest passages they arrive in the same sentence.

The early work2 reading assignments

Two texts from 1872: the first published book, and a short piece Nietzsche did not publish. Both concern how Greek culture handled what it did not deny. The free Birth of Tragedy is translated by William A. Haussmann; "Homer's Contest" is in a different volume, translated by Maximilian A. Mügge.

  • Earlier formulation

    The Birth of Tragedy §3

    The wisdom of Silenus, and what was built over it

    Haussmann's free edition; Kaufmann is the preferred translation.

    Question: If the Olympian gods are a response to the terror rather than an ignorance of it, what does that make Greek cheerfulness?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reports the saying of Silenus — that the best is not to have been born and the second best to die soon — and argues that the Greek gods were created as a bright counter-image by a people who knew exactly that, so that the serenity is an achievement over terror rather than an absence of it. It is the earliest Nietzschean answer to the problem this theme is about, and it is an aesthetic answer rather than a moral one. Read it here as background: the tragedy and art page carries the argument about art, and this page takes only the claim about what suffering required.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §28; Tragedy and art.

    Online edition: The Birth of Tragedy (trans. William A. Haussmann; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Homer's Contest 1872, unpublished

    Destructive energy given an institution

    Mügge: Homer's Contest, in Early Greek Philosophy & Other Essays.

    Question: The contest is described as keeping rivalry productive. What makes an institution capable of that, and what happens when it fails?

    Why it belongs: The essay argues that the Greeks did not abolish the cruelty and the will to destroy in their nature but built contests in which it produced something, and that the removal of rivals was treated as a danger rather than a victory. It is the earliest version of the pattern Beyond Good and Evil §229 will describe as the refinement of cruelty, and it makes the claim institutional rather than psychological — which is a different argument and, on the face of it, an easier one to test. Unpublished, early, and about Greek practice rather than about modern life.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §229; Greatness and the agon.

    Online edition: Homer's Contest (trans. Maximilian A. Mügge) · Edition guidance.

Human, All Too Human2 reading assignments

The 1878 book's second chapter, "On the History of the Moral Sensations," is where the apparatus the Genealogy will use at full strength is first assembled. The tone is analytic and the conclusions are more tentative. Preferred edition: Hollingdale.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §99

    Prising an act apart from its moral colouring

    Zimmern: The Innocent Side of So-called Evil Actions.

    Question: What is left of an action when the evaluation attached to it is set aside, and is anything lost in the setting aside?

    Why it belongs: The operation this whole theme depends on: separating what was done from the moral judgment fastened to it, so that the judgment becomes a separate thing to be explained. Without it, the claims about cruelty in Genealogy II cannot even be stated, because "cruelty" is already a verdict. Read it as method rather than as a position about the acts themselves.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§5–7; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: §99 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §107

    Irresponsibility, and how hard it is to hold

    Zimmern: Irresponsibility and Innocence.

    Question: If nobody is responsible, what happens to punishment — and what happens to the Genealogy's indictment?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche pushes the naturalist argument through to the conclusion that nobody is responsible for anything, calls the consequence bitter, and does not pretend it is easy to live with. It belongs here because the whole theme rests on it: if the history of punishment is the history of something inflicted on beings who could not have done otherwise, the descriptive claims get harder and the evaluative ones get harder still. The section is also the best evidence that Nietzsche knew the cost of his own position in 1878.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: §107 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak2 reading assignments

The 1881 book, and the most patient treatment of this material in the corpus. Preferred translation: Hollingdale; the free edition is The Dawn of Day, translated by J. M. Kennedy.

  • Earlier formulation

    Daybreak §§18, 215

    Suffering undertaken on purpose, and what it buys

    Question: What is each sufferer getting in exchange, and who is meant to notice?

    Why it belongs: The method of the theme, established on voluntary suffering and on sacrifice. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§1–15; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Book I (trans. J. M. Kennedy; §215 is in Book III — use the contents or Find) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §174

    A morality built around not being hurt

    Question: If a community's morality reflects what it most fears, what does a morality of caution tell you about the community?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche locates fear and a demand for collective security beneath a prevailing morality of sympathy, and questions the ambition to remove every rough edge from life. It is the groundwork for what Beyond Good and Evil §225 later says at much higher volume, and it is worth reading first precisely because the later version is so much less careful. The herd and the last man page carries this line of argument further.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §225; The herd and the last man.

The Gay Science2 reading assignments

The sharpest middle-period statements on pity, and the place where the argument against it is actually made rather than asserted.

  • Development · Book I (1882)

    The Gay Science §13

    Benefiting and hurting as two ways of doing the same thing

    Kaufmann: On the doctrine of the feeling of power. Common: The Theory of the Sense of Power.

    Question: If helping and harming can both express a feeling of power, what distinguishes them?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche argues that we benefit and hurt people in order to have an effect on them, and that which we choose depends on circumstance and on how much power we already have over them — with the uncomfortable implication that kindness and cruelty can be two expressions of one impulse. It is the psychological groundwork for the treatment of pity, and it is the claim that makes the theme genuinely difficult, since it applies as readily to Nietzsche's own writing as to anyone's.

    Read alongside: §338; Will to power.

    Online edition: §13 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · Book IV (1882)

    The Gay Science §338

    What the person who pities is actually responding to

    Question: How could anyone check whether the onlooker has misread what the sufferer needs?

    Why it belongs: The argued case against pity as a principle, on which the late one-line attacks depend. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra II, "On the Pitying"; The Antichrist §7.

    Online edition: Book IV (trans. Thomas Common; opens nearby — use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra3 reading assignments

The dramatic version. Zarathustra is most useful here for the two places where it makes the position harder rather than clearer: the speech against pity, and the ending in which Zarathustra's own pity is the last thing he has to overcome.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part I, "On the Pale Criminal"

    A deed misread by the one who did it

    Common: The Pale Criminal.

    Question: Zarathustra says the criminal's image made him pale after the act, not before. What does that reverse?

    Why it belongs: The speech describes someone whose deed was one thing and whose account of it afterwards is another, and who then suffers under an interpretation he has accepted from his judges. It dramatizes the mechanism the Genealogy will analyse: the sufferer taking on an explanation of his own suffering that makes him guilty. It also states the theme's least comfortable practical implication — that the language of justice may be revenge with better manners — which the submitted essay Justice Without Revenge? examines at length.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§19–22; The psychology of morality.

    Online edition: Part I, "The Pale Criminal" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part II, "On the Pitying"

    Shame, and what great love does instead

    Common: The Pitiful.

    Question: The objection here is about the shame pity inflicts. Is that the same objection as §338's, or a different one?

    Why it belongs: The central Zarathustra speech on the subject, and it makes a different argument from The Gay Science: the charge is that being pitied is humiliating, that the pitying person takes something from the one they pity, and that a great love goes beyond its pity because it still wants to create the beloved. It also contains the line about all creators being hard. Read the whole speech and notice how much of it is about the sufferer's dignity rather than about the pitier's motives.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §338; Part IV, "The Cry of Distress" and "The Sign".

    Online edition: Part II, "The Pitiful" (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra Part IV, "The Cry of Distress" and "The Sign"

    Pity as the last temptation of the one who attacked it

    Common: "The Cry of Distress" and "The Sign." Part IV was first printed privately and its status in the work is debated.

    Question: Zarathustra's final test is his pity for the higher men. What does it mean that the book ends by making this its hardest problem?

    Why it belongs: The soothsayer tells Zarathustra that his last sin is his pity for the higher man, and the book's closing chapter has him overcome it and leave. It belongs here as the strongest internal complication of the whole polemic: the character who has attacked pity for three parts is shown to be vulnerable to it, and the overcoming is staged as a genuine difficulty rather than a position already held. Part IV's uncertain status makes its evidential weight uncertain too, which is worth holding onto rather than resolving.

    Read alongside: Part II, "On the Pitying"; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part IV, "The Sign" (trans. Thomas Common; use the contents for "The Cry of Distress") · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil4 reading assignments

The evaluative statements, and the general thesis about constraint that they rest on. Section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296 across the book's nine parts.

  • Core of the disciplinary claim

    Beyond Good and Evil §188

    The long unfreedom of the spirit

    Part V, "On the Natural History of Morals."

    Question: The examples are metrical rules, rhetoric and moral codes. Does the argument work as well for the third as for the first two?

    Why it belongs: The general thesis behind everything else on this page: that every morality is a long compulsion, that what is valuable and refined on earth — in art, in thought, in manners — grew under arbitrary and severe constraint, and that the constraint was not a means chosen for the end but the condition under which the end appeared at all. It is the most explicit version of the disciplinary claim, and the place to test it, since the inference from poetic metre to social hierarchy is doing a great deal of work in a very short space.

    Read alongside: Twilight, "The 'Improvers' of Mankind"; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §225

    Happiness set against greatness

    Part VII, "Our Virtues."

    Question: What is the difference Nietzsche draws between pity for the creature in a person and pity for the creator, and what does it license?

    Why it belongs: The evaluative claim at its strongest. Nietzsche objects to both hedonism and pessimism as moralities that make suffering the measure of value, insists that the discipline of great suffering is what has produced every elevation of the human being so far, and distinguishes the pity that wants to relieve the creature from a pity directed at what a person might become. It is the passage most often used to make Nietzsche sound like a straightforward apologist for hardship, and the guard against that is to notice how much of the section is about not knowing in advance what a person's suffering is for.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §338; Daybreak §174.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Beyond Good and Evil §229

    Cruelty refined rather than removed

    Part VII, "Our Virtues."

    Question: Nietzsche ends by turning the analysis on the knower. Does that strengthen the claim or narrow it?

    Why it belongs: The most quoted and most abused section on this subject. Nietzsche argues that what earlier ages enjoyed openly has not gone away but been made spiritual and subtle; that tragic pity, the religious emotions and the disinterested contemplation of beauty all have it in them; and that the seeker after knowledge, forcing his spirit to know against its inclination, is practising cruelty on himself. The turn to the knower at the end is the part most often cut, and it is the part that determines what the section is claiming: the refinement Nietzsche admires is chiefly self-directed. Read the whole section and the ones around it before using the famous sentence for anything.

    Read alongside: Homer's Contest; Perspectivism.

    Online edition: Part VII (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §270

    What deep suffering does to a person's distance from others

    Part IX, "What Is Noble?"

    Question: If profound suffering separates, what follows for the practice of pitying — and for the practice of being pitied?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche writes that deep suffering makes noble and separates, that those who have suffered profoundly know things the others cannot, and that one of the subtlest disguises is the cheerfulness such people wear. It closes the sequence begun by §225 and it changes its character: the objection to pity here is not that it weakens the sufferer but that it presumes an understanding the pitier does not have. That is a more defensible claim than the polemical versions, and it is rarely the one that gets quoted.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §338; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Part IX (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals7 reading assignments

The theme's centre. Read the second essay for the mechanism and the third for the interpretation; they are separate arguments and the page treats them that way. The free edition numbers the Preface and each essay separately, and includes Nietzsche's closing Note after Essay I §17.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§1–15

    How an animal was made able to promise, and what it cost

    Question: Which steps are philological, which historical, which conjecture?

    Why it belongs: The analytical core of the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §99; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay II (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core of the bad-conscience claim

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§16–18

    Instincts turned inward, and the space that opens

    Question: Nietzsche calls bad conscience an illness, and also the source of everything interesting about human beings. Are both claims being made in the same sense?

    Why it belongs: The mechanism stated precisely: when the enclosure of society blocked the outward discharge of instinct, the instincts turned back against their possessor, and this internalization is what produced the human soul. Nietzsche calls it a serious illness and in the same sections says the earth became an event to be interested in because of it, and that the active bad conscience is the womb of ideal and imaginative phenomena. Note what the account does not yet contain: guilt. That arrives when the mechanism is given a religious interpretation, which is §§19–22.

    Read alongside: §§19–22; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: Essay II §§16–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§19–22

    A debt that cannot be paid

    Question: Why does Nietzsche think the Christian version is the maximum of the phenomenon rather than a departure from it?

    Why it belongs: The sections trace the debtor's obligation to ancestors, its growth as the community grows, its transformation into a debt owed to a god, and finally the point where the creditor pays the debt himself out of love — which Nietzsche calls the stroke of genius of Christianity and also the point at which guilt becomes irredeemable. This is where the mechanism of §§16–18 acquires its moral content, and it is the hinge between this theme and the critique of Christianity. The etymological argument connecting Schuld and Schulden carries a lot of the weight and is worth examining rather than accepting.

    Read alongside: Essay III §§11–22; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Essay II §§19–22 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§24–25

    Redemption imagined, and then withdrawn

    Question: Nietzsche breaks off and says the task is not his. What does the interruption do to the essay's argument?

    Why it belongs: The essay's closing chord: the imagined man of the future who would redeem us from the ideal built out of bad conscience, and who would give the earth back its purpose — followed immediately by Nietzsche stopping himself and saying he has no right to speak here. It is the only affirmative gesture in the essay, and it is deliberately unfinished. Often skipped; it should not be, because it is the clearest evidence that the Genealogy's diagnosis was not meant to arrive with a cure attached.

    Read alongside: Essay III §28; Overman.

    Online edition: Essay II §§24–25 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§1–5

    The ideal outside religion, before the priest appears

    Question: Why does Nietzsche begin with artists and Wagner rather than with the priest he is actually after?

    Why it belongs: The essay opens by asking what ascetic ideals mean in a range of cases — artists, philosophers, women, the physiologically failed — and works through Wagner and Schopenhauer before arriving at the priest. The opening matters for this theme because it establishes that the ascetic interpretation of suffering is not a religious invention that could simply be dropped: it appears wherever a form of life needs its own conditions made meaningful. Read it to see the scope of the claim before the priestly case narrows it.

    Read alongside: Essay III §§11–22; Wagner and decadence.

    Online edition: Essay III §§1–5 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§11–22

    A meaning supplied for pain, and the price of the supply

    Question: Why is changing the direction of resentment an achievement rather than a trick?

    Why it belongs: The ascetic priest's interpretation of suffering, treated as a separate mechanism from bad conscience. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Essay III §28; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Essay III §§11 onwards (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §28

    The problem was never the pain

    Question: If the objection was to meaningless suffering, what would a successful alternative to the ascetic ideal have to provide?

    Why it belongs: The book's last section, and the sentence that organizes the whole theme: what the human being suffered from was not suffering but the absence of an answer to the question why. The ascetic ideal supplied one, at the cost of making the sufferer guilty, and it prevailed because there was no competitor. Read it as the statement of the problem any affirmative position has to solve, and read the closing formula — willing nothingness rather than not willing — as a description of what the alternative to meaning has looked like so far.

    Read alongside: Nihilism; Amor fati.

    Online edition: Essay III §28 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Twilight of the Idols "The 'Improvers' of Mankind"

    Taming described as making sick

    Ludovici: "The 'Improvers' of Mankind."

    Question: Nietzsche distinguishes taming from breeding and rejects both as "improvement." What is the standard he is measuring them against?

    Why it belongs: The chapter argues that moral improvement has in practice meant either taming — which it describes as weakening and making sick, with the caged animal as the image — or the breeding of a particular type, and it uses the Indian law codes as its case. It gives the disciplinary claim its harshest form and it is where the reader can see most clearly what Nietzsche means by it and what he does not: the objection is not to constraint but to constraint that produces a diminished animal while calling itself moral improvement. The historical material is polemical and secondhand and should be treated as an illustration of the argument rather than as evidence for it.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §188; Revaluation.

    Online edition: "The 'Improvers' of Mankind" (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Antichrist2 reading assignments

The polemic at full intensity. The free translation is by H. L. Mencken; the course follows Kaufmann. The wider argument about Christianity belongs to the critique of Christianity page; only the claims about suffering and pity are taken here.

  • Complication

    The Antichrist §7

    Pity called the practice of nihilism

    Question: The section makes a claim about what pity does to vitality. What kind of evidence would bear on it?

    Why it belongs: The most compressed statement of the evaluative position: pity as depressive in its effect, as preserving what is ripe for destruction, and as the practice of nihilism. It is included because it is what most readers encounter first and because it is the least supported version of the argument — The Gay Science §338 and Beyond Good and Evil §270 make the case that this section asserts. The physiological language is doing rhetorical rather than empirical work, and the claim about what pity costs a person or a culture is nowhere measured.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §338; Nihilism.

    Online edition: §7 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    The Antichrist §22

    The organization of guilt as an institution

    Question: How much of this section is the Genealogy's argument restated, and how much is new?

    Why it belongs: The application of the ascetic-interpretation analysis to a specific institutional history: suffering rendered interpretable by an apparatus that requires the sufferer to be at fault. Read it against Genealogy III §§11–22 and note what has changed — almost nothing in the analysis, a great deal in the temperature. The comparison is a good way to see how much of the late work is argument and how much is the same argument delivered as attack.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§11–22; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: §§20–23 (trans. H. L. Mencken; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

The Will to Power: notebook readings

Seven numbered entries in three groups. The notebooks return to this material repeatedly, and on two points they go further than anything Nietzsche published: they state the evaluative claims more baldly, and they come closer than the books do to recommending hardship rather than explaining it. Both are reasons to read them carefully, and reasons not to let them stand in for the published arguments.

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. These experiments can sharpen a distinction without supplying the final account, and where the two diverge the published text governs.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. They are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different times.

Preferred edition: Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale, edited by Kaufmann. The numbered links open Anthony M. Ludovici's translation at a nearby printed page. Edition guidance.

Cruelty with nowhere to go3 assignments · 3 entries

The notebook versions of the bad-conscience argument, and of the account of punishment that goes with it. Read each against Genealogy II, which is more careful.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §294

    Conscience, self-accusation, and a reversal of guilt

    Question: If the feeling of conscience is a reversal of an earlier direction, what was it pointing at first?

    Why it belongs: The entry treats the experience of conscience and self-accusation as the turning-around of something that was once directed outward, rather than as the perception of a fault. It is the notebook form of the claim Genealogy II §§16–18 makes at length, and reading it first makes the published version's care visible: the essay builds a historical setting for the reversal, while the note simply asserts it.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18; The psychology of morality.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §376

    When blocked aggression turns inward

    Question: Does the account require that the quantity of aggression be conserved? Is that assumption doing work?

    Why it belongs: The entry describes what happens to an impulse that cannot be discharged outward and has to be spent on its possessor instead. It is the mechanism in its barest form, and the bareness exposes the assumption the published essay half-conceals: that the drive persists undiminished and must go somewhere. That is a piece of nineteenth-century psychological physics, and it is worth asking how much of the Genealogy depends on it.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18; Body and physiology.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §738

    Punishment, shame, and the possibility of return

    Question: If punishment marks a person permanently, what has the community actually decided?

    Why it belongs: The entry considers what punishment does beyond whatever it is supposed to achieve — the shame it attaches, and whether the punished person can be received back. It extends Genealogy II §§12–15 into a practical question the essay raises and does not answer, and it is the notebook material closest to a constructive proposal on this theme. The submitted essay Justice Without Revenge? takes up the same question at length.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra I, "On the Pale Criminal"; Master and slave morality.

The dispute about pity2 assignments · 2 entries

Two entries that complicate the polemic, including one that makes a concession the published attacks do not.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §366

    The dispute over pity

    Question: Which of the several objections to pity in the corpus does this entry actually make?

    Why it belongs: The entry sets out the disagreement about pity as a disagreement rather than a verdict, which is useful because the published late work rarely does. Read it as a map of the positions Nietzsche is moving between — pity as weakness, as an exercise of power, as an insult to the sufferer, as a genuine danger to the one who feels it — and then check which of them The Antichrist §7 is relying on.

    Read alongside: The Gay Science §338; The Antichrist §7.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §983

    Commanding strength and the capacity for pity

    Question: If strength can afford pity, is pity still the target — or only pity in the weak?

    Why it belongs: The entry associates commanding strength with a capacity for pity rather than with its absence, which cuts directly against the flattest reading of the published polemic. It does not withdraw the attack; it relocates it, so that what is objectionable is pity in someone who cannot afford it. Whether that distinction can be made to hold — and whether it is anything more than a way of exempting the people Nietzsche approves of — is the useful question to take away.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §270; Master and slave morality.

What suffering is worth2 assignments · 2 entries

The two entries where the evaluative question is put most directly, and where the distance between explanation and recommendation is narrowest.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1052

    Two meanings of suffering

    Question: The same suffering is read two ways. Is the difference in the suffering, in the sufferer, or only in the interpretation?

    Why it belongs: The entry contrasts suffering taken as an objection to existence with suffering taken as belonging to it, using the figures of the crucified and of Dionysus. It states the alternative that Genealogy III §28 sets up, and it states it as a choice between interpretations rather than as a discovery about pain. Read it with the published essay to see what the books supply that the note does not: an account of why one interpretation prevailed.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §28; Critique of Christianity.

  • Notebook reading · Book IV

    The Will to Power §1041

    Affirming the difficult features of existence

    Question: What would distinguish affirming what is questionable and terrible from simply enduring it?

    Why it belongs: The entry describes the affirmative position as one that says yes to what is questionable and terrible in existence rather than in spite of it. It is the closest the notebooks come to turning the analysis of suffering into a task, and it is where this theme hands over to amor fati. It is also where the risk on this page is highest: read carelessly, the entry becomes the advice that suffering is good for you, which is not what it says and not what any published passage says.

    Read alongside: Amor fati; Nihilism.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • Two mechanisms, not one. Read Genealogy II §§16–18 and then III §§11–22, and write down where the second depends on the first and where it does not. The two are routinely merged; the merge is what makes the argument look tighter than it is.
  • The famous sentence in context. Read Beyond Good and Evil §229 whole, then Homer's Contest. Is the claim about a psychological impulse, or about what institutions can do with one?
  • The argument and the attack. Compare The Gay Science §338 with The Antichrist §7. Six years apart, the same target. What has been added, and what has been dropped?
  • The objection Nietzsche staged himself. Follow Zarathustra II, "On the Pitying" into Part IV. Why does the book make pity the last thing its protagonist has to overcome?

A question to carry forward

Suppose the historical claim is right and the capacities we value were produced by cruelty. What, if anything, follows for how a society should be arranged now — and what would you need to know before answering?
