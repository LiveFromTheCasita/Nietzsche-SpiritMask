If we do not reliably know why we act, what happens to a morality built on judging intentions?

Nietzsche's work here is not primarily a moral position but a method of reading moral phenomena: treating a judgment, a feeling of obligation, or a sense of guilt as something with causes that the person holding it does not have access to. Daybreak states the difficulty at its starkest — however far self-knowledge goes, nothing is more incomplete than a person's picture of the drives that constitute him, and experience mostly supplies nourishment to drives already at work rather than information about them.

From that starting point several claims follow, and they are worth keeping apart. One is about the self: what we call the will is a relation among many drives rather than a single faculty issuing commands. A second is about motive: the reason a person gives for an act is itself a phenomenon to be explained, not evidence about what produced the act. A third is about specific moral formations: ressentiment, bad conscience, the ascetic ideal — mechanisms by which a feeling that cannot discharge outward is turned somewhere else and given a meaning.

None of this is a moral theory, and Nietzsche's claim that psychology should again become the path to the fundamental problems is a claim about method rather than a result. The material also ranges from careful description to confident speculation, and the difference is frequently invisible in the prose. Where a passage is proposing a mechanism rather than reporting one, this guide says so.

Begin with six readings. The first encounter moves from the opacity of the drives, through the transformation of an impulse by moral judgment, to the multiplicity of the will and the two mechanisms the Genealogy analyses in separate essays. Continue through the fuller route to test the account, or use the work survey for a particular book. The notebook material is a separate, optional, and explicitly secondary investigation.

Essential first encounter

Six short assignments, read in order. Ressentiment and bad conscience are given separate steps because they are separate mechanisms in separate essays, and the site keeps that distinction rather than merging them into a single reading on the Genealogy.

  1. Core

    Daybreak §119

    We do not know the drives we are made of

    Kennedy: Experience and Invention.

    Question: If experience mostly feeds the drives rather than informing us about them, what is introspection actually reporting?

    Why it belongs: The foundational section for the whole theme. Nietzsche argues that however far self-knowledge reaches, the catalogue of a person's drives remains incomplete; that the drives are nourished by the day's chance events rather than revealed by them; and that even a dream is the interpretation of a bodily stimulus invented after the fact. Every later claim on this page depends on this one. Note that the argument is itself a hypothesis about a process we cannot observe, which is a difficulty Nietzsche acknowledges here more readily than in the late books.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §§129–130; Beyond Good and Evil §19.

    Online edition: §119 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  2. Core

    Daybreak §§32, 38

    What a moral judgment does to an impulse

    Kennedy: The Brake; Impulses Transformed by Moral Judgments.

    Question: If blame changes the impulse rather than only restraining it, what is a moral judgment doing?

    Why it belongs: §38 states the mechanism directly: the same impulse, under the pressure of the blame custom attaches to it, develops into a different and painful affect — so that moral judgment is not a brake applied to a fixed drive but something that alters what the drive becomes. §32 approaches the same point from the other side, through the shock of discovering that moral suffering rested on an error. Together they show morality operating causally rather than only evaluatively, which is the claim the Genealogy will develop into a history.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18; Daybreak §215.

    Online edition: §§32, 38 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  3. Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§19, 23

    The will as many wills, and psychology as the way in

    Question: §19 says willing involves commanding and obeying at once. Who, on that account, is the one who wills?

    Why it belongs: §19 takes apart the apparently simple act of willing into a plurality of sensations, a thought, and above all an affect of command, and argues that the feeling of freedom in willing comes from identifying with the commanding part while the obeying parts do the work. §23 then proposes that psychology be recognized as the queen of the sciences and the path to the fundamental problems, and concedes that pursuing it means sailing against inherited moral prejudice. Two short sections that supply the theme's model of the self and its statement of method.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §119; Beyond Good and Evil §§13, 17.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  4. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§10–13

    Ressentiment, and the doer invented behind the deed

    Question: §13 says the strong are not free to be weak. If that is right, what work is the language of choice doing in moral judgment?

    Why it belongs: The first of the two mechanisms. §10 defines ressentiment as a valuation that begins by saying no to an outside and only then invents its own yes; §§11–12 track what that does to the concept of the enemy; §13, the lambs and the birds of prey, argues that grammar tempts us to posit a neutral subject behind an activity who could have acted otherwise, and that this fiction is what makes the weak able to credit themselves with their weakness as an achievement. Keep this distinct from bad conscience: it is a different mechanism, in a different essay, with a different object.

    Read alongside: Master and slave morality; Genealogy I §14.

    Online edition: Essay I §§10–13 (trans. Horace B. Samuel; the Preface and each essay are numbered separately) · Preferred edition.

  5. Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§16–18

    Bad conscience as instinct turned inward

    Question: Nietzsche calls bad conscience an illness, and also the source of everything interesting in human beings. How can both be true?

    Why it belongs: The second mechanism, and the more original one. §16 describes the enclosure of human beings within society and peace, so that instincts which could no longer discharge outward turned against their bearer; §17 denies that this was a gradual adaptation; §18 identifies the same force at work in the artist's delight in form, so that self-cruelty becomes the condition of self-making. This is the site's protected distinction in action: bad conscience is not the slave revolt, and reading the two essays as one argument loses what is most interesting in the second. The historical account of a conquering race imposing a form is asserted rather than evidenced, and belongs with the weaker material.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §22; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: Essay II §§16–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  6. Core

    Twilight of the Idols The Four Great Errors §§1–8

    The machinery of moral judgment taken apart

    Question: If the will is not a cause, what is left of praise, blame and responsibility — and does Nietzsche want anything left?

    Why it belongs: The compressed late summary: the confusion of cause and consequence, false causality, imaginary causes, and the error of free will. Nietzsche argues that we invent causes after the fact to make experience familiar, and that the doctrine of the will was devised for the purpose of finding people guilty and punishing them. Read last, it shows the psychology being turned into a weapon; the claim that the will was invented in order to punish is a genealogical assertion, not a demonstrated history.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39; Genealogy as method.

    Online edition: The Four Great Errors (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici; section numbers restart in each chapter) · Preferred edition.

Fuller route: tests and complications

Continue with these five investigations after the first encounter. Each assignment below opens its explanation and reading links in the work survey. Read the assignments within a group in order; the question above them explains what the comparison is meant to test.

1. Can a motive be known?

The theme's hardest problem, and the one on which everything else rests. Ask in each case what evidence a claim about hidden motives could rest on.

  1. Daybreak — §119
    The incompleteness of any catalogue of one's own drives.
  2. Daybreak — §§129–130
    The supposed combat of motives, and the two kingdoms of purpose and chance.
  3. The Gay Science — §335
    Moral self-knowledge treated as typically self-flattering.
  4. The Gay Science — §333
    Knowing described as the outcome of a struggle among impulses.

2. What kind of thing is doing the willing?

If the will is many wills, the grammar of agency needs replacing. Test how far Nietzsche gets, and what he leaves standing.

  1. Beyond Good and Evil — §19
    Commanding and obeying inside a single act.
  2. Beyond Good and Evil — §§13, 17
    A living thing discharging strength; a thought that comes when it wishes.
  3. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Despisers of the Body
    The self behind the "I", in dramatic form.
  4. The Gay Science — §354
    Consciousness as a late and social instrument.

3. The two mechanisms, kept apart

Ressentiment and bad conscience are routinely merged. Read them against each other and state the difference precisely.

  1. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §§10–13
    Valuation that begins with a no to an outside.
  2. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay I §14
    The workshop where ideals are manufactured.
  3. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §§16–18
    Instinct turned inward when it cannot discharge outward.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay II §22
    Guilt raised to a maximum, and the relief that deepens it.

4. The affects as evidence rather than noise

Nietzsche does not treat feeling as an obstacle to judgment. Follow the cases where an affect is read as a source of information.

  1. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — II, On the Tarantulas
    The preachers of equality, and the revenge inside a demand for justice.
  2. Thus Spoke Zarathustra — I, On the Pale Criminal
    An act and the image of it that the doer cannot bear.
  3. Daybreak — §215
    Sacrifice examined as something other than selflessness.
  4. On the Genealogy of Morals — Essay III §§15–18
    The priest redirecting a suffering he cannot cure.

5. Where the psychology becomes polemic

The late books turn the method on named targets. Ask what is gained in force and what is given up in care.

  1. Twilight of the Idols — The Problem of Socrates
    A philosopher diagnosed rather than answered.
  2. Twilight of the Idols — Morality as Anti-Nature
    Hostility to the passions read as a symptom.
  3. The Antichrist — §15
    A whole moral vocabulary classified as imaginary.
  4. Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Wise §6
    Freedom from ressentiment claimed, in the first person.

Keep these distinctions in view

Ressentiment and ordinary resentment
Ressentiment names a valuation that originates in a no to an outside and only afterwards produces its own affirmations. It is not anger at injury, not objection to harm, and not any complaint made by someone weaker. Collapsing it into ordinary resentment turns the analysis into an insult.
Bad conscience and the slave revolt
Two mechanisms, two essays, two objects. The slave revolt is a reversal of valuations directed outward at an enemy; bad conscience is instinct turned against its bearer when external discharge is blocked. They interact in Essay II §22, but they are not the same process and neither reduces to the other.
Explaining a belief and refuting it
An account of why someone holds a moral view leaves the question of whether the view is correct entirely open. Nietzsche sometimes observes this and sometimes does not; where a passage slides from the first to the second, the slide should be marked.
Description and endorsement
Reading an affect as a symptom is not approval of what produced it or of what it produces. Nietzsche describes cruelty, revenge and self-torment with great attention; attention is not advocacy, and the passages where he does advocate are identifiable as such.
Psychological claim and historical claim
The mechanism of bad conscience is a psychological proposal. The story of a conquering race that imposed a state on a formless population is a historical one. The second is far weaker than the first and is not needed to state the first.
Drives, affects, and instincts
The vocabulary is not stabilized across the corpus, and the translations vary further. Do not assume that Trieb, Affekt and Instinkt mark a settled taxonomy; check what each passage is actually distinguishing.
Aphorism and argument
Beyond Good and Evil Part IV is a sequence of epigrams, not a chapter of moral psychology. Some of its aphorisms bear on this theme; many concern art, women, friendship, scholarship or style, and several are jokes. Its role in the book is to train a way of reading rather than to advance a doctrine, and quarrying it for psychological theses without that context produces a Nietzsche who never wrote a qualification.
What Nietzsche anticipated and what he authorized
The resemblances to later depth psychology are real and are discussed below. They do not make Nietzsche a proto-psychoanalyst, and later theories cannot be used to fill gaps in his arguments.

The afterlife

Freud said that he had avoided reading Nietzsche, and gave as a reason that he expected to find in him too much of what psychoanalysis would later claim. The judgment is not unreasonable. The unconscious as a domain of motives not transparent to the conscious self, self-deception as a feature of ordinary self-presentation, the turning inward of an impulse that cannot discharge outward — all of this is in Nietzsche before it is in Freud. Adler and Jung, both closer readers of him than Freud, are in places working out what he made available.

Two cautions. The resemblance is one of theme and not of theory: Nietzsche has no clinical practice, no account of infantile development, no technique, and no interest in cure. And the direction of explanation should not be reversed — later psychology cannot be used to supply what a Nietzschean argument leaves unsupported, and a claim of his does not gain evidential weight because something like it was later given a different apparatus. The resemblance is a fact about intellectual history, and it is interesting; it is not a corroboration.

Human, All Too Human3 reading assignments

The 1878 book establishes the basic operation: separating an act from the moral evaluation attached to it, and treating the evaluation as the thing to be explained. The Second Division, "The History of the Moral Sentiments," is where most of this work is done.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §39

    How responsibility was assembled

    Zimmern: The Fable of Intelligible Freedom.

    Question: Nietzsche describes a sequence of transfers — act, person, motive, being. Which step does the most work?

    Why it belongs: The section reconstructs how acts came to be called good or bad, how the judgment was then transferred to the agent, then to the motive, then to the whole nature, and concludes that nobody is responsible for any of it. It is the earliest full model of what this theme does, and its conclusion is stronger than the reasoning strictly supports: the naturalism carries most of the weight, and the historical sequence is illustrative.

    Read alongside: Twilight — The Four Great Errors; §107.

    Online edition: §39 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §45

    Two different histories of good and evil

    Zimmern: The Twofold Early History of Good and Evil.

    Question: If "good" has two separate origins, what happens to arguments that assume one?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche distinguishes the good as it arises among the powerful, who call themselves good, from the good as it arises among the subjected, for whom good means harmless — nine years before the Genealogy makes this its first essay. It is the clearest evidence that the late analysis has a long middle-period preparation, and reading it first makes the Genealogy look less like a sudden polemic and more like a worked-out position.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§10–13; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: §45 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Human, All Too Human Volume I, §107

    Irresponsibility, and how hard it is to hold

    Zimmern: Irresponsibility and Innocence.

    Question: Nietzsche calls the conclusion bitter. Does the theme require it?

    Why it belongs: The section pushes the naturalist argument through to complete irresponsibility and then acknowledges how difficult the conclusion is to live with. It is the point where the psychology stops being descriptive and starts making demands, and it is notably free of the later polemical relish. Whether the theme's method actually commits anyone to this conclusion is a good question to carry into the late books.

    Read alongside: Twilight — The Four Great Errors; §39.

    Online edition: §107 (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

Daybreak6 reading assignments

The 1881 book is the theme's centre of gravity. Nowhere else does Nietzsche work so patiently on the opacity of motive, and nowhere else is he so explicit that his own accounts are conjectures.

  • Core

    Daybreak §§32, 38

    What a moral judgment does to an impulse

    Kennedy: The Brake; Impulses Transformed by Moral Judgments.

    Question: What is a moral judgment doing if blame changes the impulse rather than only restraining it?

    Why it belongs: Morality shown operating causally rather than only evaluatively. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Genealogy II §§16–18.

    Online edition: §§32, 38 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §103

    Two different things that denying morality can mean

    Kennedy: There are Two Classes of People who deny Morality.

    Question: Which denial does this theme's method support, and does it support the other?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche separates denying that moral motives actually drive conduct from denying that moral judgments rest on truths, and is careful about which he is committed to. It is the best single guard against reading the psychology as an argument for acting differently: showing that a motive is not what it appears to be does not by itself recommend anything.

    Read alongside: Genealogy as method; §148.

    Online edition: §103 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Daybreak §119

    We do not know the drives we are made of

    Kennedy: Experience and Invention.

    Question: What is introspection reporting, if experience feeds the drives rather than informing us about them?

    Why it belongs: The foundational section for the theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: §§129–130; Beyond Good and Evil §19.

    Online edition: §119 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Daybreak §§129–130

    The combat of motives, and the two kingdoms

    Kennedy: The Alleged Combat of Motives; Aims? Will?

    Question: If what we observe is the outcome rather than the contest, what have we been describing when we describe a decision?

    Why it belongs: §129 argues that what people call a combat of motives is not observed but inferred after the fact, so that the conscious deliberation is a representation of a process rather than the process; §130 questions the habit of dividing everything into the domain of purposes and the domain of chance. Together they are the sharpest middle-period attack on the phenomenology of choosing, and they show how far the theme's scepticism reaches into ordinary self-description.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §19; Twilight — The Four Great Errors.

    Online edition: §§129–130 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §148

    What follows if only other-regarding acts count as moral

    Kennedy: Looking Far Ahead.

    Question: Nietzsche takes a common definition and follows it out. What does the consequence show about the definition?

    Why it belongs: The section takes the prevailing view that only acts done for the sake of others are moral and traces where it leads, using the consequence to put pressure on the premise. It is a good example of the method operating as internal criticism rather than as external diagnosis, which is a mode readers often miss in him.

    Read alongside: §215; §103.

    Online edition: §148 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Daybreak §215

    Sacrifice examined as something other than selflessness

    Kennedy: The Morality of Victims.

    Question: If enthusiastic self-sacrifice serves a drive of the one sacrificing, has anything been given up?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche reads enthusiastic sacrifice and self-immolation as satisfying something in the person who performs them, rather than as the pure abnegation they present themselves as. It is the theme's most uncomfortable application, because it is directed at conduct nearly everyone admires, and because the reading is very hard to confirm or disconfirm — which is exactly the objection to press.

    Read alongside: Genealogy III §§15–18; Zarathustra — On the Tarantulas.

    Online edition: §215 (trans. J. M. Kennedy) · Preferred edition.

The Gay Science3 reading assignments

Book III supplies the treatment of knowing as an affair of the drives; Book V, added in 1887, supplies the account of consciousness. The survey marks which sections belong to the later addition.

  • Development

    The Gay Science §333

    Knowing as the outcome of a struggle among impulses

    Common: What does Knowing Mean?

    Question: If understanding is what remains when contrary impulses have exhausted each other, what has been understood?

    Why it belongs: Taking Spinoza's formula about not laughing, lamenting or detesting but understanding, Nietzsche argues that understanding is the final result of a contest among those very impulses, and that conscious thinking is the least part of the process. It joins the theme to perspectivism: the affects are not obstacles to knowing but its constituents.

    Read alongside: §354; Perspectivism and the will to truth.

    Online edition: §333 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    The Gay Science §335

    Moral self-knowledge treated as self-flattering

    Kaufmann: Long live physics! Common: Cheers for Physics!

    Question: If we know our own actions badly, on what basis could anyone create their own values?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche attacks the confidence of conscience, argues that judgments about one's own conduct rest on much less than they assume, and nevertheless calls for becoming the creator of one's own values on the basis of studying the world's laws and necessities. The tension is the productive part: the section demands self-creation from a creature it has just described as opaque to itself.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §119; Self-overcoming.

    Online edition: §335 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development · Book V (1887)

    The Gay Science §354

    Consciousness as a late and social instrument

    Kaufmann: On the "genius of the species." Common: The "Genius of the Species."

    Question: What follows for moral psychology if what becomes conscious is already averaged and generalized?

    Why it belongs: The conjecture that consciousness developed under the pressure of the need to communicate, and is therefore tied to what is common — which would mean that self-examination reaches the generalized surface rather than the individual process. It gives the theme's scepticism a mechanism and a social history. Nietzsche offers it explicitly as a conjecture; the passage is treated more fully on perspectivism.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §119; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: §354 (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra3 reading assignments

The book dramatizes the psychology rather than arguing it, and the dramatization sometimes gets further than the analysis does. The free Common edition numbers chapters I–LXXX continuously and uses different titles; match by part and chapter title.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Pale Criminal

    An act, and the image of it the doer cannot bear

    Common: The Pale Criminal.

    Question: Zarathustra says the deed's image made the doer pale, not the deed. What is the distinction doing?

    Why it belongs: The chapter separates an act from the interpretation the doer afterwards places on it, and argues that madness comes after the deed rather than before — the criminal becomes equal to his deed only by adopting a story about himself. It is the doer-behind-the-deed argument of Genealogy I §13 in dramatic form, three years earlier, and it makes the point about self-narration more vividly than the analysis does.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§10–13; Twilight — Skirmishes §§35–37.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra I, On the Despisers of the Body

    The self behind the "I"

    Common: The Despisers of the Body.

    Question: If the self is what commands and the "I" is what it speaks through, what is the status of first-person reports?

    Why it belongs: Zarathustra distinguishes the small reason of the conscious "I" from the great reason of the body, and presents thoughts and feelings as instruments of something that does not appear in them. It is the theme's model of the self in its most compressed and most quotable form. Being compressed, it is also easily turned into a slogan about trusting the body, which is not what the chapter says.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §19; Body and physiology.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    Thus Spoke Zarathustra II, On the Tarantulas

    Revenge inside a demand for justice

    Common: The Tarantulas.

    Question: How would one tell a demand for justice from a disguised will to revenge, on this account — and can one?

    Why it belongs: The chapter accuses the preachers of equality of harbouring revenge behind their talk of justice, and Zarathustra then says he does not wish to be confused with them. It is the theme's most contested application and the one most open to abuse, since the same move can discredit any egalitarian claim without examining it. Note that Zarathustra's own position here is anti-egalitarian and is stated as such; the site does not present that as incidental.

    Read alongside: Daybreak §215; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: Read the chapter (trans. Thomas Common) · Preferred edition.

Beyond Good and Evil4 reading assignments

Part I supplies the model of the will and the claim for psychology; Part V, "Natural History of Morals" (§§186–203), supplies the comparative programme. Part IV, "Epigrams and Interludes," runs §§63–185 and is a different kind of writing altogether; see the note in its entry below. The free Zimmern edition has no per-section anchors, so links open the relevant part and you should use Find; Zimmern's part titles differ from Kaufmann's in Parts III, IV and VIII.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§13, 17

    Discharging strength, and a thought that comes unbidden

    Question: §17 says a thought comes when it wishes. What does that leave of the thinker?

    Why it belongs: §13 argues that a living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength and that self-preservation is only a consequence, which puts a different drive at the centre of the psychology; §17 observes that a thought arrives on its own terms and that saying "I think" already adds an interpretation supplied by grammar. Two short sections that supply the theme's positive model and its grammatical scepticism.

    Read alongside: §19; Will to power.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Beyond Good and Evil §§19, 23

    The will as many wills, and psychology as the way in

    Question: Who is the one who wills, if willing is commanding and obeying at once?

    Why it belongs: The theme's model of the self and its statement of method. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra — On the Despisers of the Body.

    Online edition: Part I (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Beyond Good and Evil §§63–80, from Part IV

    Epigrams, and what they are for

    Zimmern titles this part Apophthegms and Interludes; Kaufmann, Epigrams and Interludes.

    Question: An epigram states a thesis without arguing for it. What does reading a run of them train, and what does it not establish?

    Why it belongs: A note on what this assignment is and is not. Part IV runs §§63–185; the assignment here is a selection from its opening, carried over from the earlier version of this page, and it is not the part in full. More importantly, Part IV is not a chapter of moral psychology. Its aphorisms range across knowledge, vanity, women, friendship, scholarship, cruelty and style, and a number of them are jokes or provocations whose target is the reader's assent. Several bear directly on this theme — the remarks on vanity, on self-knowledge, and on what one is willing to admit to oneself are genuinely useful — but the part's function in the book is to train a way of reading rather than to advance a doctrine. Take the individual aphorisms that bear on the theme, read them against the argued passages in Parts I and V, and do not treat the run as a systematic statement. The book course handles Part IV in its own terms.

    Read alongside: §§186–203, Part V; Aphorism and style.

    Online edition: Part IV (trans. Helen Zimmern; use Find for the section number) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Beyond Good and Evil §§186–203, Part V

    Moralities compared rather than justified

    Question: What would a natural history of morals have to contain that moral philosophy has not supplied?

    Why it belongs: The whole of Part V, which moves from the complaint that the science of morals does not exist, through the treatment of moralities as means and as long compulsions, to §201 on herd morality and §§202–203 on what Nietzsche takes present-day European morality to be. It supplies the comparative frame this theme's psychology feeds into. The part is also where the anti-egalitarian commitments become explicit and sustained, and they are not separable from the analysis: §§202–203 are political, and saying so is more honest than presenting the part as neutral taxonomy. The method itself is treated on genealogy as method.

    Read alongside: Genealogy as method; The herd and the last man.

    Online edition: Part V (trans. Helen Zimmern) · Preferred edition.

On the Genealogy of Morals5 reading assignments

The 1887 book contains the theme's two central mechanisms, in separate essays and with separate arguments. The Preface and each essay are numbered separately in every edition, and Nietzsche's closing Note follows Essay I §17. For a complete reading of all three essays in order, use the twelve-lesson course.

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §§10–13

    Ressentiment, and the doer invented behind the deed

    Question: What work is the language of choice doing, if the strong are not free to be weak?

    Why it belongs: The first of the two mechanisms. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Essay I §14; Master and slave morality.

    Online edition: Essay I §§10–13 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay I, §14

    The workshop where ideals are manufactured

    Question: The section is staged as an eavesdropping scene. What does the literary device let Nietzsche claim that an argument would not?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche stages a descent into the workshop where ideals are made and reports, through an interlocutor, how weakness is renamed as goodness, inability to retaliate as forgiveness, and submission as obedience. It is the most vivid statement of the mechanism and the most methodologically slippery: the translations are presented as overheard rather than demonstrated, and nothing in the scene establishes that the renamings are insincere rather than sincere reinterpretations.

    Read alongside: Essay I §§10–13; Daybreak §215.

    Online edition: Essay I §14 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §§16–18

    Bad conscience as instinct turned inward

    Question: How can bad conscience be both an illness and the source of everything interesting in human beings?

    Why it belongs: The second mechanism, and the more original one. See the first encounter for the full explanation, including the note on the historical narrative.

    Read alongside: Essay II §22; Suffering and cruelty.

    Online edition: Essay II §§16–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay II, §22

    Guilt raised to a maximum

    Question: Why would a device that relieves guilt end by increasing it?

    Why it belongs: The section describes the turn by which a debt owed to a creditor becomes unpayable and the will to self-torment finds its most terrible instrument in the idea of a god before whom one is irredeemably guilty. It is where the two mechanisms meet — the priestly redirection of Essay III is already visible — and where the psychology of guilt reaches its most extreme form. Keep it distinct from Essay I: this is not the slave revolt, and Nietzsche is not describing a reversal of values here.

    Read alongside: Critique of Christianity; Essay III §§15–18.

    Online edition: Essay II §22 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    On the Genealogy of Morals Essay III, §§15–18

    The priest redirecting a suffering he cannot cure

    Question: If the treatment genuinely relieves the symptom while deepening the condition, on what basis is it judged?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche presents the ascetic priest as changing the direction of ressentiment — telling the sufferer to look for the cause of the suffering in himself — and then catalogues the means used to make existence bearable: deadening the feeling of life, mechanical activity, small joys, and the organization of the weak into a herd. It is the most clinically detailed stretch of moral psychology in the corpus, and the double judgment it rests on, that the priest is both genuinely useful and genuinely harmful, is what makes it serious rather than dismissive.

    Read alongside: Critique of Christianity; Nihilism.

    Online edition: Essay III §§15–18 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) · Preferred edition.

Twilight of the Idols4 reading assignments

The 1888 book compresses the psychology and turns it on named targets. Section numbers restart in each chapter and Ludovici's chapter titles differ from Kaufmann's; see the edition guidance.

  • Complication

    Twilight of the Idols The Problem of Socrates

    A philosopher diagnosed rather than answered

    Question: What separates a psychological diagnosis of a thinker from a refusal to engage with the thought?

    Why it belongs: The chapter reads the Socratic equation of reason, virtue and happiness as a symptom of a condition rather than as a philosophical position to be answered, and draws on Socrates' appearance and origins in doing so. It is the clearest case where the method shades into ad hominem, and it is included for that reason: the theme has to be able to say where its own procedure stops being informative. The physiognomic material rests on assumptions that no longer stand.

    Read alongside: Genealogy as method; Critique of metaphysics.

    Online edition: The Problem of Socrates (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Core

    Twilight of the Idols The Four Great Errors §§1–8

    The machinery of moral judgment taken apart

    Question: What is left of praise and blame if the will is not a cause?

    Why it belongs: The compressed late summary of the whole theme. See the first encounter for the full explanation.

    Read alongside: Human, All Too Human §39.

    Online edition: The Four Great Errors (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Development

    Twilight of the Idols Morality as Anti-Nature §§1–3

    Hostility to a passion read as a symptom

    Ludovici: Morality as the Enemy of Nature.

    Question: Nietzsche distinguishes destroying a passion from spiritualizing it. What would spiritualizing one actually involve?

    Why it belongs: The chapter argues that attacking a passion at the root is the recourse of those unable to exercise moderation, and treats the choice between extirpation and transformation as diagnostic of the person making it. It supplies the theme's evaluative standard, which is otherwise left implicit: an affect is to be judged by what a life can do with it. The standard is asserted rather than defended.

    Read alongside: Self-overcoming; Critique of Christianity.

    Online edition: Morality as Anti-Nature (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

  • Extension

    Twilight of the Idols Skirmishes of an Untimely Man §§35–37

    The criminal as a type made by circumstances

    Ludovici: Skirmishes in a War with the Age.

    Question: If the criminal is a strong type in unfavourable conditions, what follows for how the type is judged?

    Why it belongs: These short sections treat the criminal as a strong human being made sick by the conditions he is placed in, and connect the diagnosis to a general claim about what social conditions do to types. It is the late, compressed version of the Pale Criminal chapter and of Daybreak §202, and it moves quickly from psychology to a judgment about society that the psychology does not establish on its own.

    Read alongside: Zarathustra — On the Pale Criminal; The Problem of Socrates.

    Online edition: Skirmishes, §§35–37 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

The Antichrist and Ecce Homo2 reading assignments

Both from 1888. Ecce Homo was published posthumously and is retrospective throughout. The free Mencken Antichrist omits portions of §§29, 35 and 38 and the separate Law Against Christianity; see the edition guidance.

  • Extension

    The Antichrist §15

    A whole moral vocabulary classified as imaginary

    Question: Classification is not explanation. What is this section doing that Daybreak §119 does not?

    Why it belongs: The section lists sin, redemption, grace, free will and the rest as an imaginary psychology built on misreadings of inner states, and treats the whole vocabulary as one formation. It shows the theme's apparatus applied at maximum compression, and it is a useful measure of what the compression costs: the middle-period sections reconstruct the mechanism, this one names the verdict. The analysis is treated at length on critique of Christianity.

    Read alongside: Critique of Christianity; Daybreak §119.

    Online edition: §15 (trans. H. L. Mencken) · Preferred edition.

  • Complication · retrospective

    Ecce Homo Why I Am So Wise §6

    Freedom from ressentiment, claimed in the first person

    Question: Can anyone certify their own freedom from a mechanism the theme describes as invisible to the person in it?

    Why it belongs: Nietzsche claims that he does not know what ressentiment is from the inside, attributes the freedom to his illness and convalescence, and describes the inability to take an injury seriously for long. Set against §§10–13 of Genealogy I, the claim is the theme's sharpest self-referential problem: the analysis says that ressentiment reinterprets itself as something more admirable, and here is its author declaring himself exempt. Read as evidence about his self-understanding, and as a live test of whether the method can be applied to the one applying it.

    Read alongside: Genealogy I §§10–13; The afterlife.

    Online edition: Why I Am So Wise §6 (trans. Anthony M. Ludovici) · Preferred edition.

Notebook material

A posthumous compilation. The Will to Power arranges Nietzsche's notebook materials; it is not a book he completed for publication. Its entries can show what he was working out in private. They do not establish what the published works claim, and on this theme the published material is unusually rich, so the notebooks are a supplement rather than a source.

References use the expanded 1,067-section compilation followed by Kaufmann/Hollingdale and the linked Ludovici translation. These are editorial entry numbers, not original notebook references. Wording and editorial corrections differ between editions, and the compilation brings together notes written at different dates. Edition guidance.

Two entries are given here, identified individually rather than as a range. No short starting route is offered: the compilation's psychological material is scattered across books assembled by its editors around other organizing questions, and a seven-entry sequence would impose a shape the notes do not have. Readers wanting a fuller notebook route should use the one on perspectivism for the epistemology and the one on master and slave morality for moral authority and rank.

  • Notebook reading · Book II

    The Will to Power §253

    Examining a morality without being charmed by it

    Question: Is the detachment described here a requirement of the method, or something the method presupposes?

    Why it belongs: The entry describes the attempt to investigate morality without coming under its spell and without assuming its evaluations. It states the stance the published work requires and states it as an attempt rather than an achievement — more cautious than the late books, and a useful gloss on the difficulty Ecce Homo "Why I Am So Wise" §6 runs into.

    Read alongside: Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Wise §6; Genealogy as method.

  • Notebook reading · Book III

    The Will to Power §490

    The subject as a plurality

    Question: If there is no single subject, what is it that a moral judgment is addressed to?

    Why it belongs: The entry tests a plurality of interacting subjects in place of an assumed single one, with an analogy to cooperating and competing cells. It pushes further than Beyond Good and Evil §19, which keeps the language of commanding and obeying within a single person, and it shows Nietzsche entertaining a position the published books stop short of. The cell analogy is an exploratory hypothesis and not an account of agency.

    Read alongside: Beyond Good and Evil §19; Body and physiology.

For the original notebook arrangement and manuscript references rather than the compilation's editorial numbering, consult Nietzsche Source's critical edition.

Related extensions: what might follow?

Choose these after the central questions, according to the direction your reading takes. They return to assignments already explained in the survey.

  • The two mechanisms, side by side. Read Genealogy I §§10–13 and II §§16–18 in immediate succession and write down the difference in one sentence. If it is hard, that is the point of keeping them separate.
  • Middle period against late. Compare Daybreak §119 with The Antichrist §15. What does the late compression gain, and what does it quietly stop claiming?
  • The self-referential problem. Pair Ecce Homo — Why I Am So Wise §6 with Genealogy I §14. Does the method leave anyone a position from which to certify their own motives?
  • Where diagnosis becomes politics. Read On the Tarantulas beside Beyond Good and Evil §§201–203. What does the psychology license, and what does it only appear to license?

A question to carry forward

The method explains a moral conviction by finding a motive behind it that the holder cannot see. What would a well-founded moral conviction look like on this account — and does Nietzsche leave room for one?
