Overview and context

Ecce Homo is Nietzsche’s account of how he became who he is, and his judgment of the books that record the process. It makes three kinds of claim, often in the same paragraph: testimony about his family, illnesses, diet, places, and friendships; self-interpretation of what his books meant and achieved; and a program, the “revaluation of all values,” that he says will divide history. This guide asks you to keep those claims apart. Testimony can be checked against other evidence. Self-interpretation can be checked against the earlier books. The program has to be judged by its reasons and its costs.

The chronology matters. Ludovici’s introduction to the free translation dates the draft from 15 October to 4 November 1888. The textual differences recorded under Texts, editions, and variants show that the free translation and the modern critical text print different versions of several passages. Nietzsche collapsed in Turin at the beginning of January 1889, and the book was not published until 1908. That proximity tempts readers toward two shortcuts: treating the book as a symptom whose claims need not be weighed, or treating it as the author’s final ruling on his work. Neither is a reading. The claims can be examined on their own terms.

The book is most rewarding after you have read several of the works it reviews. The chapter “Why I Write Such Good Books” discusses ten of them in order of publication. Its vocabulary is the vocabulary of 1888: decadence, revaluation, Dionysus against Christianity, great health. Sometimes that vocabulary brings out an emphasis a first reading missed. Sometimes it rewrites the older book in terms the book did not use. The book-by-book section below marks where to look in each case. A reader who has finished only one or two of the earlier books can still follow the path; note which judgments you cannot yet test.

The book states its hierarchies plainly, and this guide does too. Nietzsche claims to detect “base blood” by smell (“Why I Am So Wise” §8). He treats the struggle for women’s equal rights as “a symptom of disease” (“Why I Write Such Good Books” §5), calls the Germans an intolerable “race,” and hopes for a future “party of life” that would undertake “the relentless destruction of all degenerate and parasitical elements” (the review of The Birth of Tragedy, §4; Ludovici’s wording). These claims are part of the self-portrait and part of the program. Read them as claims to assess, not as colour to skip.

Carry one question through the whole book: What would count as evidence for this? Ask it of the account of his illness, of each retrospective verdict, and of the claim to be a destiny.

Guided reading path

This path covers the whole of Nietzsche’s prose book in its printed order: the Preface, the untitled page that follows it, the three “Why I…” chapters, all ten book reviews, and “Why I Am a Destiny.” The eight stages are this guide’s teaching divisions, not Nietzsche’s. Stages 1–3 build the self-portrait and its vocabulary. Stage 4 sets out Nietzsche’s theory of how he is read. Stages 5–7 divide the ten reviews into three groups, each with a comparison to make against the earlier books. Stage 8 is the final claim.

Section numbers restart in every chapter and in every book review. Cite the chapter or review with the number: “Wise §6,” “Books, Zarathustra §3.” Ludovici’s free text omits three section numerals and in several places differs from the critical text; check the edition notes before relying on a section. His introduction and the poetry printed after the prose are outside the assignment.

  1. Stage 1Preface, §§1–4; then the untitled page beginning “On this perfect day…”

    Read: Nietzsche’s Preface, then the one unnumbered page printed before the title “Ecce Homo: How One Becomes What One Is.” Stop before “Why I Am So Wise.”

    Why this stage: The Preface demands to be recognized (“do not confound me with any one else!” in Ludovici’s wording) and then ends with Zarathustra telling his disciples to lose him and find themselves. The untitled page dates the retrospect to his forty-fourth birthday and lists the year’s work. The free text’s list differs from the critical text’s; see the variants.

    Questions: What kind of reader does the Preface ask for: one who trusts the portrait, or one who tests it? Preface §3 makes “how much truth” a spirit can endure the measure of value. Is that a standard of evidence, of courage, or of rank?

  2. Stage 2“Why I Am So Wise,” §§1–8

    Read: the whole chapter. In the free text, §8 has no numeral; it begins “May I be allowed to hazard a suggestion…”. Before answering the questions, check which version of §3 your edition prints.

    Why this stage: Here Nietzsche builds the diagnostic vocabulary he will later apply to Socrates, Christianity, Wagner, and the Germans. He presents himself as both a decadent and its opposite, describes illness as the school in which he learned to “reverse perspectives,” and discusses ressentiment, pity, his “war tactics,” and the sense of cleanliness that sets him apart.

    Questions: In §§1–2, what criterion distinguishes the decadent from its “reverse,” and could anyone but Nietzsche apply it to Nietzsche? §§4–7 claim freedom from ressentiment and a war waged only against causes, never persons. Test the claim against §8, and later against the review of The Case of Wagner.

  3. Stage 3“Why I Am So Clever,” §§1–10

    Read: the whole chapter: nutrition, climate, reading, poets and musicians, Wagner, music, taste as self-defense, then §9’s answer to how one becomes what one is and §10’s formula of amor fati.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche moves the question of value from “God,” “soul,” and “sin” to what he calls the small things: food, place, weather, recuperation. §7 later reappears, substantially unchanged, as the Intermezzo of Nietzsche Contra Wagner. The critical text of §10 includes a passage absent from the free text.

    Questions: §9 says one becomes what one is only by not knowing what one is. How does that fit a book so confident about its author? §10 calls the small things more important than everything formerly held important. What follows for ethics and politics, and what does not follow?

  4. Stage 4“Why I Write Such Good Books,” opening §§1–6

    Read: the six general sections before the first book review. Ludovici’s heading is “Why I Write Such Excellent Books.” His §6 has no numeral; it begins “In order to give you some idea of myself as a psychologist…”. Stop at the heading for The Birth of Tragedy.

    Why this stage: Before judging his books, Nietzsche explains why they are misread: a reader hears only what experience has prepared him to hear. He describes his perfect reader, his theory of style, and himself as a psychologist. §5 contains his most explicit claims about women. §6 quotes Beyond Good and Evil §295 without naming the section.

    Questions: Does §1’s account of misunderstanding explain real failures of reading, or does it protect the author from every objection in advance? §4 defines good style as the faithful communication of an inner state. What does that make the reader responsible for?

  5. Stage 5Book reviews: The Birth of Tragedy §§1–4; Untimely Meditations §§1–3; Human, All Too Human §§1–6

    Read: the first three reviews. Ludovici calls the second “Thoughts out of Season.” In his text the last section of the Birth review has no numeral; it begins “A tremendous hope finds expression in this work.”

    Why this stage: These reviews cover the Wagnerian hope and the break. Nietzsche now says that the early portraits of Schopenhauer and Wagner were really portraits of himself, and that the break of 1876 was a return to himself through illness. Comparison: read the end of Birth §24 and Human, All Too Human I §37 beside the reviews (see the notes).

    Question: Where does the late author say an earlier text was “about” himself? Name one feature of the earlier text that supports his reading and one that resists it.

  6. Stage 6Book reviews: Daybreak §§1–2; The Gay Science (one section); Thus Spoke Zarathustra §§1–8

    Read: the next three reviews. Ludovici calls them “The Dawn of Day” and “Joyful Wisdom.” The Zarathustra review is the longest in the chapter and quotes Zarathustra at length.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche calls these his yes-saying books. He dates the thought of eternal recurrence to August 1881, describes the inspiration that produced Zarathustra, and names the book’s spirit Dionysus. Comparison: check Daybreak §§103 and 575 and The Gay Science §§341, 342, and 382 (see the notes).

    Question: Which ideas from 1886–1888 does Nietzsche read back into the books of 1881–1885? Find one clear case and state what changes.

  7. Stage 7Book reviews: Beyond Good and Evil §§1–2; On the Genealogy of Morals (one section); Twilight of the Idols §§1–3; The Case of Wagner §§1–4

    Read: the last four reviews, through the end of the chapter. The review of The Case of Wagner is chiefly about the Germans. The critical text of its §4 continues past the free text’s ending.

    Why this stage: After the yes-saying books, Nietzsche says, came the “no-saying” half of the task. He calls the later books bait, preparatory overtures, and war. The Twilight review narrates the composition of the “Revaluation” in September 1888. Comparison: check Genealogy II §§24–25 and III §§23, 28, and the dating of the Twilight Preface (see the notes).

    Question: The reviews become shorter and more programmatic. By what standard does the late Nietzsche now judge whether a book succeeded?

  8. Stage 8“Why I Am a Destiny,” §§1–9 (Ludovici: “Why I Am a Fatality”)

    Read: the whole final chapter, through the one-line §9. Stop before the editorial note and poetry that follow in the free volume.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche gathers the self-portrait into a claim about history. He is “dynamite,” not a founder of religion. Politics will become spiritual war. He says he chose the name Zarathustra because morality’s inventor must be the first to overcome it. He defines the immoralist by two negations and ends with the formula that the German text gives as “Dionysus against the Crucified” (Ludovici: “Dionysus versus Christ”).

    Questions: §1 refuses believers yet predicts that history will be divided by his name. Which of his claims could be tested, and by whom? In §§4 and 8, what exactly does he deny in denying “the good man,” and what does he put in its place? Name the exclusions as well as the affirmations.

After Stage 8, complete the writing exercise. It asks you to test one retrospective judgment against the earlier book it describes.

A shorter first encounter

For an entrance before the full path, read Preface §§3–4, “Why I Am So Wise” §§1–2, “Why I Am So Clever” §9, and “Why I Am a Destiny” §§1 and 9. These show the demand to be recognized, the double perspective of illness and health, the account of becoming what one is, and the final claim.

Then make one comparison. Read the Birth of Tragedy review §1 in Ecce Homo, and the closing paragraph of The Birth of Tragedy §24. The late review says the early book once alluded to the Christian priests as a malicious kind of dwarf (Ludovici: a “perfidious order of goblins”). Does the early passage say so? Write down what you find before reading this guide’s note on it. This short route is an entrance to the book, not a substitute for its reviews of the earlier works.

Texts, editions, and variants

The preferred translation is Walter Kaufmann’s, in Basic Writings of Nietzsche; Kaufmann’s translation is also printed with the Genealogy in a separate volume. This guide has not been checked against a Kaufmann printing. Quotations on this page are either Ludovici’s wording, labeled as such, or this guide’s paraphrase or translation of the German critical text.

Choose an edition and find passages

The free text. Project Gutenberg’s Ecce Homo reproduces volume 17 (1911) of the English Complete Works edited by Oscar Levy. Nietzsche’s prose is translated by Anthony M. Ludovici. The volume has three parts, and only the middle part is assigned:

  • Ludovici’s “Translator’s Introduction” is his own essay. It defends the chapter titles by appeal to rank (“Only nobodies are ever modest”) and argues against reading the book as a sign of breakdown. Treat it as early reception, not as Nietzsche’s text.
  • Nietzsche’s prose runs from the Preface through “Why I Am a Fatality” §9. The untitled page between the Preface and the title belongs to it.
  • The “Editorial Note to Poetry” and the poems that follow are a separate selection. The editor’s note says the verse is translated by Paul V. Cohn unless otherwise stated, and the title page also credits Francis Bickley, Herman Scheffauer, and G. T. Wrench. The Hymn to Life printed at the end is a musical setting, words by Lou Salomé and music by Nietzsche. None of this is part of Ecce Homo.

Numbering. Sections restart in every chapter and in every book review. In the free text, three sections are printed without numerals: “Why I Am So Wise” §8 (“May I be allowed to hazard a suggestion…”), “Why I Write Such Good Books” §6 (“In order to give you some idea of myself as a psychologist…”), and the Birth of Tragedy review §4 (“A tremendous hope finds expression in this work.”). The critical text numbers all three. The Gay Science and Genealogy reviews are single unnumbered sections.

Links and page references. The free text has anchors for each chapter and review but not for each section; links on this page open the chapter or a nearby printed page. Page numbers inside Nietzsche’s text refer to nineteenth-century editions, and Ludovici converted some to his own edition’s pages. Locate such passages by content. In “Why I Am So Clever” §5, for example, the free text sends you to Beyond Good and Evil “Aphorism 269”; the German gives a page reference, and the discussion it describes, of Wagner beside Delacroix and the late French romantics, is Beyond Good and Evil §256.

Text differences that affect the assignment

The modern critical text, available in German at Nietzsche Source, differs from the free text in the places below. This is not a full collation. It records the differences found when the assigned sections were compared, and it does not settle which version your preferred printing uses. Check before building an argument on any of these passages.

  • Untitled page after the Preface. Ludovici lists “The first book of the Transvaluation of all Values, The Songs of Zarathustra, The Twilight of the Idols.” The critical text names the Revaluation of All Values, the Dionysus-Dithyrambs, and, “for recreation,” Twilight of the Idols.
  • “Why I Am So Wise” §3. Ludovici prints the older version, used in the 1908 first edition: a section on Polish ancestry, his mother and grandmother, and his father’s years at the Altenburg court. The critical text prints Nietzsche’s later replacement. In it he repudiates his mother and sister, calls them the deepest objection to eternal recurrence, claims unmixed Polish noble descent, and names Cosima Wagner as the one person he recognizes as his equal.
  • “Why I Am So Clever” §10. The critical text contains a passage missing from Ludovici. It calls present culture ambiguous in the highest degree, mocks the German emperor for making common cause with the pope, and claims the word “greatness” for Nietzsche on the strength of what he can do.
  • Review of The Case of Wagner, §4. Ludovici ends with ironic congratulations to “my dear Germans.” The critical text continues. An old friend has written that she now laughs at him, and he replies that he carries the destiny of humanity on his shoulders.
  • “Why I Am a Destiny” §9. Ludovici: “Dionysus versus Christ.” The German reads “Dionysos gegen den Gekreuzigten”: Dionysus against the Crucified. The wording alone does not settle whom or what “the Crucified” refers to; see the passage note on Destiny §§7–9.

Composition and publication. Ecce Homo was written in 1888 and first published in 1908, eight years after Nietzsche’s death. Keep the two dates distinct when you cite it.

Words to compare. Ludovici’s transvaluation corresponds to this guide’s revaluation, and his fatality to destiny (Schicksal). Decadence is Nietzsche’s proposed pattern of life working against its own conditions, not a neutral medical category. Resentment in the free text of “Why I Am So Wise” §6 corresponds to ressentiment elsewhere on this site.

Interpretive problems

Testimony is not yet explanation

“Why I Am So Wise” reports illnesses, a doctor’s remark, a father’s early death, a lowest ebb in 1879. Then it uses those reports to explain a philosophical capacity: the ability to view health from sickness and sickness from health. The first is testimony. The second is an explanation that the testimony cannot establish by itself. Nietzsche’s reversal of perspectives may be a real insight into how he worked. The claim that it made a revaluation “possible to me alone” goes further than the evidence on the page.

The body becomes a standard of value

“Why I Am So Clever” turns diet, climate, and recreation into questions of value and states that spirit itself is “only a form of these organic functions” (§2, Ludovici). This extends the physiological critique of Twilight’s “Four Great Errors” into autobiography. It also produces confident claims about dry air and genius, German cooking and German intellect. Separate the question of whether bodily conditions shape thought from the particular diagnoses Nietzsche offers. The first is a live hypothesis. The second often rests on anecdote.

Becoming what one is

The subtitle echoes The Gay Science §270: “Thou shalt become what thou art” in Common’s translation. “Why I Am So Clever” §9 gives the phrase a surprising account. One becomes what one is by not knowing it in advance: detours, mistakes, and even misplaced modesty serve an organizing “idea” growing below consciousness. This is one of the book’s most interesting arguments. It also makes a difficulty. If self-knowledge arrives only in retrospect, the retrospect is the least independent witness. Is the organizing idea discovered, or supplied afterward?

Who owns a book’s meaning?

An author’s later reading can bring out emphases a first reader missed. Ecce Homo also changes quotations, reads 1887 additions back into 1882, and identifies the subject of early essays as himself. The reviews are evidence of how Nietzsche understood his work in 1888. They are not evidence of what the earlier books say. Compare exact passages before accepting a late verdict, and do not let it silently replace the earlier text. Twilight offers a smaller instance of the same practice; see its notes on later Nietzsche and his earlier books.

Rank, contempt, and cleanliness

The book claims freedom from ressentiment while practicing contempt on a large scale: the “rabble,” the Germans, emancipated women, those with “polluted blood” (“Why I Am So Clever” §10). The contempt is not incidental. It follows from a standard that ranks lives by health, distance, and the capacity to affirm. Distinguish the psychological claim that resentment consumes the resentful (“Why I Am So Wise” §6) from the social hierarchy the book builds on its idea of health. The first can be tested. The second needs a separate justification, which the book mostly asserts.

A destiny with political consequences

“Why I Am a Destiny” says politics will become “spiritual warfare” and that wars will follow. Its §8 condemns a morality that favours everything weak and sick, “which ought to be wiped out” (Ludovici). The rhetoric can be read as intellectual provocation, as a program of cultivation, or as something harsher. The text does not settle which. It does not permit the harsher possibilities to be ignored. Name what the final chapter opposes: a morality of self-renunciation and the priestly type. Then ask what its preferred alternative would require of the people it excludes.

The ten book reviews

“Why I Write Such Good Books” reviews ten works in order of publication. Human, All Too Human is treated with its two sequels. The Antichrist has no review of its own. The Twilight review recounts work on the “Revaluation” in September 1888 without naming a title; this guide, like the note on that review, reads that work as The Antichrist. The early unpublished essays, Nietzsche Contra Wagner, and the Dionysus-Dithyrambs are not reviewed. For each review, the notes below say what Nietzsche now emphasizes, what to check in the earlier book, and where the retrospect meets resistance.

The Birth of Tragedy · Review §§1–4

What he now emphasizes. The book’s value lies not in its application to Wagner but in two discoveries: the Dionysian as the root of Greek art, and Socrates as “a typical decadent.” A better title would have been “Hellenism and Pessimism,” he says. The book was “profoundly and politely silent” about Christianity, yet “in one part” alluded to the Christian priests as a “perfidious order of goblins” (the German is “tückische Art von Zwergen”, a malicious kind of dwarf). In §§3–4 he names himself the first tragic philosopher, and he reads Richard Wagner in Bayreuth as a portrait of himself.

Check against the book. Hellenism and Pessimism was the subtitle Nietzsche added to the 1886 reissue. In the free translation, the words decadent and decadence appear only in the appended extract from Ecce Homo, not in the 1872 sections on Socrates (§§12–15). The only such dwarfs in the book (Haussmann: “malignant dwarfs”) come at the end of §24. They are Wagnerian imagery for what the reawakened German spirit will destroy, and priests are not named. Compare the 1886 “Attempt at a Self-Criticism”, whose §5 already speaks of the book’s hostile silence toward Christianity.

Tension. The late review keeps the Dionysian and drops almost all of the Schopenhauerian metaphysics, which it reduces to “one or two formulæ.” §4 then attaches to the old book a hope the old book did not state: a party of life that will destroy “degenerate and parasitical elements.” Treat that as a claim of 1888.

Untimely Meditations · Review §§1–3

What he now emphasizes. The four essays are “warlike.” The first (1873) exposed German culture as mere “public opinion” through David Strauss. The second (1874) diagnosed the historical sense as a sickness. The third and fourth present two “un-modern types,” Schopenhauer and Wagner, “or, in a word, Nietzsche.” Much of §2 recounts the reception of the Strauss essay.

Check against the book. The second meditation does diagnose an excess of history, but it also distinguishes kinds of history and asks how each can serve life. Read the third and the fourth for what they say about Schopenhauer and Wagner themselves before substituting Nietzsche’s name, as the Birth review §4 invites. The page numbers Nietzsche cites refer to editions the reader will not have; find the passages by content.

Tension. Calling the portraits self-portraits may reveal how much the young author projected into his models. It also erases the actual Wagner and Schopenhauer as objects of praise, and so erases the question of whether that praise was a mistake.

Human, All Too Human · Review §§1–6

What he now emphasizes. The book is “the memorial of a crisis.” Ideals are not refuted in it; they “freeze.” Nietzsche dates its beginning to the first Bayreuth festival of 1876, describes illness as the liberation from philology, and tells of the book crossing in the post with Wagner’s Parsifal text. In §6 he quotes one of its passages as proof of his early certainty.

Check against the book. The quoted passage is Human, All Too Human I §37, which praises the author of On the Origin of the Moral Sensations, Paul Rée. Ecce Homo inserts “read Nietzsche, the first Immoralist,” “read The Transvaluation of all Values,” and a date, “1890!” (Ludovici’s wording). These insertions are marked as such. It also adds, without marking, a clause absent from the 1878 text: “for there is no intelligible world.” Compare the Genealogy Preface §4, which Nietzsche cites here for his disagreement with Rée.

Tension. The book’s cool method is described in heated terms, and the retrospect reshapes the quotation it offers as evidence. The dictation to Peter Gast and the “crossing of the two books” with Parsifal are testimony. Check them against a biography rather than treating this account as the record.

Daybreak · Review §§1–2

What he now emphasizes. With this book, he says, his “campaign against morality” began, though without “powder”: morality is not attacked but simply ceases to be considered. He notes the Rig-Veda motto and that the book ends with “or?”. §2 turns to his life-task: a “great noon” at which humanity would ask about its purpose, freed from priests. He compares that task to a physiologist cutting away degenerate parts without pity.

Check against the book. Daybreak §575 does end on a question (“Or, my brethren? or—?” in Kennedy’s free translation). §103 gives Nietzsche’s own earlier statement of what denying morality means: he denies morality “in the same way as I deny alchemy,” that is, its premises, while granting that many actions called immoral should still be avoided and many called moral performed, for other reasons. Test the claim that the book contains “not one attack” against §§68 and 84 on Paul and the “philology of Christianity.”

Tension. §2’s surgical image of removing degenerate parts belongs to 1888. It goes well beyond Daybreak’s careful and experimental tone.

The Gay Science · Review, one section

What he now emphasizes. The shortest review. It presents the book as a gift of “the most wonderful month of January” and quotes the verse that opens Book IV. It points to the first words of Zarathustra at the end of Book IV, to the “granite sentences” that close Book III, and to the songs of “Prince Vogelfrei,” ending with “To the Mistral.”

Check against the book. §342 (“Incipit tragoedia”) is the opening of Zarathustra. §341 introduces eternal recurrence. The questions of §§268–275 close Book III, and §270 is “Thou shalt become what thou art.” The songs of Prince Vogelfrei were added in 1887 with Book V and the new Preface. The review treats the book as one gift of January 1882, but it praises material from both editions. See the Gay Science guide on the 1887 additions.

Tension. What the review omits is as instructive as what it selects. The madman’s announcement of the death of God (§125) and Book V’s questioning of the will to truth go unmentioned.

Thus Spoke Zarathustra · Review §§1–8

What he now emphasizes. The book’s “fundamental idea” is eternal recurrence, conceived in August 1881 by a rock near Surlei and noted as “Six thousand feet beyond man and time.” Nietzsche narrates each part’s composition in short bursts, describes inspiration as revelation (§3) and the “rancour of greatness” that followed (§5). He calls Zarathustra’s type “the very idea of Dionysus” (§6) and the book’s language the dithyramb (§7). He ends with the command “Harden yourselves!” (§8).

Check against the book. §2 defines the “great health” Zarathustra requires by quoting The Gay Science §382, from Book V, written after Zarathustra was finished. In the free translation of all four parts, Zarathustra’s speeches never name Dionysus. The identification is Ecce Homo’s. “Harden yourselves” closes “On Old and New Tablets” §29 in Part III; Twilight ends with the same passage as “The Hammer Speaks.”

Tension. The review presents a sovereign, unambiguous triumph. The book itself stages hesitation, nausea, relapse, and a teacher uncertain of his listeners, especially in Parts III and IV. Read the review as one interpretation of that drama, not its resolution.

Beyond Good and Evil · Review §§1–2

What he now emphasizes. After the yes-saying part of his task, the “no-saying” half began. From then on, he says, all his writings are “so much bait.” The book is a critique of modernity and “a school for gentlemen,” with the word taken “in a much more spiritual and radical sense.” He says the whole book “does not contain one single good-natured word.”

Check against the book. Ecce Homo itself, in “Why I Write Such Good Books” §6, quotes the “genius of the heart” passage of Beyond Good and Evil §295 as a specimen of his psychology. Ask whether that passage fits “not one single good-natured word.” Read §§257–259 for how literal the book’s aristocracy is.

Tension. The review spiritualizes “gentleman.” The book it describes also argues about actual aristocratic societies, slavery in some sense, and exploitation. A spiritualized reading cannot simply replace those claims.

On the Genealogy of Morals · Review, one section

What he now emphasizes. Each essay begins coolly and ends in “thunder.” He summarizes the three “truths”: Christianity born out of ressentiment, conscience as cruelty turned inward, and the ascetic ideal as the only ideal available. Its power, he says, comes from having had no rival: “until the time of Zarathustra there was no such thing as a counter-ideal.” He calls the book “the first psychology of the priest.”

Check against the book. Essay III §28 ends with the sentence the review quotes, that man would rather will nothingness than not will, and there Nietzsche says he is repeating what he said at the beginning. III §23 asks where the opposing will is, and §§23–25 test science as a candidate and find it wanting. II §§24–25 hand the future counter-ideal to “Zarathustra the godless.” So the review’s claim has real support in the book.

Tension. The one-sentence summary of Essay I, “the birth of Christianity out of the spirit of resentment” (Ludovici), compresses an argument that passes through a priestly caste and the Jewish people before reaching Christianity (I §§6–8). Read the essay itself before relying on the summary.

Twilight of the Idols · Review §§1–3

What he now emphasizes. The book is short, cheerful, and “fateful,” a quick way into his thought. Its “idols” are “the old truth.” He says he alone has the criterion of truths. §3 narrates the writing of the Revaluation from 3 to 30 September 1888, and the Twilight Preface written on its last day.

Check against the book. In Ludovici’s free translation, the Preface to Twilight is dated Turin, 30 September 1888, “on the day when the first book of the Transvaluation of all Values was finished.” That is the book the site reads as The Antichrist. The Preface’s hammer is also a tuning fork, an instrument for hearing whether an idol is hollow.

Tension. “I alone can decide” sits uneasily beside a book whose method is to test idols by making them sound. Whether the “Revaluation” was one book or the first of several is a question on which Nietzsche’s own texts shift; compare the untitled page in the text differences.

The Case of Wagner · Review §§1–4

What he now emphasizes. Only someone who suffers from “the fate of music” can judge the pamphlet, which he now calls “comparatively mild.” “Everything decisive in this question I kept to myself—I have loved Wagner.” The review then turns to the Germans: Luther, Leibniz and Kant, the wars against Napoleon, nationalism, and the absence of German psychologists. It says he found tact among Jews but never among Germans and compares the Germans to women in having no depths.

Check against the book. The Case of Wagner Preface says, in Ludovici’s translation, “I am just as much a child of my age as Wagner, i.e., I am a decadent,” and “Wagner belongs only to my diseases.” The pamphlet’s argument concerns decadence in art and the musician’s problem. Compare that emphasis with a review that says almost nothing about Wagner’s music.

Tension. The review turns a diagnosis of Wagner into an indictment of a nation. Its closing lines in the critical text, absent from the free translation, show the pressure behind the irony. See the text differences.

Writing exercise

Test one retrospective judgment. Choose one of the three comparisons below, or another review whose earlier book you have read in full.

  • The Birth of Tragedy review §1 on the Christian priests as a “perfidious order of goblins,” against the closing paragraph of Birth §24 and the 1886 “Attempt at a Self-Criticism” §5.
  • Human, All Too Human review §6, against Human, All Too Human I §37 as published in 1878.
  • Zarathustra review §2, against The Gay Science §382 and one speech from Part I of Zarathustra.

Write two short columns. In the first, record exactly what the earlier text says, with section numbers and no more than a sentence of quotation. In the second, record what Ecce Homo says the earlier text said or achieved. Then write a paragraph that answers three questions. Is the late judgment testimony, interpretation, or revision? What does it let you see that the earlier text alone did not? What does it add or remove that the earlier text does not support? End with one sentence stating what the earlier book must still be read for on its own terms.

Where it leads

Back to the books. Ecce Homo sends the reader back to every work it reviews. The most useful return is to a book you read before this one: check which of the late verdicts survives the comparison.

Across to the late polemics. The Antichrist is, on this guide’s reading, the “Revaluation” whose composition the Twilight review describes, though the review does not name it. It develops the opposition between Christianity and life that ends this book. Twilight of the Idols supplies the physiological vocabulary that “Why I Am So Wise” turns on its author.

Toward the Wagner writings. The Case of Wagner gives the diagnosis the review turns into an indictment of the Germans. Nietzsche Contra Wagner takes its Intermezzo from “Why I Am So Clever” §7, so the same passage can be read in two different arrangements.
