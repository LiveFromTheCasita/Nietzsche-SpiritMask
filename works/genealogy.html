Overview and context

A moral judgment can feel self-evident while the history that made it convincing remains hidden. On the Genealogy of Morals asks how people came to praise particular qualities, experience their desires as guilty, and interpret suffering as deserved. Its further question is harder: what do these valuations do to the people who live by them, and what possibilities do they prevent?

Published in 1887 and subtitled A Polemic, the book organizes these questions into three connected essays. Nietzsche contests accounts of morality that begin with useful, unselfish actions and treat their eventual approval as an uncomplicated advance. His dispute with Paul Rée concerns historical explanation; his confrontation with Schopenhauer concerns the value assigned to pity and self-denial. Preface §§4–5 make those targets explicit. The urgency comes from Nietzsche's suspicion that Europe's most admired values may diminish its future.

This is a concentrated return to earlier work. In Preface §§2–4, Nietzsche traces his hypotheses back to Human, All Too Human; Daybreak's investigations of custom recur in the second essay. Beyond Good and Evil, published the previous year, supplies a closer framework of competing valuations. The Genealogy expands and tests these concerns through sustained arguments without settling them into a complete moral system.

Read with two forms of attention: follow what a hypothesis explains, and ask how Nietzsche could know it. Preface §1 begins by unsettling the knower's self-knowledge; §7 demands historical evidence; §8 requires patient rereading. His confidence, caricatures, and conjectural prehistories must answer to those demands too.

Guided reading path

This twelve-lesson course covers the complete Preface and all three essays in order, including the First Essay’s closing Note and the Third Essay’s opening epigraph. Follow the formation of moral values, the history of conscience, and the ascetic ideal before asking what authorizes the value of truth itself. Allow roughly 21–27 hours for the primary text, commentary, rereading, and writing.

Each lesson gives a precise assignment, close commentary, a difficulty to examine, five questions with answers to reveal after trying them, and a writing exercise. Read in your preferred edition; every lesson also links to a credited free alternative.

Reading 1 establishes the question of value. Readings 2–4 complete Essay I; Readings 5–7 complete Essay II; Readings 8–12 complete Essay III. Keep your opening notes: the final exercise asks you to reconsider one value through all three essays and explain the standards of your own judgment. Longer lessons can be divided across two sittings.

If you have an hour

For a short independent orientation, read Preface §6, Essay I §10, and Essay III §28. They introduce the value of values, ressentiment as a source of valuation, and the meaning the ascetic ideal gives to suffering. Allow about 50–60 minutes for the passages and a few notes. These three landmarks open questions that the full course develops in context.

Choose an edition and find passages

The site’s preferred translation is Walter Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale’s On the Genealogy of Morals. The credited free alternative is Horace B. Samuel’s translation. Use the Preface or essay number and then the internal section: I §10 means the First Essay, section 10. Numbering restarts in each essay. English titles and page numbers can vary.

Samuel often writes resentment where this guide uses ressentiment; the latter names the enduring, value-creating pattern examined in I §10. Watch the relationship between guilt and debt in Essay II: English terms such as Samuel’s ought/owe can make the connection less immediately visible. A translation choice is a reason to read the surrounding argument carefully.

The First Essay ends with Nietzsche’s unnumbered Note after §17; include it in Reading 4. The Third Essay begins with a short epigraph from Thus Spoke Zarathustra; include it in Reading 8. Editorial notes and the translator’s footnotes are distinct from Nietzsche’s text. The appended Peoples and Countries, translated by J. M. Kennedy, is separate from Samuel’s Genealogy and from this course.

Begin with three questions

Preface §6 asks what moral values make possible or obstruct. Write down one value you normally admire and separate the question of how you acquired it from the reasons you have for maintaining it. I §10 then contrasts a judgment that begins in self-affirmation with one that begins by condemning an enemy. Ask what evidence would distinguish these patterns in a particular case.

III §28 credits the ascetic ideal with making suffering meaningful while questioning the direction it gives to life. Identify both its achievement and its cost before deciding whether Nietzsche’s criticism convinces you. Return to your chosen value: what does it enable, and what would it take to judge its effects fairly?

Keep the explanation and the judgment visible

In the full course, keep three brief notes for each argument: the claim, the evidence offered for it, and the standard used to evaluate what it describes. Nietzsche’s speculative histories, physiological explanations, and social hierarchies need scrutiny alongside his insights. Disagreeing well requires stating his claim accurately before assessing its support.

Read the essays as connected inquiries. Punishment’s changing purposes in Essay II complicate simple stories about origins. The priest in Essay III works upon capacities for self-accusation already investigated in Essay II. The final challenge to truth returns to the Preface’s question about knowers who do not yet know themselves.

Core ideas

Origins open a question about value

Preface §6 distinguishes asking where values came from from asking what they are worth. An origin in fear or domination does not by itself prove a present practice worthless. Its effects, its transformations, and the kind of life it supports still require judgment. Nietzsche's criteria include strength, creativity, and future human possibilities; his preference for exceptional people makes whose future counts a central difficulty.

II §12 adds a second distinction: a practice's present purpose need not explain its emergence. Established procedures can be appropriated for new ends. Genealogy therefore follows changing interpretations and struggles over use, rather than reading today's justification backwards into the beginning. Nietzsche's further claim that life everywhere works through domination is a substantive hypothesis, not something the distinction itself proves.

Ressentiment can create a system of values

In I §10, ressentiment becomes morally creative when blocked hostility generates an enduring way of valuing. Noble valuation begins by affirming its own way of life and subsequently calls other lives bad. Reactive valuation begins with an evil enemy and defines its own goodness against that enemy. The contrast concerns how the judgment is formed; occasional anger alone does not establish the whole pattern.

Nietzsche favors the first movement, but I §11 admits the nobles' brutal violence outside their own circle. Their self-affirmation cannot establish that their victims have no reasons to object. The reader must distinguish investigating the pleasures of condemnation from dismissing resistance to actual injury. Neither social power nor the ability to feel innocent supplies its own justification.

Responsibility has a difficult history

II §1 treats forgetting as an active capacity that makes room for present life. Promising requires a selective counterforce: a will able to maintain a commitment across time. §2 imagines the sovereign individual as the mature product who can finally move beyond compulsory custom, while §3 exposes the coercion and pain behind the training of memory.

The result is a tension between cultivated independence and its violent social conditions. The sovereign individual's confidence and hierarchical contempt should both remain visible. This conscience of responsibility also differs from the self-accusing bad conscience investigated later. Nietzsche is examining several formations of conscience, not using one word for an unchanging moral faculty.

Bad conscience and priestly guilt are different developments

II §§16–18 propose that instincts denied an outward outlet turn against their possessor. This internalization produces self-cruelty and an enlarged inner life, including new powers of imagination and self-formation. Nietzsche presents a hypothesis about a forced social transformation; it is not an established anthropology, and its creative results do not vindicate the coercion.

A further strand concerns debt. II §§4–6 locate obligations within creditor–debtor relations, while §§19–22 follow their religious transformation into an apparently unpayable debt. Later, the ascetic priest redirects sufferers' hostility towards themselves in III §15 and interprets their condition as sin in §20. He works upon a capacity for self-torment whose emergence the previous essay has already investigated. Internalization, indebtedness, and priestly interpretation interact; they should not be compressed into one event.

An ideal can preserve life while condemning it

III §13 states the paradox: the ascetic ideal helps suffering people persist by giving their lives a purpose, even when that purpose devalues earthly existence. Discipline itself has no single meaning. In III §§7–8, a philosopher's selective renunciations can protect the conditions for intellectual work. Their value depends on what they serve.

The inquiry finally reaches its own commitment to truth. III §§24–25 argue that unconditional faith in truth continues the ascetic ideal. Nietzsche presents science as allied with that ideal through their shared, unquestioned valuation of truth. This does not make every interpretation equally sound. III §12 describes objectivity as the ability to bring different affective perspectives under disciplined control and use them to know an object more fully. Criticism needs better inquiry while also examining why inquiry matters.

Three-essay walkthrough

The essays change their object: first the formation of opposed valuations, then the making and moralization of conscience, finally the ascetic ideal's power to organize suffering and knowledge. Their arguments overlap, but each introduces explanatory steps the others need.

Preface, §§1–8: Why put morality in question?

§§1–4 move from the knower's unfamiliarity with himself to the history of Nietzsche's questions and his disagreements with other genealogists. §§5–7 identify the value of morality as the governing problem and call for evidence about what people actually practiced. §8 makes interpretation a demanding activity: understanding an aphorism requires working through it. The extended third essay becomes an example of that labor. Keep the preface's methodological aspirations beside the essays' actual performance.

Essay I, §§1–17: How do good/bad and good/evil diverge?

§§1–5 challenge explanations that derive goodness from its usefulness to recipients. Nietzsche instead follows naming, rank, and proposed word histories. §5 also invokes supposed Aryan racial characteristics and speculative links between ancestry and political movements. These are part of his attempted explanation and require criticism; philological conjecture does not validate a racial hierarchy.

§§6–9 trace a conflict between warrior and priestly valuations, then present Judaism and Christianity as vehicles of a reversal. §7 and §8 attribute collective revenge and elaborate strategy to Jews. These anti-Jewish generalizations are not evidence for a people's shared motives. The argument's account of moral reversal must be assessed separately from the collective psychology with which Nietzsche dramatizes it.

§§10–12 develop the contrast between self-affirmation and reactive valuation, then ask whether civilization has purchased safety at the cost of human possibilities. §§13–15 examine the imagined doer behind deeds; Nietzsche then interprets promises of heavenly justice as concealed revenge. The lambs and birds of prey in §13 challenge the idea of a chooser wholly separable from its actions; their roles also load the argument against the injured.

§§16–17 recast European history through the polemical opposition of Rome and Judea. The essay ends by retaining distinctions of value beyond good and evil, and asking what different rankings promote. Its historical drama and its preference for aristocratic achievement need arguments beyond the drama's force.

The unnumbered closing Note turns this challenge into a research program: philology should investigate moral language, and physiology and medicine should contribute to evaluating valuations. Nietzsche assigns philosophers the task of ordering values. His question of value for what and for whom expressly opposes majority welfare to the development of stronger types.

Essay II, §§1–25: How does cruelty become inward?

§§1–3 establish the problem of a promising animal and the long training of memory. §§4–10 develop the creditor–debtor hypothesis: injury can be compensated through an authorized pleasure in inflicting pain, and communal punishment extends relations of obligation. §10 links increasing communal power to milder punishment, then imagines a society strong enough to spare offenders altogether. Power here includes the capacity to forgo retaliation.

The discussion of cruelty must confront §7's racist and sexist comparisons of sensitivity to pain. Nietzsche supplies no reliable basis for treating those group rankings as facts. His larger question about how suffering acquires meaning can be pursued without adopting them.

§§11–15 distinguish law from private revenge, separate origins from purposes, and catalogue punishment's changing uses. Nietzsche argues that punishment chiefly teaches caution and distrust rather than producing remorse. §12 also calls sacrificing humanity to a stronger type progress: that hierarchy is an additional value judgment requiring scrutiny, not a consequence of recognizing historical change.

§§16–18 offer the internalization hypothesis and explore its destructive and productive consequences. §§19–23 return to debt, extending it to ancestors and gods before examining its moralization. Crucially, §21 qualifies §20's prospect that atheism might release inherited guilt: moralized self-accusation complicates that inference. The contrasting Greek gods in §23 show that religion need not perform one psychological function.

§§24–25 ask whether the force used against natural impulses could instead be directed against ideals hostile to life. Nietzsche announces a future creator in prophetic terms, then turns towards Zarathustra without providing a completed practical solution.

Essay III, §§1–28: What gives suffering a purpose?

The epigraph from Thus Spoke Zarathustra personifies wisdom as a woman who loves a warrior. Its gendered image invites a question for the essay: what kind of struggle does inquiry require?

§§1–5 begin with the ascetic ideal's different users and Wagner's relation to chastity and philosophical authority. §§6–10 move from aesthetic disinterest to the philosopher's conditions of work and the religious disguise through which philosophy acquired permission to exist. The arguments about marriage and women in §7 and §8 turn a narrowly male model of intellectual life into a general rule; the exclusions are part of what needs examining.

§§11–16 confront the priest's valuation of life, develop perspectival objectivity, and explain the ideal as a means of preservation. Nietzsche's hierarchy of healthy and sick becomes explicit in §14, including a misogynistic account of sick women. His proposal to separate the exceptional from sufferers depends on disputed judgments about whose claims matter. §15 then identifies the redirection of resentment as the priest's distinctive intervention.

§§17–22 distinguish ways of managing distress: reduced stimulation, absorbing work, small reciprocal benefits, community, and intense emotional excitation. §20 acknowledges the priest's sincere belief in the remedy while criticizing its consequences. These conjectural physiological explanations are not validated medicine. The key distinction is between relieving felt distress and addressing its causes.

§§23–28 ask where a rival ideal could come from. Nietzsche argues that unconditional devotion to truth continues ascetic valuation; art's willingness to create appearances offers a provisional contrast in §25. §27 follows truthfulness into criticism of its own authority. §28 concludes that the ascetic ideal gave suffering meaning and preserved willing, at the cost of directing the will against life. Explaining that achievement leaves the question of another purpose open.

Art appears as a provisional contrast because it can affirm created appearances; this is not a license to abandon evidence or to treat any convenient falsehood as justified.Read III §23 in the free edition Read III §24 in the free edition Read III §25 in the free edition
  • III §26; III §27 — Truthfulness becomes a problem for itself — The criticism of modern intellectual postures leads to truthfulness questioning the authority that cultivated it and eventually its own value.   • III §28 — A purpose that preserves a will to nothingness — The conclusion credits the ascetic ideal with giving suffering a purpose and preserving the ability to will. Its cost is a direction hostile to the conditions of life. Where it leads

Return to the earlier experiments

Read Human, All Too Human for the provisional moral psychology that the preface revisits, and Daybreak for the authority of custom and the difficulty of changing moral feelings. Compare the explanatory scale: when does a local observation become a general history, and what further evidence would that move require?

The Gay Science brings the value of truth into especially close focus: III §24 explicitly returns to its §344, and III §27 draws on §357. Thus Spoke Zarathustra gives a different literary form to the challenge of willing and creating after inherited purposes lose authority. The Genealogy's second essay closes by handing its future-facing question to that figure.

Follow the polemic into the later books

Keep Beyond Good and Evil beside the essays as their immediate predecessor. Then continue through the corpus to The Case of Wagner, Twilight of the Idols, and The Antichrist. Carry forward a specific question: how do these later polemics distinguish a persuasive diagnosis from a justified valuation? The Genealogy equips that question without making its author's own judgments exempt from it.
