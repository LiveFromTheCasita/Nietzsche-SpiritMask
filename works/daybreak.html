Essence & Context

Daybreak asks how moral judgments acquire their extraordinary hold on us. A duty can feel unquestionable; a forbidden desire can feel guilty before we have examined either the duty or the desire. Nietzsche turns toward the histories, habits, and interpretations that make these feelings possible. The question is not only whether we can give reasons for our values. It is how we became people for whom certain values seem to need no reasons at all.

Published in 1881, with the subtitle Thoughts on the Prejudices of Morality, the book develops the historical and psychological inquiry of Human, All Too Human. Its 575 numbered aphorisms are arranged in five books. Their subjects range from religious guilt and the authority of custom to the interpretation of experience, the praise of work, relations between people, and the pleasures and dangers of knowledge. The short form lets Nietzsche approach a problem through different examples without resolving every tension between them.

The title’s promise is deliberately open. What follows the weakening of inherited moral certainty is a beginning whose possibilities still need to be discovered. §453 describes an interval in which old judgments lose authority while new ways of living remain experimental. This gives the book its distinctive combination of suspicion and patience: changing an argument may be quick, but changing what one instinctively fears, admires, or condemns can take much longer.

A later preface to an earlier undertaking

Nietzsche added the preface in 1886. It looks back on the book as a patient undermining of confidence in morality and raises a complication in Preface §4: the demand for honesty that drives this criticism has itself been inherited from moral discipline. The critic has a history too. Read the preface as a later interpretation of the undertaking, while allowing the 1881 aphorisms their own questions and emphases.

The book’s argument deserves to be understood before it is compressed into a slogan. In §103, Nietzsche explicitly distinguishes denying sincere moral motives from denying the truth of the judgments on which those motives depend. He adopts the second position. People can act from moral conviction, and many familiar actions can still be encouraged, while the reasons offered for them change. Understanding that distinction makes the rest of the book considerably less easy to dismiss—and more demanding to assess.

How to Begin

For a guided introduction, begin with What Does It Mean to Question Morality?, the first session in the Essential Reading Path. It takes you through §103 with explanations and questions.

Choose an edition and keep the section numbers

The site’s preferred edition is R. J. Hollingdale’s translation, edited by Maudemarie Clark and Brian Leiter. The free alternative is J. M. Kennedy’s translation, titled The Dawn of Day. Both contain the same numbered work. Use the aphorism numbers as your coordinates; page numbers and English headings vary. Kennedy’s introduction and footnotes are commentary by the translator, distinct from Nietzsche’s text.

Translation matters especially around pity, compassion, and sympathy. The discussion of Mitleid should not automatically be expanded into a rejection of everything now called kindness or empathy. Ask what experience Nietzsche is examining in each passage: sharing another person’s suffering, relieving one’s own distress, taking practical action, or making selflessness the standard of moral worth.

Read a few aphorisms with room between them

§454 recommends brief encounters with the book, interrupted by other activity and changes of surroundings. The 1886 Preface §5 gives slow reading a more explicit defense. A useful practice is to identify one claim, the example meant to support it, and a case it might fail to explain. On returning, read the neighboring sections and see whether they complicate your first interpretation.

For orientation, §103 clarifies the kind of criticism being undertaken; §9 gives a central account of custom; and §453 opens the question of what might follow. They supply different bearings for reading the whole work. The five-book walkthrough below then helps you locate the larger changes of emphasis without making every aphorism fit a single argument.

Give the hypotheses something to answer to

Many passages offer conjectural histories or striking psychological explanations. A motive that could explain an action has not thereby been shown to explain it. Ask what evidence Nietzsche provides, what alternatives remain, and whether his own account of our limited self-knowledge should make him more cautious. The same discipline applies to his discussions of women, peoples, and colonial expansion: the book’s challenge to inherited prejudice also supplies questions to put to its author.

Core Ideas

Custom can make independence feel immoral

In §9, morality is examined through obedience to inherited practice. What matters within this form of valuation is that a person follows tradition; an independently chosen action can be suspect even when its outcome is useful. The innovator therefore faces more than resistance from other people. Conscience itself may condemn the departure. §14 and §18 explore the severe forms through which a break with custom could be authorized or paid for.

This is one reason moral criticism requires historical imagination. A judgment that now feels immediate may carry the effects of social conditions we no longer recognize. But an account of where a judgment came from and an argument about whether it is justified remain distinct tasks. Nietzsche’s reconstructions can unsettle an assumption’s apparent inevitability without settling every question about its present value.

Our feelings have interpretations inside them

§38 proposes that a drive acquires a different emotional character when a community names it a virtue or a vice. The person learns to experience a disposition through approval, shame, or fear. Moral language participates in forming the experience it describes. This helps explain why abandoning a belief intellectually may leave its associated guilt intact.

The inquiry then turns inward. In §109, the wish to control a drive is itself drawn into a contest among drives; in §115 and §116, familiar words and conscious intentions prove inadequate to explain the whole person. §119 uses dreams and ordinary encounters to explore how different needs make different experiences out of apparently similar events. These are philosophical investigations to test, not established findings about the nervous system. Their force lies in making our everyday confidence about motives answerable to questions.

Criticizing compassion changes the question of helping

Nietzsche challenges the elevation of sympathetic suffering into the foundation of morality. §133 asks what actually moves a helper and emphasizes a mixture of possible motives. §134 asks whether sharing another person’s pain can sometimes obstruct effective assistance. Together they make room for examining the quality and consequences of help instead of treating an intense feeling as its own justification.

The criticism also carries a harder question about whose flourishing counts. In §146, Nietzsche’s defense of distant intellectual and cultural aims extends to the sacrifice of other people. That claim cannot be dissolved into a harmless invitation to set personal boundaries. It exposes a serious ethical difficulty in the book: when immediate obligations lose their automatic authority, what limits the claims made in the name of a supposedly higher future?

Independence has social conditions

§173 asks why exhausting work receives moral praise. Labor can use up the time and energy through which a person might question the surrounding order. §179 examines the intellectual resources absorbed by political life. These passages make independence depend on arrangements of attention and activity, as well as on courage in thought.

Nietzsche’s concern for independence remains selective and often hierarchical. §206 attacks the loss of personality in factory work, yet its proposed escape includes colonial expansion. §262 identifies a restless desire for power, while §548 finds an achievement in bringing power under the direction of reason. Read these differences closely before turning the book into either a modern political program or a general endorsement of domination.

Inquiry needs a future without a final master

§453 describes new ways of living as experiments. The sciences can contribute knowledge on which ideals are built, but the passage explicitly refuses to make them the source of ready-made ideals. Learning how people develop does not automatically decide which possibilities should be cultivated.

That openness extends to knowledge itself. §547 questions the ambition to solve everything through one thinker’s achievement, and §550 asks whether delight in knowing can itself threaten honesty. The final aphorism, §575, imagines inquiry continuing beyond any individual’s limits. Its hope includes the possibility of exhaustion and shipwreck. The new morning offers work and possibility rather than a guarantee about where they will lead.

A Walkthrough of the Five Books

The descriptions below identify recurring emphases. Nietzsche gives the divisions numbers, and their subjects overlap. Read the changes of scale—from inherited custom to inward experience, social arrangements, and the future of inquiry—without expecting each book to complete one stage of a system.

Book I, §§1–96: Custom, conscience, and religious interpretation

The opening book makes familiar valuations strange by giving them possible histories. §9, §14, and §18 examine obedience, innovation, and the religious interpretation of suffering. §38 connects the moral naming of drives to the feelings that accompany them. Together they ask how an inherited order becomes something a person experiences from within.

The religious investigations bring that method to Christianity. §68 reads Paul through his struggle with the law; §76 examines the effect of condemning sexual feeling; §84 criticizes interpretive dishonesty. Nietzsche’s account of Paul is speculative and polemical, so the last passage also supplies a useful question for the earlier one: Where does evidence end and the interpreter’s preferred story begin?

Book II, §§97–148: Action, self-knowledge, and sympathetic morality

§103 clarifies what it means here to deny morality. The investigations of drives and consciousness then make the explanation of action increasingly difficult. §109 questions the independence of the will that attempts self-control, while §§115–119 examine how much escapes the account we give of ourselves. Keep asking whether a compelling explanation of another person’s motive has earned its certainty.

In §§132–134 the inquiry turns toward prevailing ideals of service and sympathy. These passages distinguish the helper’s feelings, the sufferer’s experience, and the effects of intervention. §146 then presses beyond immediate neighbors toward distant purposes, with an explicit willingness to sacrifice others. The question to carry onward is demanding: What could justify a changed valuation, and who would bear its costs?

Book III, §§149–207: Work, politics, and social life

The scale widens to institutions, public ideals, national cultures, and the allocation of human effort. §173 examines the praise of toil, §174 the morality of a commercially organized community, and §189 the popular appetite for national power. Nietzsche asks what forms of person a social order rewards and what possibilities it uses up.

The closing stretch includes §206, which contests the idea that higher wages alone could remedy factory life’s destruction of individuality. Its proposed alternative also reveals the limits of Nietzsche’s concern: the freedom imagined for European workers includes colonial domination elsewhere. Ask: Whose independence is being defended, and whose freedom disappears from the account?

Book IV, §§208–422: Power, character, and relationships

The book with the most aphorisms ranges through compact observations about ambition, habits, social judgment, affection, and self-deception. It rewards comparison more than a single outline. §262 makes the desire for power a psychological problem; §359 uses marriage to distinguish a practice’s acceptance from a justification of its value. The ordinary explanation—everyone accepts this, or I have chosen it—becomes something to investigate.

This is also a place to test the reach of the method. §346 turns suspicion toward men’s hostility to women, while §403 proposes gender differences through what Nietzsche calls perfect women and men. §272 questions primordial racial purity but goes on to rank forms of mixture and purification. These tensions should remain visible. Ask: Does the observer submit his own preferred distinctions to the scrutiny he gives everyone else’s?

Book V, §§423–575: Knowledge and experiments in living

The final book gives increasing attention to the thinker’s practices, pleasures, and limitations. §424 separates the truth of a claim from its power to console. §453 considers life during an interval between moral certainties, and §454 pauses to reflect on reading itself. Inquiry becomes a way of arranging one’s attention and life.

Toward the end, §547 questions the ambition to explain everything in one final system; §548 values the work of bringing power under reason; and §550 examines the pleasure of knowledge. §575 leaves thinkers in flight, with further horizons and real limits. The closing question is: How can inquiry remain worth pursuing when neither a final answer nor your own arrival is assured?

Thematic References

These groups gather passages for closer study. They are a reference map: follow a question across the book, compare the examples, and return to the surrounding aphorisms. The short titles below are this guide’s descriptions, not quotations of the translated headings.

Custom and the formation of conscience↳ Genealogy across the corpus

The opening investigations ask how obedience becomes good conscience and departure becomes guilt. They also suggest that communities help shape the drives they evaluate. Treat the proposed histories as explanations that need evidence, while attending to the question they make possible: how much of what seems inward and self-evident has been learned?

4 annotated references
  • §9 — The morality of custom — Nietzsche describes a form of valuation in which obedience to inherited practice matters more than an action’s independently chosen purpose. Originality can therefore appear immoral even when beneficial. The passage helps explain why breaking with convention may require a struggle with conscience as well as with public disapproval.Read §9 in the free edition
  • §14 — The difficult authorization of innovation — Where custom monopolizes legitimate authority, apparent divine madness can make a departure permissible. Nietzsche explores the terrible pressures this places on innovators, including attempts to produce the state that would authorize them. His broad historical and medical assertions should be examined rather than repeated as established facts.Read §14 in the free edition
  • §18 — When suffering becomes a virtue — Voluntary suffering is explained through beliefs about cruel gods, sacrifice, and protection of the community. Nietzsche then links this inheritance to innovators who feel that independence requires compensation. The proposed history asks how pain acquired moral prestige; it does not establish that suffering has one meaning in every culture.Read §18 in the free edition
  • §38 — How a drive acquires a moral character — Related dispositions can receive different names and accompanying feelings under different moral interpretations. The contrast between cowardice and humility illustrates how valuation enters experience. A good or bad conscience is part of what a person learns to feel, not simply a neutral report about an unchanged inner event.Read §38 in the free edition

Religion, guilt, and the persistence of moral habits↳ Christianity across the corpus

Religious doctrines become objects of psychological interpretation, but the inquiry also concerns what survives their rejection. A person may abandon a belief while retaining habits of guilt or ideals of self-denial. Keep the explanation of a belief’s attraction separate from evidence about its truth, and apply Nietzsche’s demand for honest interpretation to his own reconstructions.

4 annotated references
  • §68 — Paul and the burden of the law — Nietzsche interprets Paul’s conversion as a response to the anguish of failing to fulfill the law. A personal crisis is made central to an account of Christianity’s direction. This is a speculative psychological portrait; its assertions about Paul’s illness, conduct, and inner life are not established biographical evidence.Read §68 in the free edition
  • §76 — Condemning a passion — The condemnation of sexual feeling is said to generate recurring inward misery and intensify the fascination of the forbidden. Nietzsche asks what that condemnation does to the person who inherits it. The passage makes a distinction worth testing: a reason to regulate a passion need not establish that the passion itself is evil.Read §76 in the free edition
  • §84 — The honesty of interpretation — Nietzsche attacks readings that impose Christian meanings on earlier texts and conceal the uncertainty of their interpretations. The issue is how a desired conclusion governs what someone claims to find. Compare this demand for textual honesty with §68 and ask how much its psychological reconstruction actually demonstrates.Read §84 in the free edition
  • §132 — Moral inheritance after belief — Modern ideals of service and self-denial are traced to Christian moral habits that can outlast Christian dogma. Nietzsche worries that making usefulness to a collective the standard of goodness suppresses individual possibilities. The claim invites examination of continuity in valuation, without establishing a complete history of every form of modern solidarity.Read §132 in the free edition

Drives, interpretation, and the limits of self-knowledge↳ Moral psychology across the corpus

These passages question the self that supposedly knows its motives and directs its desires from an independent position. Language, conscious intention, and retrospective explanation give partial accounts. Nietzsche’s alternatives are themselves hypotheses: the reader should resist exchanging an overconfident account of rational choice for an equally overconfident diagnosis of hidden drives.

4 annotated references
  • §109 — What wants self-control? — After describing six ways of weakening an impulse, Nietzsche asks what motivates the attempt. The intellect appears within a conflict among drives, serving a rival tendency rather than standing entirely outside desire. Read the methods as material for this philosophical question, not as a contemporary treatment program.Read §109 in the free edition
  • §115 — The self our vocabulary reveals — Words pick out conspicuous states while leaving quieter processes largely unrecognized. Nietzsche adds that the incomplete picture we form of ourselves can influence what we become. The limitation is therefore productive as well as concealing: a self-description can help shape the person it fails fully to describe.Read §115 in the free edition
  • §116 — The unknown causes of action — Nietzsche challenges the confidence with which we explain both our actions and those of others. Recognizing what seems good does not disclose everything that brings about an action. The passage raises a difficulty for judgment: consciously available reasons may not be a complete account of what has happened.Read §116 in the free edition
  • §119 — Experience as something interpreted — Dreams and contrasting reactions to ordinary encounters suggest that drives help determine what an event means to us. The closing questions extend the uncertainty to the distinction between experiencing and inventing. Preserve their exploratory force; they do not amount to a demonstration that reality is imaginary or that every interpretation is equally warranted.Read §119 in the free edition

Compassion, helping, and the grounds of action↳ Suffering and cruelty across the corpus

Nietzsche separates moral conviction from its truth, and the feeling of sympathy from the practical quality of assistance. These distinctions complicate an easy opposition between selfishness and goodness. They also open a difficult space in which his preference for distant purposes can license sacrifices imposed on other people. Read the inquiry and its costs together.

5 annotated references
  • §103 — What denying morality means — Nietzsche distinguishes denying moral motives from denying the truth of the judgments that inform them, and adopts the second position. Many actions commonly called moral should still be encouraged, but for changed reasons. He imagines learning to feel differently as well as learning to judge differently.Read §103 in the free edition
  • §133 — The mixed motives of helping — Pity is analyzed through several possible responses, including distress, fear, pride, indignation, and pleasure in effective action. Nietzsche explicitly rejects a single-motive explanation. The helper’s feeling also need not reproduce the sufferer’s experience, which complicates the claim that sympathy gives immediate access to another person’s condition.Read §133 in the free edition
  • §134 — Steadiness and assistance — Nietzsche asks whether sharing pain can multiply suffering and impair the ability to help. The physician offers an example of assistance requiring some composure. Examine the distinction between effective concern and emotional contagion without assuming that every form of compassion fits the passage’s stark generalizations.Read §134 in the free edition
  • §146 — The claims of a distant future — Attention to immediate neighbors is challenged in the name of longer intellectual and cultural aims. Nietzsche also explicitly contemplates sacrificing other people to those aims. That extension is a serious ethical problem: a future purpose does not become justified merely because it requires courage or looks beyond the present.Read §146 in the free edition
  • §174 — Cultivation and constant intervention — The moral preferences of a commercial community are linked to fear and the demand for security. Nietzsche questions whether incessant helping becomes superficial or intrusive, and suggests that cultivating oneself may benefit others differently. The garden image invites comparison of forms of contribution, not an assumption that withdrawal always serves everyone best.Read §174 in the free edition

Work, power, and social order↳ Independence across the corpus

Public ideals organize time, attention, and opportunity. Nietzsche asks what the praise of work or national greatness does to the people who accept it. His defense of independence also carries exclusions and hierarchies, especially in his treatment of colonial expansion and peoples. The passages on power should be read with those consequences and with his valuation of self-command in view.

7 annotated references
  • §173 — Whose interests does the praise of work serve? — Exhausting labor can consume the energy needed for thought, desire, and independence, making it a means of social discipline. Nietzsche asks why collective security receives priority over individual development. The target is severe toil and its glorification; the passage does not condemn every purposeful or satisfying activity.Read §173 in the free edition
  • §179 — The attention absorbed by the state — Political administration and daily political preoccupation are accused of diverting gifted minds from other tasks. Nietzsche ranks intellectual aims and the people capable of pursuing them. His call to reduce the state’s claims on attention should be assessed within that hierarchy rather than translated directly into a present-day party platform.Read §179 in the free edition
  • §189 — The popular appetite for national power — The desire to feel power is examined in populations as well as rulers. National enthusiasm can help sustain conquest and obscure injustice, while conquerors and conquered evaluate the same events differently. The question is how identification with collective strength alters what people permit themselves to admire.Read §189 in the free edition
  • §206 — Factory life and a compromised escape — Higher wages alone are said to leave untouched the loss of personality in mechanized labor. Nietzsche rejects both industrial servitude and dependence on socialist promises, but proposes escape through overseas colonization and European mastery. The passage’s racial stereotypes and colonial alternative belong to its argument and limit its claim to defend freedom.Read §206 in the free edition
  • §262 — The desire that survives satisfaction — Material comfort and security are presented as insufficient to quiet the desire for power. The closing reference to Luther locates that desire within religious language too. Treat this as a sweeping psychological hypothesis to compare with cases, rather than as a completed metaphysical doctrine of will to power.Read §262 in the free edition
  • §272 — Mixture and the hierarchy of purification — Nietzsche challenges the idea of primordial racial purity, then develops an evaluative account of purification and makes derogatory claims about mixed peoples. The opening cannot stand for the whole passage. Ask how a historical account of cultural formation becomes a hierarchy, and what evidence could support that transition.Read §272 in the free edition
  • §548 — Power brought under direction — The valued spectacle is power mastered, ordered, and placed in the service of reason. Nietzsche also locates an intellectual achievement in the thinker’s work on themselves, beyond the works an audience can admire. Compare §262: recognizing the desire for power leaves open the question of how power should be organized and used.Read §548 in the free edition

Women, marriage, and inherited judgments↳ Women and gender across the corpus

The book’s suspicion can uncover motives behind hostility toward women and unsettle conventional approval of marriage. It can also coexist with broad gender stereotypes. These passages put the method close to home: explaining a judgment’s attraction is useful, but the observer must still justify the distinctions through which the explanation is offered.

3 annotated references
  • §346 — Hostility turned outward — A man’s declarations against women are interpreted as unbridled desire coming to hate itself and its objects. Psychological suspicion here targets misogyny. This possibility should be recognized without making the passage cancel the author’s own gendered generalizations elsewhere.Read §346 in the free edition
  • §359 — Approval is not a justification — Marriage supplies an example of how ignorance, familiarity, or personal involvement can produce approval. Accepting an institution, or choosing to participate in it, does not by itself establish its value. The brief observation offers an accessible way to distinguish explaining assent from giving reasons for what is assented to.Read §359 in the free edition
  • §403 — A distinction that needs evidence — Nietzsche contrasts a woman’s fear that her lover is unworthy of her with a man’s fear of being unworthy of the woman he loves, specifying what he calls perfect women and men. Ask what supports this proposed pattern and the idealized types behind it. His account of learned valuations offers a way to examine these distinctions too.Read §403 in the free edition

Truthfulness and the temptations of knowledge↳ Inquiry and style across the corpus

Inquiry has its own pleasures, ambitions, and inherited obligations. Nietzsche asks readers to separate consolation from truth and an individual’s desire for greatness from the future of knowledge. The same scrutiny reaches the attraction of inquiry itself: enjoying an explanation and having reason to accept it remain different matters.

4 annotated references
  • §424 — Truth and consolation — A claim’s failure to comfort someone does not count against its truth. Nietzsche accompanies this distinction with a hierarchy between supposedly healthy seekers and suffering people who want remedies. Retain the epistemic question while testing the psychology: a person’s suffering does not by itself establish the quality of their reasoning.Read §424 in the free edition
  • §547 — Beyond the solitary final answer — Nietzsche criticizes the ambition to settle the world’s problems through one thinker’s definitive solution. Knowledge can continue beyond personal importance and an individual lifespan. The passage asks for a change of ambition: what could one contribute if inquiry mattered more than becoming its final authority?Read §547 in the free edition
  • §550 — The pleasure of finding out — Even knowledge of apparently ugly realities can bring pleasure through a definite gain in understanding. Nietzsche then asks whether this delight could compromise honesty. A love of inquiry remains answerable to inquiry; its emotional rewards cannot certify the explanations that produce them.Read §550 in the free edition
  • 1886 Preface §4 — The inherited conscience of criticism — Writing in 1886, Nietzsche presents the withdrawal of confidence in morality as itself driven by a moral demand. Honesty makes a return to discredited ideals unacceptable. The complication is central: criticism does not begin from a place without inherited commitments, and must consider the authority of its own demands.Read 1886 Preface §4 in the free edition

Experimental living and an open future↳ Revaluation across the corpus

The weakening of inherited authority does not immediately supply a replacement way of life. These passages make space for small experiments, patient reading, and work that others may carry further. Their hope depends on continued investigation, with uncertainty about both the values to cultivate and the destination of the inquiry.

4 annotated references
  • §453 — Between moral certainties — Old moral judgments are losing their authority while their successors remain unknown. Nietzsche proposes small experiments in living and explicitly distinguishes the foundations sciences can provide from the ideals they cannot simply deliver. The invitation leaves work for judgment instead of offering a scientific formula for the good life.Read §453 in the free edition
  • §454 — A book to put down and return to — Short encounters, intervals away from the page, and a change of surroundings become part of reading well. Nietzsche asks for ideas to enter the reader’s activity and observation. The passage provides a practical reason to give an aphorism time to generate examples and objections before moving on.Read §454 in the free edition
  • §575 — A voyage beyond one thinker — The ending imagines thinkers in flight, reaching limits that later travelers may pass. Its questions also include disorientation and possible shipwreck. The image makes room for hope without an assurance of progress, and for the value of inquiry without the promise that an individual will reach its destination.Read §575 in the free edition
  • 1886 Preface §5 — Learning to read slowly — The 1886 preface closes by defending patient, attentive work with language against the pressure of haste. Where It Leads

Toward The Gay Science

The Gay Science continues the question of how knowledge can become a way of living. Compare its experiments in joy, style, and the costs of truth with Daybreak’s attention to changing habits of feeling. §550 and §575 provide useful points of contact: the pleasure of inquiry is powerful, but it remains exposed to questions about honesty, endurance, and direction.

The later book gives the death of God and eternal recurrence their own dramatic settings. Daybreak already asks how Christian valuations can survive changing beliefs, especially in §132, but its distinctive work lies in the detailed examination of moral experience. Let that inquiry establish its own importance before approaching it through the later, more famous formulations.

Toward the Genealogy

The accounts of custom, suffering, and conscience prepare questions that return in On the Genealogy of Morals. Compare the kinds of explanation offered: which agents shape a practice, how an interpretation gains authority, and what happens when a feeling is redirected. The genealogy theme provides a way to pursue these connections across works.

Continuity does not require treating Daybreak as a compressed version of the later essays. Its varied aphorisms, its criticism of sympathy, and its uncertainty about future ideals belong to a particular undertaking. The 1886 Preface §4 supplies a further bridge by asking how moral honesty can turn against the inherited morality that cultivated it.

Toward a more demanding account of independence

Beyond Good and Evil develops questions about drives, philosophical motives, and the rank of values. When moving onward, keep Daybreak’s account of limited self-knowledge in view. It should make readers cautious about both the philosopher’s claims to diagnose others and their own satisfaction in possessing a diagnosis.

Daybreak makes the authority of our values into a question while leaving the work of living with that question open. Its most fruitful demand is a double attention: investigate what has formed a judgment, then ask what reasons remain for making it. That demand applies as much to the claims we encounter in Nietzsche as to the convictions we bring to him.
