Overview and context

The Case of Wagner is a short book written as a letter, headed “A Letter from Turin, May 1888” and published later that year. It has a Preface, twelve numbered sections signed at the end with Nietzsche’s name, two postscripts, and an Epilogue. It begins with an evening at Bizet’s Carmen and ends with a definition of the modern person as a “contradiction of values.” Between those points it moves through four kinds of claim, often in a single paragraph: description of Wagner’s music and dramaturgy; claims about its effects on listeners; a diagnosis of those effects as decadence, in the vocabulary of illness and physiology; and a valuation that ranks ages, moralities, and kinds of people as ascending or declining life.

This guide asks you to keep the four apart. A description can be tested by listening and by reading a score or libretto. A claim about effects needs evidence about listeners. A diagnosis needs a theory of what counts as illness and some way to establish it independently of the taste it is meant to explain. The valuation is the hardest to test, and the Epilogue says outright that such “optics” cannot be refuted by argument. The book’s force comes from moving quickly between these levels. Its readers’ task is to slow the movement down.

Chronology matters here. Nietzsche’s friendship with Wagner belongs to the late 1860s and the 1870s. The Birth of Tragedy (1872) opened with a foreword addressed to Wagner, and the fourth Untimely Meditation (1876) praised him at length. Human, All Too Human (1878) marked the break, largely without naming him. Wagner died in 1883. This book comes a dozen years after the break and five years after Wagner’s death, in the year of Twilight of the Idols, The Antichrist, Ecce Homo, and Nietzsche Contra Wagner. The Preface is itself a retrospect: Nietzsche says he was a Wagnerite and a decadent, and that the break was a recovery. Keep that account distinct from what the earlier texts actually said, and from the later retellings in Ecce Homo and Nietzsche Contra Wagner.

The book states its hierarchies plainly, and this guide does too. It praises a conception of love whose “very essence is the mortal hatred between the sexes” (§2, Ludovici’s translation), calls woman’s love “a refined form of parasitism” (§3), and explains Wagner’s success “with nerves, and therefore with women” (§5). It defines “a German” as “an obedient man with long legs” (§11; see the variants). A footnote to the Postscript asks whether Wagner was “a German at all,” and in the German text it adds a pun on his stepfather’s name that can be read as an insinuation about ancestry. The Epilogue treats scorn and horror of decadents as almost a proof of noble taste. These are part of the argument, not incidental colour. The passage notes cite each one.

Carry one question through the book: What would a listener who disagrees need to be shown? Ask it of each claim about the music, the audience, the Germans, and the modern soul.

Guided reading path

This path covers all of Nietzsche’s text in its printed order: the title and motto, the Preface, §§1–12, the Postscript with Nietzsche’s footnote, the Second Postscript, and the Epilogue with Nietzsche’s footnote. The seven stages are this guide’s teaching divisions, not Nietzsche’s. Stages 1–3 build the case: a confession, a counter-image, and the announcement of a diagnosis. Stages 4–5 give the theory of decadent style and the actor, then the letter’s closing demands. Stages 6–7 widen the case to an institution, a nation, all modern music, and modern values.

Only §§1–12 are numbered. Cite the Preface, Postscript, Second Postscript, and Epilogue by name, with a phrase to locate the passage. In the free text, all footnotes are printed at the end of the volume. Three of them are Nietzsche’s and belong to the assignment; the others are the translator’s. See the edition notes before relying on a footnote.

  1. Stage 1Title, subtitle, and motto; Nietzsche’s Preface

    Read: the title page of the work, the heading “A Letter from Turin, May 1888,” the motto “Ridendo dicere severum,” and the Preface. Stop at §1, which begins “Yesterday—would you believe it?—I heard Bizet’s masterpiece.” The free text prints the heading and motto before the Preface; the critical text places them after it. See the variants.

    Why this stage: The Preface sets the terms of everything that follows. Nietzsche admits that his praise of Bizet is not free of malice. He calls turning his back on Wagner a fate and learning to like anything afterward a triumph. He declares himself “just as much a child of my age as Wagner—i.e., … a decadent” (Ludovici), and makes decadence his deepest problem, with “good and evil” only a subdivision of it. He then insists that the philosopher cannot do without Wagner, who is modernity’s most intimate guide. The motto adapts Horace’s formula for telling the truth while laughing into “saying something severe while laughing.”

    Questions: Does the confession make Nietzsche a better witness against Wagner, or a less impartial one? The Preface calls Wagner both harmful and indispensable. What is the difference between needing Wagner as a guide to the modern soul and needing him as a model?

  2. Stage 2§§1–3

    Read: the three Bizet sections: orchestration and the “first principle” of Nietzsche’s aesthetics (§1), Carmen’s climate and its conception of love (§2), then the catalogue of Wagnerian redemptions, the Goethe episode, and holiness (§3). Stop at §4, which begins “I shall once more relate the history of the ‘Ring.’”

    Why this stage: Bizet is a counter-image. Nearly every quality praised in him is a negation of Wagner: ease instead of sweat, a completed form instead of “endless melody,” a listener treated as intelligent instead of worn down by repetition, southern dryness instead of northern damp, love as fate instead of love as redemption. In §3 the target shifts from sound to subject matter. Wagner’s opera is “the opera of salvation,” and Lohengrin’s ban on questioning stands for the Christian command to believe.

    Questions: Sort the qualities Nietzsche attributes to Carmen. Which could be heard in a score, which describe his own state while listening, and which describe a type of person? §1 ends by saying that Bizet makes him productive and that he has no other test of what is good. Can anyone besides Nietzsche use that test? §2 calls a love whose essence is mortal hatred between the sexes “the only one worthy of a philosopher,” and its example ends with a man who has killed a woman. What does the praise select, and what does it omit?

  3. Stage 3§§4–6

    Read: the history of the Ring (§4), the opening “The artist of decadence. That is the word.” (§5), and the parody in which Wagner’s success, dressed as a friendly musical scholar, advises young composers (§6). Stop at §7, which begins “Enough! Enough!”

    Why this stage: §4 closes the redemption theme with Wagner himself redeemed. In Nietzsche’s story, the revolutionary optimism of the Ring runs aground on Schopenhauer, and Wagner reinterprets the shipwreck as the goal. §5 announces that the jesting is over and makes the book’s first sustained medical claims. §6 then turns them into a mock method: effect over beauty, colour of sound over melody, passion because it is cheap, idealism because it elevates.

    Questions: §5 says that Wagner “increases exhaustion—therefore he attracts the weak and exhausted to him” (Ludovici). Which half of the sentence is an observation, and which a hypothesis? What would count against it? In §6, which of the recipes name features of Wagner’s music that an admirer would also recognize, and which assign a motive?

  4. Stage 4§§7–9, with Nietzsche’s note on the word “drama”

    Read: the style of decadence and Wagner as miniaturist (§7), Wagner as actor (§8), his plots, heroines, and “mythical substance” (§9), and Nietzsche’s footnote to §9 on the word drama. In the free volume it is note 10 among the endnotes. Stop at §10.

    Why this stage: This is the analytical centre of the book. §7 defines decadent style by the relation of part to whole and then extends that definition to the will, morality, and politics. It also contains the book’s highest praise of Wagner’s music. §8 moves Wagner out of the history of music into the history of the theatre. §9 tests the “eternal substance” of the operas by translating them into modern, bourgeois terms.

    Questions: Find the sentence in §7 where a description of literary style becomes a claim about “equal rights for all.” Is the link causal, an analogy, or a rhetorical flourish? §7 calls Wagner “a master of the first rank” in small forms. Does that praise support the diagnosis or put pressure on it?

  5. Stage 5§§10–12

    Read: Wagner’s writings and his inheritance from Hegel (§10), the historical rise of the actor in music and the coincidence of Wagner’s rise with the German Empire (§11), and the three demands (§12). Stop after the signature “Friedrich Nietzsche,” before the Postscript.

    Why this stage: The letter ends by turning from Wagner to his admirers and his historical moment. §11 contains the book’s most generous praise of what Wagner did for performers, conductors, and musical scholarship, and then explains it as a school of obedience. §12 reduces the whole complaint to three demands about the stage, the actor, and music.

    Questions: In §11, keep the praise and the explanation apart. Which is argued, and which is asserted? Do §12’s three demands depend on the diagnosis of decadence, or could someone who rejects the diagnosis still accept them?

  6. Stage 6Postscript, with Nietzsche’s note; Second Postscript

    Read: the Postscript, which Nietzsche presents as excerpts from an unprinted essay titled “What Wagner has cost us.” Read it together with his footnote beginning “Was Wagner a German at all?” (note 12 in the free volume; the critical text is longer). Then read the Second Postscript. Stop at the Epilogue.

    Why this stage: The claims widen. The Postscript turns from the musician to the movement: the Wagner societies, the belief in genius, the rule of the theatre over the arts, and the young men and women drawn into Bayreuth. The Second Postscript refuses to make any other musician the answer, dismisses Brahms, and declares that modern music as a whole expresses “physiological contradiction.”

    Questions: The Postscript praises the Germans’ early resistance to Wagner because it came “not with arguments—it is impossible to refute an illness” (Ludovici). What does that praise do to Nietzsche’s own arguments? The Second Postscript says that corruption is universal and that “not even a God can save music.” What is left of the Bizet ideal of §§1–3?

  7. Stage 7Epilogue, with Nietzsche’s note

    Read: the Epilogue to its final sentence, “this essay, as you observe, was inspired by gratitude” (Ludovici), and Nietzsche’s footnote on the Genealogy (note 14 in the free volume). Stop before the title Nietzsche Contra Wagner, which begins a different work.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche leaves Wagner in order to define the modern. Every age permits the virtues that fit its strength. Aesthetics depends on biology. Master morality and Christian values are opposed optics that argument cannot refute. The modern person is “biologically” a contradiction of values, and Wagner is the most instructive case for a vivisection of the modern soul.

    Questions: If “true” and “untrue” have no sense in optics, what kind of claim is the diagnosis of Wagner? The Epilogue says “all of us” are physiologically false. Does Nietzsche place himself inside that verdict, as the Preface did, or outside it?

After Stage 7, complete the writing exercise. It asks you to compare one of the book’s claims with an earlier or later text on the same subject.

A shorter first encounter

For an entrance before the full path, read the Preface, §1, §5, §7, and the second part of the Epilogue, from “But the kind of falsity which is characteristic of the Bayreuthians” to the end (printed page 51). In the free edition these come to fewer than twenty pages. They give the confession, the counter-image, the diagnosis, the theory of decadent style, and the final claim about modernity.

Then make one comparison. Set §5’s verdict, “Wagner’s art is diseased,” beside §7’s praise of Wagner as “our greatest musical miniaturist” (both Ludovici). Mark which sentences in §5 describe the music, which describe listeners, and which rest on the vocabulary of illness. Then ask whether the praise in §7 could be true if the diagnosis in §5 were. Write down your answer before reading this guide’s account of the steps. This short route is an entrance, not a substitute for the Postscripts and Epilogue, where the claims widen most.

Texts, editions, and variants

The preferred translation is Walter Kaufmann’s, in Basic Writings of Nietzsche. Kaufmann’s translation has also been published with The Birth of Tragedy in a separate volume. This guide has not been checked against a Kaufmann printing. Quotations on this page are either Anthony M. Ludovici’s wording, labeled as such, or this guide’s translation of the German critical text, also labeled. None is given as Kaufmann’s.

Choose an edition and find passages

The free text. Project Gutenberg’s combined volume reproduces the third edition of Ludovici’s translation (Edinburgh and London: T. N. Foulis, 1911). It contains several items, and only one is assigned:

  • Ludovici’s “Translator’s Preface” and “Preface to the Third Edition” are both his own essays, although the second is headed only “Preface.” The first argues that the two Wagner books were not dictated by personal animosity and calls Ecce Homo “sincerity itself.” Read them as early reception, not as Nietzsche’s text.
  • The Case of Wagner runs from the heading “The Case of Wagner: A Musician’s Problem” through the Epilogue. This is the assignment.
  • Nietzsche Contra Wagner follows. It is a different work, assembled from earlier passages at the end of 1888. See its own guide.
  • “Selected Aphorisms from Nietzsche’s Retrospect of his Years of Friendship with Wagner (Summer 1878)” closes the volume. These are Nietzsche’s unpublished 1878 notes, selected for this English volume. They belong to neither book.

Footnotes. The free volume prints every note at the end of the book and numbers them continuously. The notes that end “—Tr.” are Ludovici’s: 7 (on Senta), 8 (on Biterolf), 9, 11 (on Hegel’s prose), and 13. Note 10 (to §9, on the word drama), note 12 (to the Postscript, “Was Wagner a German at all?”), and note 14 (to the Epilogue, on the Genealogy) are Nietzsche’s. Read these three as part of the text. Two of Ludovici’s notes need care:

  • Note 9 answers §7’s promise of a chapter “Concerning the Physiology of Art” in Nietzsche’s “principal work” by sending the reader to The Will to Power. That book is a posthumous editorial compilation of notebook material, not the finished work Nietzsche announced, and its numbering is editorial. The closest published statements are Twilight, “Skirmishes” §§8–11 and 19–20.
  • Note 13 identifies the unnamed musician of the Second Postscript, the only one who can still compose an overture as an organic whole, as Peter Gast. Nietzsche’s text names no one. The identification is Ludovici’s.

Numbering. Only §§1–12 are numbered. The Preface, Postscript, Second Postscript, and Epilogue have no internal numbers. The Postscript is built on a refrain, “One pays dearly for having been a follower of Wagner” (Ludovici), which opens each paragraph after the first and can serve as a locator.

Links. The free text has an anchor for the start of the work and for each printed page, but none for individual sections. The links on this page open the printed page on which a section begins, or a nearby page. The critical text at Nietzsche Source has section-level anchors for the German.

Title words. The German subtitle is “Ein Musikanten-Problem.” Musikant is a plainer and slightly deprecating word for a musician. Ludovici renders it “A Musician’s Problem.” The closing pieces are the Nachschrift, Zweite Nachschrift, and Epilog: Postscript, Second Postscript, and Epilogue.

Text differences that affect the assignment

The modern critical text, available in German at Nietzsche Source, was compared with the free translation for the passages this guide discusses. This is not a full collation. Some of the items below are differences of text, and some are translation choices that affect an argument. Neither kind settles what a Kaufmann printing reads.

  • Where the letter begins. Ludovici prints “A Letter from Turin, May 1888” and the motto before the Preface. The critical text places the Preface first and then repeats the title with the heading and motto before §1. On that arrangement the Preface stands outside the letter, the letter is signed at the end of §12, and the Second Postscript speaks of “my letter” as something already sent.
  • Preface: “self-overcoming.” Asked what to call his escape from Wagnerism, Nietzsche answers “Vielleicht Selbstüberwindung”: perhaps self-overcoming. Ludovici has “Perhaps a piece of self-mastery,” and uses “self-mastery” again later in the Preface. The German uses the term this site follows as self-overcoming.
  • §1: “proof.” The section ends “ich habe auch keinen andern Beweis dafür, was gut ist”: I have no other proof of what is good. Ludovici has “touchstone for testing what is good.” The German word makes the evidential claim stronger.
  • §§1 and 5: “innocent.” In §1 Wagner’s orchestral sound is “brutal, künstlich und ‚unschuldig‘ zugleich”. In §5 the three stimulants of the exhausted are “das Brutale, das Künstliche und das Unschuldige (Idiotische)”. Ludovici translates the first “unsophisticated” and the second “innocence (idiocy),” which hides the fact that §5 repeats §1’s triad. The diagnosis of §5 names what §1 heard.
  • §3: the catalogue of redemptions. Ludovici’s list includes “young hysterics like to be saved by their doctor? (the case in ‘Lohengrin’).” The critical text has no such item. It still says “once again the case of Lohengrin” a few lines later, when the opera returns for going to bed at the wrong time. Neither version should be cited for the missing item without checking your printing.
  • §3: the reference to Beyond Good and Evil. After “Il faut méditerraniser la musique,” Ludovici cites “pp. 216 et seq.”; the German cites page 220 of Nietzsche’s edition. The passage meant is Beyond Good and Evil §255, which dreams of “a redemption of music from the North” (this guide’s translation).
  • §7: “likeness.” After describing literary decadence, the German says “Aber das ist das Gleichniss für jeden Stil der décadence”: that is the likeness, or image, for every decadent style. Ludovici has “the formula for every decadent style.” Nietzsche’s own word marks the extension to the will, morality, and politics as an analogy. See From technique to diagnosis.
  • §11: “Teutons.” The definition “an obedient man with long legs” is given of the Germanen, the Germanic peoples, not the Deutsche. Ludovici has “The definition of a German.”
  • Postscript: Parsifal. After “I admire this work. I would fain have composed it myself” (Ludovici), the German continues “in Ermangelung davon verstehe ich es”: lacking that, I understand it. Ludovici omits the clause.
  • Postscript, Nietzsche’s footnote. Ludovici’s version ends a sentence with “His father was an actor of the name of Geyer.…” The critical text continues: “Ein Geyer ist beinahe schon ein Adler…” (A Geyer is almost an Adler). The free text omits the sentence. See the passage note.
  • Second Postscript: “lineages.” Nietzsche allows that there may still be remnants “stärkerer Geschlechter” in Europe: of stronger lineages or stocks. Ludovici has “stronger natures,” which removes the reference to descent.

Words to compare. Ludovici’s “salvation,” “saviour,” and “saved” translate Erlösung, Erlöser, and erlöst. This guide uses redemption, redeemer, and redeemed, because the word links Wagner’s operas, Schopenhauer, and Christian theology. Nietzsche writes décadence and décadent in French. Decadence is his proposed pattern of life working against its own conditions, not a neutral medical category. “Wagnerite” and “female Wagnerite” translate Wagnerianer and Wagnerianerin.

Composition and publication. The letter is dated Turin, May 1888. Ludovici’s preface says that Nietzsche drafted it there in May, finished it in Sils Maria at the end of June, and published it that autumn. Nietzsche Contra Wagner, whose Preface is dated Christmas 1888, is a later and separate work.

Interpretive problems

Taste as testimony

The book’s first evidence is Nietzsche’s own body. Wagner’s orchestration brings on the sirocco and a “disagreeable sweat” (§1). Bizet makes him a better listener and a more productive philosopher, and he has no other proof of what is good. First-person reports are real evidence, but they are evidence about the one reporting. To become evidence about the music, they need other listeners, comparable conditions, and some way to rule out mood, expectation, and grudge. The book itself supplies reasons for caution. The Preface admits malice in the praise of Bizet. §1 describes a twentieth hearing of Carmen. The Second Postscript declines to glorify any other musician. The Gay Science §368 (1887) had already called Nietzsche’s objections to Wagner “physiological objections” and offered his breathing and his foot as the facts.

The insider’s authority

Nietzsche claims to know Wagnerism from inside: nobody was more dangerously involved in it, and he was “one of the most corrupted Wagnerites” (§3, Ludovici). That can be read two ways. The former believer knows what the music does and why it attracts. The former believer also has a stake in having been right to leave. The book does not let the reader choose easily. The Postscript still says of Parsifal, “I would fain have composed it myself,” and in the German adds that, lacking that, he understands it. Keep both readings available and ask which claims depend on the insider’s knowledge and which on the convert’s need.

From a figure of speech to a diagnosis

The vocabulary of illness does a great deal of work: neurosis, hysteria, exhaustion, degeneration, a hospital ward. The book appeals to “our medical men and physiologists” (§5, Ludovici) without naming one. §7 promises that a later chapter will show the theatricalization of art to be “a sign of physiological degeneration (or more precisely a form of hysteria).” That chapter was never published. The key sentence of §7 calls its own extension from style to politics a Gleichniss, a likeness. The diagnosis may still be illuminating. It is not yet established. The step-by-step account below marks where each extension happens.

Redemption is the target

The Wagner Nietzsche opposes is the artist of redemption, in the plots (§3), in the composer’s own history with Schopenhauer (§4), in the music’s claim to save (§6), and in the Christianity that Bayreuth flattered (Postscript, Epilogue). Two claims should be kept apart here. That redemption is a recurring theme of Wagner’s operas can be checked in the libretti. That the need for redemption is itself “the most straightforward expression of decadence” (Epilogue, Ludovici) is a valuation this book asserts. The Genealogy and The Antichrist argue for it at length.

An argument against argument

The Postscript praises the Germans for resisting Wagner by instinct, “not with arguments—it is impossible to refute an illness.” The Epilogue says that “one cannot refute Christianity: it is impossible to refute a diseased eyesight,” and that “true” and “untrue” have no sense in optics (both Ludovici). Yet much of the book argues: about form, drama, the actor, and the history of taste. If the diagnosis is only an optic, it cannot claim the authority of a finding. If it is a finding, it invites the evidence the Epilogue declares irrelevant. Use this tension to sort the book’s claims into those that ask to be tested and those that ask to be shared.

Contempt as a credential

The book’s disdain falls on women, the “mob,” German youths, the Reich, the educated, and anyone moved by what moves crowds. The Epilogue gives the disdain a theoretical role: noble taste has an instinctive “scorn and horror” of decadents, and “such things almost prove it” (Ludovici). A contempt that counts as its own evidence cannot easily be corrected. Separate the parts of the book that can be argued with, such as the critique of the theatre’s power over the other arts in the Postscript, from the parts that equate women, crowds, and decline without argument. The site’s women and gender and master and slave morality themes follow these claims across the corpus.

How the polemic turns

The book has the informality of a letter and something like the structure of a sermon, which it also parodies. It moves from praise of an alternative (§§1–2) to a mock catalogue (§3), to the story of Wagner’s own redemption (§4), then to an announced seriousness (§5), a mock sermon (§6), a theory of decadent style (§7), the actor (§§8–9), the writings (§10), historical meaning (§11), and three demands (§12). The postscripts and Epilogue then widen the case three times. The notes below follow those turns. For each, they say what happens, where to watch the claim change level, and what to read alongside.

Preface · A confession as credential

What happens. Nietzsche announces relief, admits that malice plays some part in his praise of Bizet, and describes his escape from Wagnerism as a long history he would call, if he were a moralist, self-overcoming. The philosopher must overcome his age in himself, and Nietzsche is as much a child of his age as Wagner. Decadence is his deepest problem. Morality conceals “impoverished life, the will to nonentity, great exhaustion” (Ludovici), so he had to take sides against everything sick in himself, “including Wagner, including Schopenhauer.” He ends by making Wagner indispensable: modernity speaks its most intimate language through him.

Watch the step. The Preface makes decadence a single problem that includes morality, and makes Wagner a summary of modernity. Both claims are stated before the book has said anything about music. What follows is presented as illustration of a diagnosis already reached.

Read alongside. The Ecce Homo review of this book, which says, “I have loved Wagner” (see that guide), and the self-overcoming theme.

§§1–3 · Bizet as a counter-image

What happens. Bizet’s music comes forward lightly and “does not sweat.” It builds, organizes, and completes, in contrast to “the polypus in music,” the “endless melody” (§1, Ludovici). It treats the listener as intelligent. Its climate is southern and dry, its gaiety “African,” its love fate and war rather than redemption (§2). With §3 the joke turns on Wagner’s plots. Someone always wants to be redeemed: Tannhäuser, the Flying Dutchman, Kundry, Eva, Isolde, even Wotan. Ludovici’s list has one more item, on Lohengrin, that the critical text lacks (see the variants). Lohengrin forbids questions, as faith does. Goethe too is “saved” by Wagner, in a way that sides with the “cultured woman.”

Watch the step. §1 moves from sound (orchestration) to the listener’s body (the sirocco and sweat) to a claim about “the three senses of the modern soul.” §3 moves from a list of plots to a claim about women (“adoring women are their ruin”) and about what the crowd and women can see of higher values. The catalogue is funny. It also carries the book’s first generalizations about women and faith.

Read alongside. Beyond Good and Evil §255, the passage cited after “Il faut méditerraniser la musique,” which already opposes the South to German music. The Gay Science §370 criterion of art from abundance and art from impoverishment is the standard these sections apply without naming.

§4 · The Ring and Schopenhauer

What happens. Nietzsche retells the Ring as Wagner’s own redemption. For half his life, he says, Wagner believed in revolution. Siegfried, born of adultery and incest, was to overthrow the “old contracts” of custom and morality and free Brünnhilde for a golden age of free love. Then the ship struck a reef: Schopenhauer’s philosophy. Ashamed of having set optimism to music, Wagner reinterpreted the wreck as the goal and “translated the ‘Ring’ into Schopenhauerian language.” Brünnhilde now had to put the fourth book of The World as Will and Idea into verse. “It was the philosopher of decadence who allowed the artist of decadence to find himself” (Ludovici).

Watch the step. The story has a basis in the history of the Ring’s revised endings, but it is compressed into a joke. Check a Wagner biography before relying on its details. The last sentence makes a larger claim. Schopenhauer’s pessimism is decadence, and Wagner’s art became itself by adopting it. That is the book’s thesis, stated as the moral of an anecdote.

Read alongside. “Schopenhauer as Educator” and the Schopenhauerian metaphysics of The Birth of Tragedy and its 1886 self-criticism, where Nietzsche assesses his own earlier debt to Schopenhauer.

Open §4 in the free edition (printed page 9)

§§5–6 · “Here I begin to be serious”

What happens. §5 names the case: Wagner is “the artist of decadence.” The claims come fast. He has made music sick. Europe does not resist him because its instincts are weakened. The harmful lures the exhausted, as the vegetable lures the vegetarian. Wagner increases exhaustion and therefore attracts the exhausted. His art is diseased; his stage problems are those of hysterics; his heroes and heroines, as physiological types, make “a hospital ward.” He supplies the three great stimulants of the exhausted, which are brutality, artifice, and innocence. His success is with nerves, “and therefore with women.” §6 then imagines Wagner’s success as a kindly scholar teaching young composers how to overwhelm, elevate, and make people wonder vaguely. The advice is to prefer the gigantic to the beautiful, colour of sound to melody, and passion to counterpoint, and to “improve mankind.”

Watch the step. §5 is where the book first converts attraction into evidence of condition. That people are drawn to Wagner is offered as a sign that they are exhausted, and the exhaustion is offered as the reason they are drawn. §6 is funnier and in some ways more precise. Its recipes name real features of the music, and the parody assigns them a motive.

Read alongside. Genealogy III §§2–5, where Wagner’s late homage to chastity opens a more careful argument about what ascetic ideals mean for artists.

§§7–9 · Style, the actor, and the drama

What happens. §7 defines the decline of the artist: the musician is becoming an actor. Its theory of decadent style follows. In literary decadence the word leaps out of the sentence, the sentence obscures the page, and the page lives at the expense of the whole. The same pattern, extended, is “anarchy among the atoms, disaggregation of the will,” morally “freedom of the individual,” and politically “equal rights for all” (Ludovici). Wagner begins from an hallucination of attitudes, vivifies small unities, and cannot develop a whole. Yet in the smallest things he is “a master of the first rank,” “our greatest melancholic in music.” §8 calls him “an incomparable histrio,” an innovator who increased music’s power of speech immeasurably but used music as a servant of the stage. His music is “never true,” in the actor’s sense that what must seem true must not be true. §9 says he plans from the effective scene, is “no dramatist,” and has heroines who, stripped of myth, are Madame Bovary.

Watch the step. The part-and-whole description in §7 is a real claim about musical form that can be tested against the scores. Nietzsche himself calls the extension to will, morality, and politics a likeness. The move from “the musician is now becoming an actor” to “his art is developing ever more and more into a talent for telling lies” turns a claim about technique into a moral one.

Read alongside. Twilight, “Skirmishes” §§19–20, where beauty and ugliness become physiological categories, and Beyond Good and Evil §256, which in 1886 counts Wagner among Europe’s great spirits and already places him, “as an artist in general,” among the actors (this guide’s translation).

§§10–12 · Writings, history, and three demands

What happens. §10 reduces Wagner’s prose writings to three propositions, beginning “Everything that Wagner cannot do is bad” (Ludovici). It argues that music which needs literature to mean “an infinity of things” is the music of a rhetorician, and makes Wagner “Hegel’s heir,” winning German youths with “the idea.” The section ends with what “we halcyonians” miss in him: light feet, wit, “the calm sea—perfection.” §11 gives Wagner’s historical meaning as the rise of the actor in music, where “the mob is allowed to decide.” It then praises his effect on conductors, singers, orchestras, and the music theorist Riemann: here his influence “has really been beneficent.” It explains the new discipline as obedience and links Wagner’s rise to the Empire’s. §12 states three demands. The stage should not become master of the arts. The actor should not become the corrupter of the genuine. Music should not become an art of lying.

Watch the step. §11 treats a coincidence of dates, Wagner’s rise and the Empire’s, as proof that “both phenomena are a proof of one and the same thing” (Ludovici). A shared cause would need to be shown. §12’s demands are more modest than the diagnosis that precedes them, and several could be accepted by a reader who rejects that diagnosis.

Read alongside. Twilight, “What the Germans Lack”, written in the same year, and “Schopenhauer as Educator” §6 on institutions and taste.

Postscript · “What Wagner has cost us”

What happens. After a short introduction come five paragraphs, each opening with the refrain that following Wagner costs dearly. The first praises the Germans’ long, instinctive resistance to him. The second recalls the wreath at Wagner’s funeral inscribed with the last words of Parsifal, “Redemption to the Redeemer,” and the joke that corrected it to “Redemption from the Redeemer” (this guide’s translation; Ludovici has “Salvation to the Saviour”). The third names three effects on culture: the arrogance of the lay enthusiast, the belief in genius in place of schooling, and “theatrocracy.” The fourth asks what Wagner does to the spirit and answers that he flatters every nihilistic and Christian instinct. It praises Parsifal as a masterpiece of seduction. The fifth describes the young men whose taste, ideas, and nerves are corrupted, and calls Wagner “fatal for women.” Nietzsche’s footnote asks whether Wagner was a German at all.

Watch the step. The case moves from a composer to an institution and a public. Some of the claims about institutions can be investigated: the societies, the journal, and the prestige of the stage. The claims about nerves and women are asserted through caricature. The praise of resistance by instinct, “not with arguments,” sits awkwardly in a book that argues.

Read alongside. The Wagner and decadence theme’s note on the Postscript, and Genealogy III §§2–5 on Parsifal and chastity.

Second Postscript · No other musician

What happens. Nietzsche complains that the Germans no longer understand him and denies that his attack on Wagner glorifies any other composer. Decay is universal. Wagner is not the cause of music’s ruin but accelerated it, and his superiority lay in believing in decadence and following it to its conclusions. Brahms is the musician of longing and “the melancholy of impotence,” of a type of “dissatisfied women.” Everything that claims the grand style is false either to us or to itself. No schooling can supply what is “no longer in our bodies,” whether “the strong race of a Handel or the overflowing animal spirits of a Rossini” (Ludovici). “Not even a God can save music.”

Watch the step. The case now concerns the whole of modern music and the bodies of modern people. Bizet, the book’s counter-image, has quietly disappeared. The claim that the “first condition” of great music is bodily and hereditary is the book’s broadest physiological claim, and it receives no support beyond the examples.

Read alongside. What “decadence” is doing, on the Wagner and decadence theme.

Epilogue · The modern as a contradiction

What happens. A philosopher wants to wash his hands after the case of Wagner, so Nietzsche gives his idea of the modern. An age either has the virtues of ascending life or needs the virtues of declining life. Aesthetics is bound to these biological presuppositions, and “beauty in itself” is a chimera. Master morality affirms and Christian morality denies. They are opposed optics, and neither can be refuted. What must be resisted is the falseness that will not feel them as opposites. Wagner’s falseness was to look toward master morality and the Icelandic saga while preaching the gospel of the lowly and the need for redemption. The “Christian gentleman” is the same falseness in general form. The modern person is “biologically” a contradiction of values: “regarded from a physiological standpoint, we are false” (Ludovici). Wagner is the most instructive case for a vivisection of the modern soul, and the essay was “inspired by gratitude.”

Watch the step. The case of one composer becomes a diagnosis of “all of us.” The Epilogue’s parenthesis says that the gospels present the same physiological types as the novels of Dostoevsky. It is a claim about the New Testament that the book does not argue. Nietzsche’s footnote sends the reader to the Genealogy for the argument about noble and Christian morality.

Read alongside. The Antichrist §§28–35, where §31 wishes that a Dostoevsky had lived near Jesus, and master and slave morality.

From technique to diagnosis

This is the move the book most needs its reader to examine. At each step below, a claim that could in principle be checked by listening, reading a score, or reading a libretto becomes a judgment about bodies, audiences, or cultures. The left side of each step is often defensible. The right side needs more. The notes say what more it would need.

  • §1 · From orchestration to the modern soul. Technique: Wagner’s orchestral sound is massive, calculated, and designed for impact. Further judgment: it is “brutal, artificial and ‘innocent’” (this guide’s translation; Ludovici has “unsophisticated” for the third word), and so it appeals to “all the three senses of the modern soul at once” (Ludovici). What it would need: evidence that these qualities have the same appeal for many listeners, and an account of the modern soul that does not depend on this music for its definition. The only witness is Nietzsche’s sweat.
  • §5 · From attraction to exhaustion. Observation: Wagner’s music is overwhelmingly successful. Further judgment: “Wagner increases exhaustion—therefore he attracts the weak and exhausted” (Ludovici), and his success with nerves is “therefore” success with women. What it would need: an independent measure of exhaustion, evidence about who the audience were before and after exposure, and a reason to prefer this explanation to others. The book itself names several: the money to be made (§5), the German taste for “the idea” (§10), the Empire (§11), and the Wagner societies (Postscript).
  • §6 · From instrumentation to the nervous system. Technique: colour of sound over melodic line, harmonic shock, and an avoidance of closed melody. Further judgment: “overthrowing” the listener “belongs … to physiology”; some instruments persuade the bowels, others the spinal marrow. What it would need: an account of how particular sounds act on particular bodies. The parody asserts the mechanism as a boast put in Wagner’s mouth.
  • §7 · From part and whole to equal rights. Technique: vivid small units and weak large-scale development, a claim about form that can be tested against the scores. Further judgment: the same pattern is “disaggregation of the will,” morally “freedom of the individual,” politically “equal rights for all,” and in the artist “a form of hysteria.” What it would need: a showing that musical form, a body’s drives, and a society’s institutions share a cause and not merely a shape. Nietzsche’s word for the relation, Gleichniss, concedes that the link is a likeness. The physiological proof is deferred to a chapter he did not publish.
  • §§8–9 · From theatrical technique to lying. Technique: music subordinated to gesture, scene, and effect; plots built backward from the strongest scene. Further judgment: the actor’s principle that what must seem true must not be true, so “Wagner’s music is never true”; the heroines, without children, are “five paces away from the hospital.” What it would need: an argument that theatrical expressiveness is falsehood rather than a different kind of truth, a question the early Wagner writings approached very differently. Childless heroines are facts about the plots. As evidence about modern bodies they prove nothing.
  • §11 · From a coincidence of dates to a common cause. Observation: Wagner’s rise coincided with the founding of the German Empire, and his performers became more disciplined. Further judgment: both prove “obedience and long legs.” What it would need: a causal account connecting the two, not a shared decade.
  • Postscript and Second Postscript · From a composer to a culture and its bodies. Observation: Wagner’s movement produced societies, a journal, a festival, and a new prestige for the theatre. Further judgment: it corrupts the taste, ideas, and nerves of the young; it is “fatal for women”; the first condition of great music is “no longer in our bodies.” What it would need: evidence about the lives and bodies of the people concerned, which the book replaces with caricature and the example of Handel’s “strong race.”
  • Epilogue · From a composer to “all of us.” Claim: the modern person holds values of opposite origin at once. Further judgment: this is a biological fact, and “regarded from a physiological standpoint, we are false.” What it would need: an account of how values are carried in bodies, one that could distinguish a contradiction of values from ordinary disagreement. The Epilogue also says that optics cannot be refuted, which removes the standard any such evidence would have to meet.

Nietzsche did sometimes imagine the evidence his diagnoses would need. In Twilight, written the same year, he says that the weakening effect of the ugly “can be measured with a dynamometer” (“Skirmishes” §20, this guide’s translation). The Case offers no such measurement. A year earlier, in Genealogy III §4, he advised separating an artist from his work, so as not to take the artist as seriously as the work. Insight into a work’s origins, he said, concerns “the physiologists and vivisectors of the spirit,” never “the aesthetic people, the artists” (this guide’s translation). The Case does not keep those roles apart. It judges works by diagnosing their maker, and it ends by calling for a vivisection. Ask what the book gains by merging the two roles, and what it loses.

Early praise, the polemic, and retrospect

Nietzsche wrote about Wagner for twenty years, and the texts do different jobs. Some praise him, some revise an earlier judgment without naming him, one attacks him, and two retell the story afterward. The Wagner and decadence theme surveys them all. The comparisons below are the ones that most help with this book. Keep the dates in view: a late account of an earlier view is evidence of how Nietzsche saw it later, not of what he thought then.

Early praise (1872–1876)

The Birth of Tragedy opens with a foreword to Wagner and hopes for a rebirth of tragedy from German music. “Richard Wagner in Bayreuth”, the fourth Untimely Meditation, praises Wagner as a renewer of culture on the eve of the first Bayreuth festival. Read §§5–6 of the Case against these texts. The early writings treat theatrical expressiveness, the union of the arts, and the German spirit as promise. The Case treats them as symptoms. Note which criteria have changed and which have only been reversed in value. In Ecce Homo, Nietzsche later says that the Bayreuth essay was really a portrait of himself (see that guide). That is a third view, and a later one.

Revisions before the polemic (1878–1887)

Human, All Too Human (1878) takes apart the cult of genius and the theory of the artist on which the early praise depended, largely without naming Wagner. Beyond Good and Evil §§255–256 (1886) opposes southern music to German. It counts Wagner among the great Europeans of the century, and in the same section places him, “as an artist in general,” among the actors, a fanatic of expression “at any price” and a discoverer of effects (this guide’s translation). It closes with verses aimed, Nietzsche says, against “the ‘last Wagner’ and his Parsifal music” (this guide’s translation). The Gay Science §§368 and 370 (Book V, 1887) state the physiological objection as a first-person fact and supply the criterion of art from abundance or from impoverishment, naming Schopenhauer and Wagner as romantics. Genealogy III §§2–5 (1887) asks what Wagner’s late homage to chastity means, separates the artist from the work, and treats artists as the “valets” of some morality, philosophy, or religion (this guide’s translation). Much of the Case’s vocabulary is already present in these texts. What the Case adds is the name, the heat, and the widening.

Retrospect after the polemic (late 1888)

Nietzsche Contra Wagner, dated Christmas 1888, is not a sequel of new chapters. It reassembles earlier passages. Its chapter “Wherein I Raise Objections” reworks The Gay Science §368 and adds a sentence absent from the 1887 text: “Aesthetics is nothing but applied physiology” (this guide’s translation). Compare it with §§1 and 5 of the Case to see the physiological objection stated three times in two years. Ecce Homo, written in 1888 and published in 1908, calls this book “comparatively mild,” says that Nietzsche loved Wagner, and turns its review into an indictment of the Germans. In “Why I Am So Clever” §§5–6 he says he would not give up the days at Tribschen at any price, and calls Wagner the antidote to everything German. Neither retrospect is a neutral record of 1888, still less of 1876.

Where does each text move from a claim about music to a claim about bodies, audiences, or culture? What evidence would that move need, and does either text supply it? Does the later text add an argument, or only intensity and scope? End with one sentence stating what the earlier text must still be read for on its own terms.

Where it leads

Across to the late books. Twilight of the Idols, written the same year, develops the physiology of art that §7 promises, and the standard of ascending and declining life that the Epilogue assumes. The Antichrist argues at length the Epilogue’s claim that Christianity is decadence.

Forward to the retellings. Nietzsche Contra Wagner rearranges earlier passages into a last statement of opposition, and Ecce Homo reviews this book and retells the friendship. Compare each with the Preface’s confession and ask what each version needs the story to have been.

Back to the beginning. The fourth Untimely Meditation and The Birth of Tragedy are the texts the Case most needs to be read against. They make the strongest case for what the Case rejects.
