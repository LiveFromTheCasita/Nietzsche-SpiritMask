Overview and context

Nietzsche Contra Wagner is a book made almost entirely of Nietzsche’s earlier writing. In December 1888 he took passages from books published between 1879 and 1887, gave them new headings, cut and rewrote them, and arranged them as a case against Wagner. He added a short Preface, a page on music from the still-unpublished Ecce Homo, and a closing poem. The subtitle, Aktenstücke eines Psychologen, means the documents in a psychologist’s case file. Nietzsche presents the book as evidence.

His letters say what the evidence was for. On 11 December 1888 he wrote to the Swiss writer Carl Spitteler that critics treated his turn against Wagner as a change of mind contemporary with The Case of Wagner. In fact, he said, he had been waging war for ten years, and he had stated no general proposition in that book that he had not already put forward in his writings (letter to Spitteler, this guide’s translation). The collection was to prove the point.

That is why this guide reads each chapter twice. Nietzsche’s Preface says the chapters were chosen “not without care,” here and there “made a little more intelligible, but above all, more brief” (Ludovici). Comparison with the earlier texts shows more than that. Some chapters were shortened. Others were lengthened. Passages that did not name Wagner now name him. A passage that once called his art “wonderful and high” loses the phrase (this guide’s translation). A passage that once ended with a Wagnerite’s retort loses the retort. A reflection on Parsifal gains a final verdict: “it is a bad work.” The collection is evidence of a long quarrel, and it is also a revision of that quarrel’s record. The provenance map and the chapter notes set the two side by side.

Keep three dates apart. The earlier texts belong to 1879–1887 and have their own arguments. The Case of Wagner, written and published in 1888, is the polemic this book was meant to support. This selection belongs to the last weeks before Nietzsche’s collapse in Turin at the beginning of January 1889, the same weeks in which he was preparing Ecce Homo. It is a third account, not a neutral archive of the first two.

The book states its hierarchies plainly, and this guide does too. It says that in the theatre one becomes “mob, herd, woman, Pharisee, electing cattle, patron, idiot—Wagnerite” (Ludovici). It calls music a woman, says that the Germans have no future, and ranks human beings by how deeply they can suffer. The Intermezzo says that the great “German” musicians were really foreigners, “Slavs, Croats, Italians, Netherlanders—or Jews” (this guide’s translation). One chapter says that Wagner stooped “even to anti-Semitism,” and dates the break in a way the historical record complicates. The passage notes cite each claim.

Carry one question through the book: Does this passage say what its new heading says it says?

Guided reading path

This path covers the whole work as the modern critical text prints it, in order: the Preface; nine headed chapters and their numbered subsections; the Intermezzo; the two-part Epilogue; and the closing poem. The eight stages are this guide’s teaching divisions. Each stage names the chapter’s earlier source so that you can read the two together; the provenance map gives dates and boundaries.

The free English text by Ludovici omits the Intermezzo and the poem, and it ends at Epilogue §2. For those two items, read the German at Nietzsche Source or use a printing that includes them. Check a Kaufmann printing before assuming it does; see the edition notes.

  1. Stage 1Title, subtitle, and Preface (Turin, Christmas 1888)

    Read: the title page and the Preface, which is signed and dated. Stop at “Where I Admire.” In the free volume, begin at the heading Nietzsche contra Wagner, after the end of The Case of Wagner.

    Where it comes from: written for this book, in December 1888.

    Why this stage: The Preface makes three claims to test. The chapters were chosen from older writings, “some of them date back as far as 1877,” and were clarified and shortened. Read in order, they show that Nietzsche and Wagner are “antipodes.” And the book is “an essay for psychologists and not for Germans” (all Ludovici). It then lists readers abroad and closes with jokes against Germany’s alliance with Italy.

    Questions: What would count as proof that two people are “antipodes”: opposed values, opposed temperaments, or a history of disagreement? As you read, keep a list of the places where the Preface’s description of the collection, as older writing only clarified and shortened, fails to fit.

  2. Stage 2“Where I Admire”; “Where I Raise Objections”

    Read: the two opening chapters. Ludovici titles them “Wherein I Admire Wagner” and “Wherein I Raise Objections.” Stop at the Intermezzo in the critical text, or at “Wagner as a Danger” in the free text.

    Where they come from: The Gay Science §87 (1882) and §368 (Book V, 1887), both revised. Neither earlier title survives.

    Why this stage: The book opens by praising Wagner as the master of the very small and of hidden suffering, then states that its objections are physiological: the music makes breathing hard and the foot rebel. Praise and objection are placed side by side as the book’s basic pair.

    Questions: In The Gay Science §87 the musician is not named. What does naming him change? “Where I Raise Objections” says that “Æsthetic is indeed nothing more than applied physiology” (Ludovici). The sentence is new in 1888. Does the chapter give a physiological argument, or a first-person report restated as a principle?

  3. Stage 3Intermezzo (critical text; absent from Ludovici)

    Read: the Intermezzo, a page on music ending with a short poem about a Venice night. It is not in the free Case of Wagner volume. For a free English text, read Ludovici’s translation of Ecce Homo, “Why I Am So Clever” §7, which translates the same German text. It begins partway down printed page 45, at “To the most exceptional of my readers,” and ends with the poem’s last line, “Hearkened any thereto?” Its bracketed note on “this side of the Alps” is Ludovici’s. The German is at Nietzsche Source. Stop at “Wagner as a Danger.”

    Where it comes from: Ecce Homo, “Why I Am So Clever” §7, written in the same weeks and then unpublished. The critical texts of the two works print the same words.

    Why this stage: This is the book’s most concrete picture of the music Nietzsche wants, beside the Epilogue’s call for an art for artists: music should be “cheerful and deep, like an afternoon in October” (this guide’s translation); the South, Venice, Chopin, Rossini, and Peter Gast are set against German music. It also contains the book’s bluntest national and racial claims. Nietzsche moved this page back and forth between the two books in December 1888; see composition and printing.

    Questions: What kind of standard is an afternoon in October? Does the Intermezzo’s claim about who the real German musicians were rest on any evidence, or on the conclusion it is meant to support?

  4. Stage 4“Wagner as a Danger” §§1–2; “A Music without a Future”

    Read: both numbered sections of “Wagner as a Danger,” then “A Music without a Future.” Stop at “We Antipodes.”

    Where they come from: Assorted Opinions and Maxims §134 and §171 (1879), and The Wanderer and His Shadow §165 (1880). These are the oldest published sources in the book.

    Why this stage: Here Nietzsche can show that he criticized Wagner in print within a few years of the break. §134 already names Wagner and warns that “endless melody” endangers rhythm. §171 already predicts that Wagner’s art will not last. But the 1879 texts also contained qualifications, technical detail, and praise that the 1888 versions remove.

    Questions: Compare §1 with Assorted Opinions §134. Which sentences make the earlier warning into a verdict? “A Music without a Future” adds “music is a woman” (this guide’s translation) and “The Germans themselves have no future” (Ludovici). Are these conclusions of the chapter’s argument about music and culture, or additions to it?

  5. Stage 5“We Antipodes”; “Where Wagner Belongs”

    Read: “We Antipodes,” then “Where Wagner Belongs” (Ludovici: “Where Wagner Is At Home”). Stop at “Wagner as the Apostle of Chastity.”

    Where they come from: The Gay Science §370 (1887), and Beyond Good and Evil §§254 and 256 (1886), all heavily cut and rewritten.

    Why this stage: “We Antipodes” gives the book its title word and its criterion. There are two kinds of sufferers, from abundance and from impoverishment of life, and Wagner and Schopenhauer serve the second. “Where Wagner Belongs” places Wagner in Paris, among the late French romantics, and ends on a new judgment: “But they were ill” (Ludovici).

    Questions: In 1887 Nietzsche said that he had once misunderstood Schopenhauer and Wagner, “not to their disadvantage” (this guide’s translation). In 1888 the same paragraph says that they deny and slander life. What happened to the confession? Beyond Good and Evil §256 called the late romantics a daring kind of “higher men.” Why might the 1888 version call them only “artists,” and then “ill”?

  6. Stage 6“Wagner as the Apostle of Chastity” §§1–3; “How I Got Free of Wagner” §§1–2

    Read: the three sections on Parsifal and chastity, then the two sections of the break (Ludovici: “How I Got Rid of Wagner”). Stop at “The Psychologist Speaks.”

    Where they come from: the closing poem of Beyond Good and Evil §256 (1886); On the Genealogy of Morals III §§2–3 (1887); and §§3–4 of the Preface added in 1886 to the second volume of Human, All Too Human.

    Why this stage: The book reaches its sharpest judgment and its personal story together. Parsifal is first mocked as Roman Catholic sentiment, then treated as a question, and at last condemned in words not found in the Genealogy. The story of the break then dates it to the first Bayreuth festival of 1876 and presents Wagner’s turn to the Christian cross as its confirmation.

    Questions: In the Genealogy, the question whether Parsifal was meant seriously opens an inquiry into what ascetic ideals mean for artists. What does the added verdict do to the inquiry? The opening of “How I Got Free of Wagner” §1 is new in 1888. Which details in it would a biography need to confirm?

  7. Stage 7“The Psychologist Speaks” §§1–3

    Read: all three sections. Stop at the Epilogue.

    Where they come from: Beyond Good and Evil §269, divided into two, and §270 (1886).

    Why this stage: The book moves from Wagner to the psychologist who sees through great men and artists, and whose danger is to suffocate with pity. “Great men” become fictions invented afterward by their work; deep suffering “makes noble” and “almost determines the order of rank” (Ludovici). These chapters never name Wagner. Their placement makes him the implicit case.

    Questions: Is Wagner the object of the psychologist’s diagnosis here, or is Nietzsche describing himself? If the depth of suffering almost determines rank, and “Where I Admire” says that Wagner “has suffered much,” where does that leave Wagner?

  8. Stage 8Epilogue §§1–2; then the closing poem, “On the Poverty of the Richest Man”

    Read: both sections of the Epilogue, then the poem in the critical text or a printing that includes it. In the free volume, stop at the end of Epilogue §2, before “Selected Aphorisms,” which are not part of this book.

    Where they come from: the Epilogue reworks §§3–4 of the Preface added to The Gay Science in 1887, with a new opening on amor fati. The poem is the last of the Dionysus-Dithyrambs, which Nietzsche was preparing in the same weeks.

    Why this stage: The case against Wagner ends with Nietzsche’s account of what illness taught him: great pain as the last liberator of the spirit, a “second taste,” an art “for artists, only for artists,” and the Greeks who were “superficial—from profundity” (Ludovici). The poem ends the book in Zarathustra’s voice, with a richness that cannot be given away.

    Questions: The Epilogue never mentions Wagner. What makes it an ending to this book? The poem’s last word belongs to Zarathustra’s own truth, which tells him he must become poorer to be loved. Does that answer or undercut the book’s claim of superiority?

After Stage 8, complete the writing exercise. It asks you to compare one chapter with the passage it came from.

A shorter first encounter

For an entrance before the full path, read the Preface, “Where I Admire”, “Where I Raise Objections”, “How I Got Free of Wagner” §1, and Epilogue §1. In the free edition these come to about ten pages. They give the claim, the praise, the objection, the story of the break, and the lesson Nietzsche draws from suffering.

Then make one comparison. Read The Gay Science §87, “On the Vanity of Artists,” in your edition or in the German. It describes a musician without naming him and ends by saying that he does not know his own gift because he is too vain to know it. Mark what “Where I Admire” adds and what it removes. Then decide whether the new version is still a portrait of vanity. Write down your answer before reading this guide’s note on the chapter.

What the map shows. The Preface says that some chapters “date back as far as 1877.” The earliest published sources are the two sequels to Human, All Too Human, from 1879 and 1880. This guide has not established whether any of those passages was first drafted in 1877. Most of the sources come from 1886 and 1887: the new prefaces, Beyond Good and Evil, Book V of The Gay Science, and the Genealogy. The Preface says that the chapters were “above all” shortened. Several were, often drastically. “Where I Raise Objections” and “Where I Admire” grew longer, and at least four chapters gained new judgments or new names. The Preface describes the collection as old writing. Two items, the Intermezzo and the poem, were written in the same season as the Preface.

The first plan. Nietzsche’s letter to Spitteler of 11 December 1888 lists eight pieces, with page references to his own editions, and asks Spitteler to publish them with a preface of his own. The titles point to “We Antipodes,” “A Music without a Future,” “Wagner as a Danger” §2, “Where I Raise Objections,” “Where Wagner Belongs,” the chastity chapter, and the break. The list also names a piece called “Barocco,” from pages 62–64 of his edition of the second volume of Human, All Too Human; it cannot be matched with certainty to a chapter of the finished book. It does not include “Where I Admire,” “The Psychologist Speaks,” the Epilogue, the Intermezzo, or the poem. Within days Nietzsche sent his own arrangement to his publisher; the Preface he wrote for it is dated Christmas. Several of the book’s most personal parts, including its praise, its psychology, and its account of illness, are not in the first plan. (The identifications of the planned pieces are this guide’s, from their titles and page references.)

Texts, editions, and variants

The preferred translation is Walter Kaufmann’s, in The Portable Nietzsche. This guide has not been able to inspect a Kaufmann printing. It does not know how Kaufmann titles the chapters, or whether a given printing includes the Intermezzo and the closing poem. Check your copy against the map above before relying on it. Quotations on this page are either Anthony M. Ludovici’s wording, labeled as such, or this guide’s translation of the German critical text, also labeled. None is given as Kaufmann’s.

Choose an edition and find passages

The free text. Project Gutenberg’s combined volume reproduces the third edition of Ludovici’s translation (Edinburgh and London: T. N. Foulis, 1911). Only one part of it is assigned here:

  • Ludovici’s “Translator’s Preface” and “Preface to the Third Edition” are his own essays. The first dates this book to about the middle of December 1888 and says that it was printed and corrected before the New Year but published only long afterward. Treat these essays as early reception, not as Nietzsche’s text.
  • The Case of Wagner comes first. It is a different work, with its own guide.
  • Nietzsche Contra Wagner runs from its title, with the subtitle “The Brief of a Psychologist,” through Epilogue §2. This is the assignment, except that the free text lacks the Intermezzo and the closing poem.
  • “Selected Aphorisms from Nietzsche’s Retrospect of his Years of Friendship with Wagner (Summer 1878)” closes the volume. These are unpublished notes of 1878, selected for the English edition. They are not part of this book.

Where the free text is incomplete. Read the Intermezzo and the closing poem in the German critical text, or in a printing that includes them. The Intermezzo’s text also appears in Ecce Homo, “Why I Am So Clever” §7, so an English Ecce Homo gives you the same page. See the Ecce Homo guide. For the poem, look for “On the Poverty of the Richest Man” in an English edition of the Dionysus-Dithyrambs.

Differences between the free text and the critical text

These were found by comparing Ludovici with the critical text for this book. This is not a full collation, and it does not settle what a Kaufmann printing reads.

  • The Intermezzo and the closing poem are absent from Ludovici. His text passes from “Where I Raise Objections” directly to “Wagner as a Danger,” and ends with Epilogue §2.
  • Epilogue §2. After the “second taste,” the critical text adds: “Moral: man ist nicht ungestraft der tiefste Geist aller Jahrtausende, — man ist es auch nicht unbelohnt… Ich gebe sofort eine Probe.” In this guide’s translation: “Moral: one is not the deepest spirit of all the millennia with impunity—nor without reward… I shall give a sample at once.” Ludovici lacks the sentences. They are also absent from the 1887 Gay Science Preface this section reworks.
  • “Where I Raise Objections”: “the loneliest music.” Nietzsche sets against Wagner the actor “the Wagner who made the loneliest music there is” (“die einsamste Musik”, this guide’s translation). Ludovici has “the most unique music that has ever existed,” which loses the link to solitude that the chapter’s next sentences develop.
  • Preface. The German says that an intelligent people can make only a misalliance with the “Reich.” Ludovici drops “intelligent.”
  • Titles. Ludovici adds “Wagner” to the first chapter heading. His “Brief” for Aktenstücke suggests a lawyer’s argument; the German suggests the documents in a file.
  • The chastity poem. Ludovici’s verse is a free rendering. Its line “Should Teutons, sin repenting, lash themselves” stands for a German line that asks whether this self-laceration is German. Use the German, or a literal translation, before building an argument on its images.

Composition, printing, and publication

Nietzsche’s letters, printed in the critical edition, record the book’s short history. Dates are as the edition gives them.

  • 11 December 1888. Nietzsche proposes to Carl Spitteler a collection of eight pieces from his writings, titled Nietzsche contra Wagner. Aktenstücke aus Nietzsche’s Werken, which Spitteler would publish with a preface.
  • About 15 December. Nietzsche sends the manuscript to his publisher, C. G. Naumann, to be printed before Ecce Homo. On 16 December he tells Heinrich Köselitz (Peter Gast) the new title, calls the book “extremely anti-German,” and says that a song of Zarathustra, “On the Poverty of the Richest,” stands at the end (this guide’s translation).
  • 20 December. A postcard to Naumann says that a sheet headed “Intermezzo” had been sent for this book. Nietzsche now wants it placed in Ecce Homo, “as it was originally intended,” without the title (this guide’s translation).
  • 22 December. To Köselitz: “We do not want to print the work N. contra W.,” because Ecce Homo contains everything decisive about the relationship. He adds that he may also move the poem into Ecce Homo (this guide’s translation).
  • 27 December. To Naumann: two proof sheets of the book have been returned ready for printing, and Nietzsche plans to publish it in 1889, perhaps before Twilight of the Idols. The same day he writes to Carl Fuchs that a page “Music” about music in the book is perhaps the most remarkable thing he has written.
  • 2 January 1889. To Naumann: “Events have completely overtaken the little work Nietzsche contra W.” He asks for the return of “the poem that makes the ending” (this guide’s translation). Within days he collapsed in Turin.

The critical text prints the book with the Intermezzo and the poem, and its title page reads Leipzig, 1889. The letters show Nietzsche changing his mind about both items and about whether to publish the book at all. This guide does not establish how far the 1889 printing went or when the book first reached the public. Do not treat the 1889 date as a publication in the ordinary sense without checking an edition’s apparatus.

Interpretive problems

A dossier offered as evidence

The book claims to show a consistent opposition over a decade. Its best evidence is real. Assorted Opinions §134 named Wagner and warned against his rhythm in 1879. The Gay Science §368 stated physiological objections in 1887. The Genealogy made Parsifal a problem the same year. Nietzsche did criticize Wagner in print before 1888. The book is weaker where it depends on its own revisions: where a passage that did not name Wagner now names him, or where a verdict is added that the source did not reach. Treat each chapter as two documents, the source and the revision, and ask which one supports the book’s claim.

Clarified, or changed?

The Preface calls the revisions clarifications and above all abbreviations. Some are. Others change what a passage claims. In 1887 Nietzsche wrote that he had misunderstood Wagner and Schopenhauer, “not to their disadvantage.” In 1888 the same paragraph calls them his antipodes because they deny life. In 1879 he called Wagner’s art “that wonderful and high art”; the 1888 version omits the phrase. In 1887 a Wagnerite answered the physiological objection by asking whether Nietzsche was simply not healthy enough for the music; the 1888 version drops the reply (all this guide’s translations). Decide for each change whether it states the earlier thought more clearly or replaces it.

Praise that survives

The book does not deny Wagner’s gifts. “Where I Admire” calls him “the Orpheus of all secret misery” and “the master of everything very small” (Ludovici). “Where I Raise Objections” credits him with the loneliest music there is. “Where Wagner Belongs” keeps Beyond Good and Evil’s description of the late romantics as daring, magnificently violent, high-flying artists before adding “But they were ill” (Ludovici). The praise is placed where the diagnosis can use it. Ask whether it could stand on its own, and what the book would need to show to turn a gift into a symptom.

The body as judge

“Where I Raise Objections” turns a report about breathing, the foot, the stomach, and the voice into a principle: aesthetics is applied physiology, and there is no soul. The claim that bodily response matters to judgment is serious. The chapter offers one body as its evidence, and the revision removes the objection that this body might be the problem. Compare the Case of Wagner guide’s account of the same move, where the steps from technique to diagnosis are set out.

Suffering and rank

The book uses suffering in two ways. “We Antipodes” sorts sufferers into those who suffer from abundance of life and those who suffer from its impoverishment, and places Wagner among the second. “The Psychologist Speaks” §3 says that how deeply one can suffer “almost determines the order of rank,” and “Where I Admire” calls deep suffering Wagner’s distinction above other musicians. The Epilogue credits great pain with Nietzsche’s own philosophy. Ask whether these can all be true together. If deep suffering ennobles, what criterion separates Wagner’s suffering from Nietzsche’s, other than the conclusion the book wants?

An anti-German book with national and racial claims

Nietzsche told Köselitz that the book was “extremely anti-German” (this guide’s translation). Its targets include the Reich, the young emperor, the North German press, and German taste. The book does not reject national and racial categories; it uses them. The Intermezzo reassigns the great “German” musicians to other peoples, or to Jews, or to “Germans of the strong race” now extinct (this guide’s translation). “A Music without a Future” calls Handel’s music the “Judeo-heroic trait” of the Reformation become music (Ludovici). “How I Got Free of Wagner” counts antisemitism among the things Nietzsche despises. Read the claims together before characterizing the book, and keep the historical record on Wagner’s antisemitism separate from Nietzsche’s account of it (see the note on that chapter).

Woman as a figure of judgment

Figures of woman carry much of the book’s evaluation. The theatre makes one “herd, woman, Pharisee.” Music “is a woman.” Woman’s pity overestimates its power, and her belief that love can do everything is “the superstition peculiar to her” (Ludovici). The Intermezzo wants music to be a sweet little woman of charm and malice. In the Epilogue truth is a woman who has reasons not to show her reasons. The revisions also remove two older phrases about the “all too feminine” nature of music and “the feminine” in romanticism (this guide’s translations). Ask what the figure does in each case: insult, praise, epistemology, or all three.

Preface · A claim about the evidence

Then. New in December 1888.

What it claims. The chapters were selected “not without care,” some go back to 1877, and they have been clarified and shortened. Read in order they leave no doubt that Nietzsche and Wagner are antipodes. The book is for psychologists and not for Germans. Nietzsche lists readers in Vienna, St Petersburg, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Paris, and New York, and closes by warning Italy: “Quousque tandem, Crispi” (echoing Cicero’s “How long, Catiline”), and an intelligent people makes only a misalliance with the Reich. Francesco Crispi was then Italy’s prime minister, and Italy was allied with Germany and Austria-Hungary in the Triple Alliance.

What it does. It tells the reader how to take everything that follows: as documents. The provenance map is this guide’s test of the claim.

Free text · German critical text

Where I Admire

Then. The Gay Science §87, “On the Vanity of Artists” (1882). Artists often do not know what they do best, because they are too vain. A certain musician is the master of suffering, oppressed, tormented souls and gives speech “even to mute animals.” He knows the colours of late autumn and the soul’s secret midnights. His real masterpieces are tiny, often a single bar long. The section ends: he does not know it, and he is too vain to know it. Wagner is not named.

What changed. The musician becomes Wagner. “Mute animals” becomes “mute misery.” New phrases add “the cynical revolts, for instance, of which only the greatest sufferer is capable” and the soul’s “amphibious nature” (Ludovici). The ending is replaced: “Wagner is one who has suffered much—and this elevates him above other musicians.—I admire Wagner wherever he sets himself to music” (Ludovici).

What the placement does. The portrait of a vain artist becomes the book’s one chapter of praise, and its lesson, that the artist’s love and insight are not of the same rank, prepares the objections that follow. The last line is carefully limited: Nietzsche admires Wagner where Wagner puts himself into music. Ask whether that is praise of the music, of the man, or of the sufferer.

Where I Raise Objections

Then. The Gay Science §368, “The Cynic Speaks” (Book V, 1887). Nietzsche’s objections are physiological: he cannot breathe easily, his foot wants dance and march, his stomach and heart protest. Wagner is essentially an actor; his practice made the attitude the end and music the means. The section ends with a parenthesis: a Wagnerite had answered the objections by asking whether Nietzsche was simply not healthy enough for “our music.”

What changed. A new first sentence denies that the music is healthy. New sentences state that “Æsthetic is indeed nothing more than applied physiology,” that “there is no such thing as a soul,” and that “Wagner makes one ill” (Ludovici). Wagner’s Kaisermarsch of 1871 enters with a joke that not even the young German emperor could march to it; Wilhelm II had come to the throne in June 1888. Nietzsche needs throat lozenges (Géraudel’s pastilles) to listen. A passage on the theatre as mass art is added, together with the admission of “the Wagner who made the loneliest music there is” (this guide’s translation; see the variants). “The theatre” becomes “Bayreuth.” The list of what one becomes in the theatre loses “public, democrat, neighbour, fellow man” (this guide’s translation) and gains “patron, idiot—Wagnerite” (Ludovici). The Wagnerite’s reply is cut.

What the placement does. The chapter now speaks as a physiological principle rather than a cynic’s complaint, and it no longer shows the most obvious objection to itself. The attack on the theatre also becomes an attack on a particular institution. The passage is a close cousin of The Case of Wagner §§1 and 5; see that guide.

Intermezzo

Then. Not an earlier publication. The same page stands in Ecce Homo as “Why I Am So Clever” §7, where it belongs to Nietzsche’s account of what nourishes him: climate, food, books, music. Ecce Homo was written in 1888 and published only in 1908.

What it says. Music should be cheerful and deep, “like an afternoon in October,” and a sweet little woman full of charm and malice. No German can know what music is. The great “German” musicians were foreigners or Jews, or else “Germans of the strong race,” extinct, like Schütz, Bach, and Handel. Nietzsche is “still Pole enough” to give the rest of music for Chopin, excepting Wagner’s Siegfried Idyll, perhaps Liszt, and everything beyond the Alps, which means Venice, including the music of his “maestro Pietro Gasti,” Peter Gast. A short poem remembers a night by a Venetian bridge (all this guide’s translations).

What the placement does. Between the objections and the dangers, the Intermezzo supplies the book’s most concrete picture of the music Nietzsche wants. The Epilogue later adds a more general call for an art for artists. Its national claims are asserted, not argued, and its racial language (“the strong race,” Jews as non-Germans) is the vocabulary of the time put to Nietzsche’s anti-German purpose. Read it with the Ecce Homo guide, and note that Nietzsche could not decide which book it belonged to.

Wagner as a Danger §§1–2

Then. §1 reworks Assorted Opinions and Maxims §134, “How the Soul Should Move according to Modern Music” (1879). Endless melody makes the listener swim instead of dance; this is perhaps the most essential of Wagner’s innovations. The section describes his technique: three-bar rhythms against two, five- and seven-bar periods, a repeated phrase stretched to double or triple length. It warns that careless imitation could degrade rhythm, especially when music follows a naturalistic acting that has no measure and cannot give measure to “the all too feminine nature of music.” §2 reworks The Wanderer and His Shadow §165, “On the Principle of Performance in Music” (1880). Performers who give every piece the greatest possible relief sin against Mozart. The question is on whom such effects work: “never on the people,” the immature, the sentimental, the sickly, the blunted. Wagner is not named.

What changed. In §1 the technical description is cut, and so is the phrase about music’s feminine nature. New sentences say that Wagner “overthrew the physiological first principle of all music” and that expressiveness at all costs, with music as the slave of attitude, “is the end” (Ludovici). In §2 Mozart becomes a composer “who fortunately was no German,” his seriousness is set against a German philistine’s, and the list of those worked upon ends: “upon idiots! upon Wagnerites!” (Ludovici).

What the placement does. §1 is Nietzsche’s strongest evidence of early published criticism, and the revision makes it more certain than it was. The 1879 section called the danger one that could arise from imitation. The 1888 version states it as fact. §2 recruits a general complaint about performance into the case against Wagner. The source never mentioned him.

A Music without a Future

Then. Assorted Opinions and Maxims §171, “Music as the Late-Comer of Every Culture” (1879). Music is the last art of a culture, sounding in its autumn. Handel gave voice to the “great Jewish-heroic trait” that created the Reformation. Music is not a universal language. Wagner’s art gathers the Catholicism of feeling and national longing of the Restoration, and leads a last campaign against the Enlightenment. Nietzsche calls it “that wonderful and high art,” which may one day become suddenly unintelligible, and ends by saying that thoughts outlast every other product of human art.

What changed. About half the section is cut, including the universal-language argument, the Enlightenment campaign, the phrase “wonderful and high art,” and the closing claim about thought. New phrases add that Handel’s music was “the Old Testament, not the New, become music,” that Wagner’s sagas are “Scandinavian monsters” whose Germanness scholars once believed in and “now we laugh at,” that “music is a woman,” and that “The Germans themselves have no future” (Ludovici, except “music is a woman,” this guide’s translation).

What the placement does. An essay on the historical lateness of music becomes a prophecy about Wagner and the Germans. The prediction of 1879 was hedged; the 1888 version adds a national verdict the argument does not reach.

We Antipodes

Then. The Gay Science §370, “What Is Romanticism?” (Book V, 1887). Nietzsche once took nineteenth-century pessimism and German music for signs of strength, and so misunderstood their romanticism. There are two kinds of sufferers, from overfullness of life and from its impoverishment, and Schopenhauer and Wagner answer the second. He misunderstood them then, “not to their disadvantage.” The section goes on to a second distinction, between the will to eternalize from gratitude and the will to eternalize from a tortured sufferer’s tyranny, names “romantic pessimism” in Schopenhauer and Wagner, and closes by announcing a “Dionysian pessimism” of the future.

What changed. “German music” becomes “the music of Wagner” (this guide’s translation). The confession of error now ends: “you see also, what I bestowed upon Wagner and Schopenhauer—myself” (Ludovici). “Not to their disadvantage” becomes “they both deny life, they both slander it … precisely on this account they are my antipodes.” New sentences add revenge on life, “humaneness,” the “free spirits” and “beautiful souls” as décadents, the Christian as a kind of Epicurean, and Goethe against Flaubert. The criterion changes: 1887 asked whether hunger or overflow had become creative; 1888 asks whether “a loathing of life, or … an excessive plenitude of life” did (Ludovici). The second distinction and the announcement of Dionysian pessimism are cut.

What the placement does. The title word “antipodes” enters, and a self-critical history becomes a verdict. The new criterion, hatred of life, is harsher than hunger, and it builds the verdict into the test. Read the Wagner and decadence theme on §370.

Where Wagner Belongs

Then. Beyond Good and Evil §254 and §256 (1886). §254 says that France is still the seat of Europe’s most refined culture, hidden behind a coarsened public France. §256 argues that Europe “wants to become one,” and counts Wagner with Napoleon, Goethe, Beethoven, Stendhal, Heine, and Schopenhauer among those who prepared it. It ties him to the French late romantics, describes them as daring and violent “higher men,” allows that Wagner’s Siegfried is beyond the reach of the Latin races, and ends with a poem against the late Wagner’s Parsifal.

What changed. The two sections are joined and most of both is cut, including the argument that Europe wants to become one and the praise of Siegfried. New matter includes a jibe at the North German press, the claim that Schopenhauer is better read in French, the German “horned cattle,” Wagner’s mockery of Paris in its “agony in 1871,” and the young emperor who understands nothing of Wagner. The late romantics are now “artists” rather than “higher men,” and the chapter ends: “But they were ill” (Ludovici). The North German jibe says that Nietzsche would look for “the blackest spot on earth, where slaves still required to be liberated” near Northern Germany (Ludovici). It turns a colonial image of Africa against Germany.

What the placement does. In 1886 Wagner’s French affinities were evidence of a European synthesis. In 1888 they show that he is not German and that he is sick. The late romantics are praised in almost the same words, and demoted by a single added sentence.

Wagner as the Apostle of Chastity §§1–3

Then. §1 is the poem that closes Beyond Good and Evil §256: is this sultry, incense-laden, self-lacerating music still German? What you hear is Rome, “Rome’s faith without words.” §§2–3 come from On the Genealogy of Morals III §§2–3 (1887), where Wagner’s late homage to chastity is a case in the question “What do ascetic ideals mean?” Sensuality and chastity need not be opposed, and good marriages go beyond the opposition. Was Parsifal meant seriously? One could wish it were a satyr play, a parody of the tragic. The section goes on to suggest that it would have been worthy of a great tragedian to laugh at himself.

What changed. §1 changes a few words. In §2 the Genealogy’s question is removed, together with a suggestion that Wagner should have written a Luther comedy; the “unfortunate pigs” who worship chastity become “Circe’s unfortunate animals.” §3 adds that Gottfried Keller laughed at Parsifal and that it is an operetta subject. It drops the praise of a tragedian who laughs at himself, and ends with a new verdict: “For ‘Parsifal’ is a work of rancour, of revenge … it is a bad work. The preaching of chastity remains an incitement to unnaturalness: I despise anybody who does not regard ‘Parsifal’ as an outrage upon morality” (Ludovici).

What the placement does. The Genealogy opened a question and went on, in §4, to warn against taking an artist as seriously as his work. The 1888 chapter closes the question with contempt. The Genealogy’s wish that Parsifal might be a joke becomes a rhetorical step toward a verdict.

How I Got Free of Wagner §§1–2

Then. §§3–4 of the Preface Nietzsche added in 1886 to the second volume of Human, All Too Human. It was then high time to take leave, and proof came soon: Wagner, a decayed and despairing “romantic,” sank before the Christian cross. Nietzsche went on alone, ill and tired, disgusted with “the feminine and the rapturously undisciplined” in this romanticism, and then forbade himself romantic music. §4 describes siding against himself and the “tyrant” of one’s task, which punishes every evasion with illness.

What changed. §1 gains a new opening: “Already in the summer of 1876, when the first festival at Bayreuth was at its height, I took leave of Wagner in my soul. I cannot endure anything double-faced. Since Wagner had returned to Germany, he had condescended step by step to everything that I despise—even to anti-Semitism” (Ludovici). “Romantic” becomes “décadent.” The phrase about the feminine is cut, and so is the continuation about romantic music. New lines close the section: “For I had no one save Richard Wagner.… I was always condemned to the society of Germans” (Ludovici). §2 is nearly unchanged; “romantic” falsehood becomes “idealistic.”

What the placement does. The added opening gives the break a precise date and a moral motive. Treat both as Nietzsche’s retrospective claims. The historical record complicates them. Nietzsche and Wagner still met in Sorrento in the autumn of 1876, after the festival. Wagner’s antisemitism was not a late development: his essay on “Jewishness in Music” appeared under a pseudonym in 1850 and was reissued under his own name in 1869, during the years of Nietzsche’s closest friendship with him. Wagner moved to Bayreuth in 1872. The 1886 Preface had already called the Bayreuth essay of 1876 “a detachment, a leave-taking” (§1, this guide’s translation), but the section selected here says only that it was “then” high time to go, and gives no reason involving antisemitism. The festival as the moment of parting, and antisemitism as a reason, are new in 1888.

The Psychologist Speaks §§1–3

Then. Beyond Good and Evil §§269–270 (1886), in Part Nine, “What Is Noble?” The born psychologist who studies higher men risks suffocating with pity, because their ruin is the rule. Great men are fictions invented afterward, and the work invents its author. Poets such as Byron, Musset, Poe, Leopardi, Kleist, and Gogol conceal a break in their souls. Woman’s pity overestimates its power. §269 goes on to suggest that the life of Jesus may hide a martyrdom of knowledge about love. §270 says that deep suffering makes noble and needs disguises, and ends that a finer humanity has reverence “for the mask.”

What changed. §269 is divided into §§1–2. The veneration of great men as models for the young is cut; so is the passage on Jesus. Nietzsche adds “I do not dare to mention much greater names, but I imply them” and “We are all special pleaders in the cause of mediocrity” (Ludovici). §3 adds Hamlet as the case of the free spirit who hides a broken heart, and drops the closing sentence about reverence for the mask.

What the placement does. These chapters never mention Wagner. Placed after the story of the break, they make the psychologist Nietzsche and the ruined great man Wagner, or perhaps both Nietzsche. The cut passage on Jesus removes the sympathetic case; the cut sentence on the mask removes the reason not to pry.

Epilogue §§1–2

Then. §§3–4 of the Preface to the second edition of The Gay Science, dated autumn 1886 and published in 1887. A philosopher who has passed through many states of health passes through as many philosophies. Great pain is the last liberator of the spirit. One returns from such illness with a second, more dangerous innocence, disgusted with the educated public’s art and wanting an art for artists. The Greeks were superficial out of profundity.

What changed. §1 gains a new opening: “I have often asked myself whether I am not much more deeply indebted to the hardest years of my life than to any others,” with the formula “Amor fati: this is the very core of my being” (Ludovici). The discussion of philosophy as the transfiguration of states of health is cut, and so is a reference to Nirvana. §2 adds the sentence on being “the deepest spirit of all the millennia” (this guide’s translation; absent from Ludovici, see the variants) and “Tout comprendre—c’est tout mépriser.” The Preface’s date is removed.

What the placement does. A preface about recovery becomes the last word in a quarrel. The Epilogue does not mention Wagner, but after the story of the break its illness is the illness of 1876–1879 and its recovery is the recovery from Wagner. The art for artists it wants is the antithesis of Bayreuth.

On the Poverty of the Richest Man

Then. The same poem is the last of the Dionysus-Dithyrambs, which Nietzsche prepared in the same weeks and did not publish.

What it says. Zarathustra, ten years without a drop of rain or love, asks his wisdom to overflow. His truth then speaks: he is too rich, he makes too many envious and poor, and he must first give himself away. “One loves only those who suffer”; he must become poorer if he wants to be loved (this guide’s translation).

What the placement does. The book closes in a voice other than the psychologist’s. After chapters that rank men by suffering, the poem says that love goes only to the sufferer and the hungry. Nietzsche planned it as the ending on 16 December and asked for it back on 2 January; see composition.

Writing exercise

Read one document against its source. Choose one chapter from the map. These three repay the work well:

  • “Where I Admire,” against The Gay Science §87.
  • “We Antipodes,” against The Gay Science §370.
  • “Wagner as the Apostle of Chastity” §3, against On the Genealogy of Morals III §3 and §4.

Write two short columns. In the first, record what the earlier passage says and where it stood in its book, with section numbers and no more than a sentence of quotation. In the second, record what the 1888 version says, what it adds, and what it removes. Then write a paragraph that answers three questions. Is the change a clarification, an abbreviation, or a new claim? What does the new heading and the chapter’s new position lead the reader to expect? Does the revision strengthen Nietzsche’s claim of a ten-year war, or weaken it? End with one sentence stating what the earlier passage must still be read for on its own terms.

Where it leads

Back to the sources. Each selected chapter sends you back to an earlier published book. The Preface is new, and the Intermezzo and poem come from unpublished work of the same weeks. The most rewarding returns are The Gay Science Book V and its 1887 Preface, Beyond Good and Evil Parts Eight and Nine, and the Third Essay of the Genealogy, where the passages have arguments this book does not need.

Across to the other 1888 accounts. The Case of Wagner is the polemic this collection was meant to support, and Ecce Homo shares its Intermezzo and retells the friendship. Compare the three versions of the break.

Back to the praise. “Richard Wagner in Bayreuth” and the foreword to Wagner in The Birth of Tragedy are the texts this book most needs to be read against. It quotes neither.

  • Beyond Good and EvilRead §§254–256 and 269–270 before their cuts.
  • On the Genealogy of MoralsFollow the question about Parsifal into the argument about ascetic ideals.
  • Human, All Too HumanRead the 1879–1880 sequels and the 1886 Preface that supply the book’s oldest pages.
  • Body, physiology, naturalismTest the claim that aesthetics is applied physiology.
  • Women and genderFollow the figures of woman that carry the book’s judgments.
