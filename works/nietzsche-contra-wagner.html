Nietzsche Contra Wagner

Nietzsche chooses passages from his earlier writing to make the history of his break with Wagner readable in a new order.

Overview and context

At the end of 1888 Nietzsche assembled passages chiefly from his earlier publications, plus an intermezzo drawn from the still-unpublished Ecce Homo (“Why I Am So Clever” §7), shortening and adjusting them in places to set himself against Wagner. The result is an authored selection, not a new treatise written continuously and not an editor’s posthumous notebook compilation. Its order and juxtapositions are new; much of its prose had appeared elsewhere. Reading it well means asking both what an excerpt says and what its relocation now makes it do.

The Preface calls Nietzsche and Wagner opposites, yet the opening selection praises Wagner’s attention to suffering. The tension matters. The book asks whether one can admire a talent, diagnose its costs, and still learn something from a former allegiance. Do not use its retrospective arrangement to erase the admiration expressed in the early Wagner meditation, or to make every earlier passage say exactly what its late placement suggests.

Guided reading path

Read the author-assembled work from its Preface through the Epilogue and the closing poem, “On the Poverty of the Richest Man,” in an edition that includes them. The Intermezzo belongs between the chapters on objections and Wagner as a danger. Chapter headings vary across English editions; the free Ludovici volume places this work after The Case of Wagner but omits the Intermezzo and closing poem. Its text ends at the Epilogue, before the separate “Selected Aphorisms.”

  1. Read nowPreface; “Wherein I Admire Wagner”; “Wherein I Raise Objections”

    Then read: The Intermezzo in an edition that includes it; the linked Ludovici text omits it.

    Question: Which Wagnerian ability does Nietzsche still prize, and why does that praise fail to overcome his objection? Compare his account of intimate suffering with his demand for an art that does not exhaust the listener. What changes when the Intermezzo interrupts the argument?

  2. Read now“Wagner As A Danger” through “Wagner As The Apostle Of Chastity,” inclusive

    Question: How does Nietzsche move from artistic technique to a claim about what the audience needs? Notice that a charge about sensuality, chastity, or salvation is a philosophical judgment about values, not just a report of a sound.

  3. Read nowRemaining chapters and Epilogue

    Then read: “On the Poverty of the Richest Man” in an edition that includes it; the linked Ludovici text omits the poem.

    Question: What kind of distance from Wagner does Nietzsche finally claim? In the Epilogue, trace how suffering and amor fati enter his self-description. Read the closing poem as a further ending: does it simply deny his earlier attraction, or reinterpret it?

Writing exercise: Take one chapter, find the earlier book named or implied by its source, and compare its original context with this selection’s new surroundings. Explain one interpretive gain and one possible distortion caused by the excerpt’s new placement.

Texts and editions

Walter Kaufmann is the preferred translator in The Portable Nietzsche; check that printing for the Intermezzo and closing poem. The preferred Case of Wagner is in a different Kaufmann volume, Basic Writings. The free alternative by Anthony M. Ludovici combines both Nietzsche works and the translator’s prefaces. It omits the Intermezzo and closing poem found in the critical text, so its ending at the Epilogue is incomplete as a route through that text. Its separately headed “Selected Aphorisms” are Nietzsche’s unpublished 1878 notes selected for this English volume; they are not part of Nietzsche Contra Wagner. Use the contents and headings, not the combined volume’s cover title, to keep boundaries clear.

Nietzsche dated the Preface Christmas 1888. A Naumann private printing bears the date 1889; the work was not publicly issued in the ordinary way during his active lifetime, unlike the published 1888 Case of Wagner. Nietzsche selected and sometimes edited these passages himself, while their first contexts remain relevant to interpretation.

Interpretive questions

What an excerpt can change

Selection is an argument. A passage from an earlier book may originally have addressed a wider problem, then become evidence of a long-standing anti-Wagner position here. Compare the dates and contexts before assuming the new sequence proves an unchanged view. Nietzsche’s Preface itself acknowledges careful selection and some shortening.

Admiration and diagnosis

The opening praise of Wagner’s expression of hidden distress makes the objections harder to dismiss as a personal feud. Yet a capacity to express pain is not by itself evidence that an artwork makes its audience unhealthy. Track where Nietzsche provides a reason and where he relies on an evaluative contrast he expects readers to share.

The author as interpreter of himself

Nietzsche’s narration of what he always saw is part of his late philosophical self-presentation. Compare it with Ecce Homo, which also rereads his books. Neither late account needs to be treated as a neutral record of his earlier motives.

Where it leads

For the fresh 1888 polemic, read The Case of Wagner. For the early Wagner relationship, read Untimely Meditations. Then use Wagner and decadence to trace the conflict beyond these two late books.
