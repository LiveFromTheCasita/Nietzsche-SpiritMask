Overview and context

Beyond Good and Evil investigates the values already at work when we claim to discover the truth. A philosopher may present an argument as impersonal while its questions, priorities, and picture of the world express a particular organization of desires. Nietzsche then asks what would distinguish a more honest, demanding, and creative philosophy from another disguised prejudice.

Published in 1886, the book follows Thus Spoke Zarathustra and precedes On the Genealogy of Morals (1887). It connects their questions about value creation and moral hierarchy through experiments in psychology, criticism, and cultural diagnosis. The forms differ: the dramatic teacher of Zarathustra gives way to a shifting philosophical voice, while the Genealogy will develop several of these problems through three sustained essays.

The nine parts contain 296 continuously numbered sections, framed by an unnumbered Preface and a closing poem. The sections range from single sentences to substantial arguments. Their sequence matters even when it proceeds through contrasts, interruptions, and changes of tone. Part IV’s short aphorisms belong to this design; they train forms of attention that the longer arguments also require.

The title does not promise an end to valuation. Nietzsche rejects the authority of a particular opposition between good and evil while continuing to rank characters, practices, and ways of living. His alternative rankings can be severe. This guide treats his claims about social domination, women, peoples, and religion as parts of the philosophical problem, with reasons and evidence to assess.

A useful question throughout is: What authorizes this judgment? Ask it of inherited morality, of the philosopher who seeks to create values, and of your own response to Nietzsche. The book opens that inquiry without supplying a settled rule that resolves every case.

Guided reading path

This complete ten-lesson introductory course uses selected readings from all nine parts, together with the complete Preface and closing poem. It does not assign every section of the book. Readings 1–2 establish the questions of truth, selfhood, and will; Readings 3–9 follow Parts II–VIII; Reading 10 brings Part IX and the poem into a final synthesis.

Allow roughly 13–18 hours for the primary text, commentary, rereading, questions, and writing. Each lesson supplies exact section numbers, five questions with suggested answers to reveal after trying them, and a writing exercise. The final response returns to the value you chose at the beginning. Longer lessons can be divided across two sittings.

Read in your preferred edition. Links to a credited free alternative are available on every lesson page. The full part ranges below provide a route for returning to the entire book after completing the introductory selection.

If you have an hour

For a short independent orientation, read §1 in Part I, §211 in Part VI, and §260 in Part IX. These introduce the value of truth, the philosopher as a creator of values, and the contrast between noble and slave moralities. Allow 50–60 minutes for the sections and a few notes. The longer course supplies their surrounding problems and tests their connections.

Write three sentences: why you value truth; what could authorize someone to create values for others; and whether the origin of a moral judgment settles its worth. Return to them after reading. Where does Nietzsche change your question, and where does his answer need a stronger argument?

Choose an edition and find passages

The site’s preferred translation is Walter Kaufmann. The free alternative is Helen Zimmern’s translation at Project Gutenberg. Its closing poem is translated separately by L. A. Magnus. Kaufmann’s From High Mountains appears there as From the Heights.

The section numbers run continuously from 1 to 296; they do not restart with each part. Use section numbers rather than page numbers. Every listed section is assigned in full. The Preface and closing poem are unnumbered. Modern introductions and editorial notes are separate from Nietzsche’s text.

The online links open the relevant part because this edition has no individual section anchors. Use Find for a section number followed by a period, then read until the next numbered section. Part titles differ: Zimmern calls Part III The Religious Mood, Part IV Apophthegms and Interludes, Part VI We Scholars, and Part VIII Peoples and Countries. The numbered ranges below remain the same.

Zimmern’s older vocabulary can obscure a distinction. This guide uses drives where she often writes impulses, and honesty for the virtue discussed in §227. Do not assume that every occurrence of truthfulness, sincerity, and intellectual honesty names exactly the same activity. Section references let you compare the surrounding argument.

Core ideas

Truth can become a question of value

§§1–6 ask what directs inquiry and whether truth’s value is unconditional. Psychological explanation and logical assessment remain distinct: discovering a motive does not refute an argument. The harder task is to show how a valuation shapes the questions asked, the evidence admitted, or the possibilities excluded.

The self is an organization to investigate

§§12, 16–19 challenge the apparently simple thinker and willing subject. A will includes commanding, obeying, sensations, thoughts, and an attribution of success. The analysis opens a way to study relations among drives; it does not make the absence of a simple soul equivalent to the absence of all agency.

Will to power appears in different registers

§13 makes an assertive claim about living things. §36 explores a conditional extension from drives and willing to the world as a whole. §259 makes a forceful claim about appropriation and exploitation. Their differences matter. Treating every passage as a settled universal doctrine, or every passage as a harmless possibility, removes a real tension in the book.

Creating values requires capacities and claims authority

§188 makes prolonged discipline a condition of cultural achievement; §211 gives philosophers the task of determining ends. Creativity here exceeds expressing a personal preference. It involves cultivated capacities and claims over a human future. What licenses that authority, and whose interests count in deciding the future, remain central questions.

Rank concerns society as well as the individual

§§257–260 connect cultural elevation to aristocratic social arrangements and distinguish noble and slave valuations. They also allow mixed moral types within one person. The psychological complexity does not erase the argument about actual domination. Later accounts of self-reverence and solitude complicate nobility without making its costs disappear.

An honest critic can still have prejudices

§227 praises honesty while warning that a virtue can become a source of vanity. §§231–239 make this problem acute in Nietzsche’s claims about women. Part VIII similarly combines resistance to nationalism and antisemitic agitation with collective stereotypes and an aristocratic political horizon. Read his judgments against the standards his inquiry asks others to meet.

Nine-part walkthrough

These are the full ranges of the book’s parts. The introductory assignments above select from them; use this map for a later reading of every section.

I. On the Prejudices of Philosophers · §§1–23

The opening unsettles the assumptions through which philosophy claims certainty: oppositions of value, a simple subject, an immediately known will, and a world already divided into established categories. Nietzsche turns toward psychology while exposing interpretation inside accounts of nature. Ask when his diagnosis is supported and when it is being proposed for investigation.

Open Part I in the free edition

II. The Free Spirit · §§24–44

The free spirit is tested through independence, danger, masks, unintended motives, and the capacity to bear unwelcome knowledge. Nietzsche distinguishes this figure from the conventional freethinker. §36’s speculative experiment belongs here among questions about intellectual courage and method. Independence acquires demands and exclusions that complicate its appeal.

Open Part II in the free edition

III. What Is Religious · §§45–62

Nietzsche examines religious experience, the religious person’s inner transformations, and the changing objects of sacrifice. §56 introduces an ideal of affirmation. §§61–62 ask what religion cultivates when it serves a larger project and what happens when its judgments govern human worth. Religion has different effects for different people in a hierarchy that itself requires judgment.

Open Part III in the free edition

IV. Epigrams and Interludes · §§63–185

A sequence of compressed observations interrupts the longer arguments. Some formulations reverse a familiar maxim; others become uncertain when followed beyond their first effect. Read them in company, attend to qualifications, and distinguish an arresting possibility from a universal finding. The brevity makes the reader responsible for more of the interpretive work.

Open Part IV in the free edition

V. On the Natural History of Morals · §§186–203

The announced natural history of morals begins by questioning whether moral philosophers have gathered enough comparative material. Discipline and obedience then acquire explanatory importance, followed by accounts of prophetic value reversal and herd morality. Nietzsche moves from descriptions of formation to urgent evaluations of Europe’s future. Track the additional assumptions required by that move.

Open Part V in the free edition

VI. We Scholars · §§204–213

The book distinguishes scholarly competence, objective receptivity, criticism, and philosophical legislation. Nietzsche values capacities he also finds insufficient to determine ends. The future philosopher would combine demanding preparation with the power to create values. The central difficulty is whether exceptional capacities justify the authority he gives their possessor.

Open Part VI in the free edition

VII. Our Virtues · §§214–239

The inquiry turns toward the virtues of its own kind of reader. Honesty, pity, suffering, and the cruelty involved in inquiry become subjects of examination. The final sections on women test whether Nietzsche’s self-disclosure helps him investigate a prejudice or gives it a shelter. Those sections belong to the assessment of his method.

Open Part VII in the free edition

VIII. Peoples and Fatherlands · §§240–256

Music and national character provide starting points for a diagnosis of Europe. Nietzsche criticizes nationalism and antisemitic agitation, yet his account retains stereotypes, selective cultivation, and a hoped-for governing elite. His larger European horizon changes the scale of the political problem without settling the claims of the people within it.

Open Part VIII in the free edition

IX. What Is Noble? · §§257–296

The social conditions of aristocracy lead into noble and slave valuations, then into more elusive questions of self-reverence, solitude, masks, and communication. §§295–296 end with Dionysus and the problem of fixing thoughts in words. The closing poem makes changed friendship part of the conclusion. Read these forms of inwardness beside the earlier claims about who supports and pays for cultural achievement.

Open Part IX in the free edition

The Preface and the closing poem

The Preface presents a struggle whose accumulated tension could make new goals possible. The closing poem stages a failed reunion with former friends and the possibility of new company. Together they make philosophy a question of inherited capacities, transformed selves, and difficult communication. Include both in a complete reading.

Where it leads

Back to Zarathustra. Compare the creator of values in §211 with the teacher who struggles to find companions in Thus Spoke Zarathustra. Compare §56’s ideal of affirmation with the dramatic difficulty of affirming recurrence. The books can illuminate one another without making every speech by Zarathustra a direct statement of the later book’s arguments.

Forward to the Genealogy. Read §260 alongside On the Genealogy of Morals, Essay I, for a more developed account of rival valuations. Compare §188 with Essay II’s formation of memory and conscience; compare §§1 and 227 with Essay III’s final inquiry into the value of truth. These are routes for comparison, not a claim that the essays simply repeat this book.

Then to Twilight of the Idols. The compressed polemics of Twilight of the Idols return to reason, morality, causation, and cultural judgment. Carry forward the distinction between exposing an assumption and establishing a better account. A sharper diagnosis still has to answer for its own values.
