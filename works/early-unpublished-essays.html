Overview and status

This guide covers two complete essays that Nietzsche wrote while he was a professor of classical philology at Basel, in the months after The Birth of Tragedy appeared in 1872. Neither was published in his lifetime. They are separate pieces of writing, and Nietzsche did not arrange them as a pair. This guide reads them in chronological order and sets them side by side, but the pairing is the guide’s.

“Homer’s Contest” (Homer’s Wettkampf) is dated at its end 29 December 1872. It is the last of five short prefaces to books Nietzsche did not write, which he wrote out as a set for Cosima Wagner at Christmas 1872. It asks what the Greeks did with the cruelty and envy they did not try to abolish, and answers: they gave the good kind of strife, rivalry, an institution, the contest. “On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense” (Ueber Wahrheit und Lüge im aussermoralischen Sinne) was written in 1873 in two numbered sections. It asks where the human drive to truth comes from, and answers that truthfulness begins as a social obligation to use the agreed metaphors, which people then forget are metaphors.

The two essays are connected in ways that can be checked, and different in ways that matter. The fable of the “clever animals” that opens the truth essay first appears, almost word for word, in the first of the same five prefaces, “On the Pathos of Truth.” Both essays set Greek culture against modern culture, and both treat a force a culture cannot abolish as the material from which it builds. But one is a philologist’s argument about an ancient institution, full of examples from Hesiod, Herodotus, and Thucydides. The other is a general argument about language, knowledge, and art, conducted in the terms of Kant and Schopenhauer. A reader who treats them as one theory will miss what each one argues.

They are early and unpublished. That matters for two reasons. The essays cannot be read as Nietzsche’s settled positions of 1872–1873, still less as the later books in draft. And their sharpest claims are exposed in a way published arguments are not: there is no preface, no revision, and no reply to objections. This guide states what each essay claims and asks what those claims would need.

Both essays state hierarchies plainly, and this guide does too. “Homer’s Contest” praises a Greek “tiger-like pleasure in destruction” and calls the modern idea of humanity “emasculate” (Mügge’s translation). Its contest is a contest among citizens and cities; it says nothing of the women, slaves, and foreigners outside it. It reads the fall of Athens and Sparta as proof that a state without envy and rivalry degenerates. “On Truth and Lies” says that concepts make possible “a pyramidal order with castes and grades” (Mügge), and in its last pages it ranks the intuitive man above the man of reason. The passage notes cite each claim.

Carry one question through both essays: Is this a description of how something came about, or a judgment of what it is worth?

Guided reading path

This path covers both essays complete, in the order they were written: “Homer’s Contest” in three stages, then “On Truth and Lies” in four. Neither essay is divided into numbered sections except the truth essay’s §§1–2, so the stages begin and end at named sentences. The page numbers are those of the free Mügge translation, whose links open the page on which a stage begins. The stages are this guide’s divisions.

Mügge titles the truth essay “On Truth and Falsity in Their Ultramoral Sense.” His volume also contains a translator’s preface and five other essays, which are not assigned here. See the edition notes.

  1. Stage 1“Homer’s Contest”: from the opening to the Orphic answer (Mügge, pp. 51–54)

    Read: from the first sentence, “When one speaks of ‘humanity’,” through the paragraph on Orpheus and Musaeus, which ends with the promise of another Greek answer “in the whole breadth of Greek history.” Stop at “In order to understand the latter we must start from the fact.”

    Why this stage: The essay opens with a thesis: the “natural” and the properly “human” qualities have grown up inseparably, and the capacities called inhuman may be the soil of all humanity. Nietzsche then gives his evidence. Alexander drags the defender of Gaza behind his chariot; victorious cities execute the men of a defeated city and sell the women and children; the sculptors and the Iliad exult in fighting. Behind Homer lies a pre-Homeric world of “night and horror.” One answer to that world was the Orphic loathing of existence.

    Questions: Is the opening claim that “humanity” and “nature” cannot be separated a description, a hypothesis, or a program? Nietzsche calls Homer’s world an “artistic deception” laid over the horrible. Which kinds of evidence does he give for the pre-Homeric world beneath it, and which does he imagine?

  2. Stage 2“Homer’s Contest”: the two Eris-goddesses to ostracism (pp. 54–58)

    Read: from “In order to understand the latter we must start from the fact” through the paragraph on ostracism, which ends: “it desires as a preventive against the genius—a second genius.” Stop at “Every natural gift must develop itself by contest.”

    Why this stage: Here is the essay’s central argument. The Greek genius accepted the “fearful impulse” instead of condemning life for it. Hesiod’s Works and Days distinguishes two Eris-goddesses: a bad one who leads to war and a good one who, as envy and jealousy, stirs men to work and rivalry. The Greeks feared the envy of the gods, and so man must never contend with a god. Ostracism, in its original sense, removed the one who had become the best so that the contest could continue.

    Questions: What distinguishes the good Eris from the bad one: the feeling itself, its object, or the institution it acts within? Ostracism is said to be a “stimulant,” not a “safety-valve.” What would count as evidence for one reading of the institution rather than the other?

  3. Stage 3“Homer’s Contest”: agonistic education to the end (pp. 58–62)

    Read: from “Every natural gift must develop itself by contest” to the last sentence, on Alexander and “the so-called ‘Hellenism’.” Stop at the heading of the next essay in the volume. The German text ends with a date, absent from Mügge; see the variants.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche turns from institutions to education and art. Greek pedagogy unchained ambition; the Athenian youth competed for his city’s glory, and his selfishness was “kindled into flame” and “bridled and restricted” in the same act. Teachers, poets, sophists, and Plato competed too. Then comes the reverse case: take away the contest, and the pre-Homeric abyss returns. Miltiades, alone after Marathon, falls into petty revenge and sacrilege; Athens and Sparta, victorious, fall through hybris.

    Questions: The essay claims that individuals in antiquity were “freer, because their aims were nearer and more tangible.” Freer in what sense, and for whom? The fall of Athens and Sparta is offered “as a proof.” What would it take for a historical example to prove a general claim about states?

  4. Stage 4“On Truth and Lies” §1: the fable and the intellect as dissimulation (pp. 173–176)

    Read: from the fable, “In some remote corner of the universe,” through the paragraph that ends with the question “Whence, in the wide world, with this state of affairs, arises the impulse to truth?” Stop at “As far as the individual tries to preserve himself.”

    Why this stage: The essay begins with a cosmic joke: clever animals invent knowing on a star that soon cools, and nothing is changed. The gnat, too, feels itself the flying centre of the world. The intellect is a device for preserving weak creatures, and its chief power is dissimulation. So the question is not how humans deceive, but how an “honest and pure impulse to truth” could ever arise among them.

    Questions: What does the fable establish, and what does it only make vivid? The passage says that nature “threw away the key” to what lies beneath consciousness. Is that a claim about what we can know, or about what we can bear to know?

  5. Stage 5“On Truth and Lies” §1: the peace treaty, words, and concepts (pp. 176–180)

    Read: from “As far as the individual tries to preserve himself against other individuals” through the paragraph beginning “What therefore is truth?”, which ends with coins “no longer of account as coins but merely as metal.” Stop at “Still we do not yet know whence the impulse to truth comes.”

    Why this stage: Here is the argument most readers know. Society needs a peace treaty, and the treaty fixes a binding designation of things: here the contrast of truth and lie first arises. The liar is excluded not because he deceives but because he harms. A word is the expression of a nerve stimulus in sound; each step from stimulus to image to sound is a leap into a different sphere. A concept arises by “equating the unequal”: no leaf is like another, yet we form the concept “leaf.” Truth is “a mobile army of metaphors, metonymies, anthropomorphisms.”

    Questions: The passage argues from the way words and concepts are formed to a claim about what they can grasp. Where exactly is the step, and is it valid? Nietzsche also says that we cannot claim the division into individual and species fails to correspond to the essence of things, since that would be “a dogmatic assertion.” How does that caution limit his own conclusion?

  6. Stage 6“On Truth and Lies” §1: the feeling for truth to the forms of time and space (pp. 180–187)

    Read: from “Still we do not yet know whence the impulse to truth comes” to the end of §1, “an imitation of the relations of time, space and number in the realm of metaphors.” The four footnotes that follow in the free text are Mügge’s. Stop at §2.

    Why this stage: Nietzsche now answers his question. Truthfulness is the obligation to lie according to a fixed convention; forgetting that obligation produces a moral feeling for truth. Concepts make possible a new world of “laws, privileges, sub-orders,” the rigid “Roman Columbarium” of ideas. Finding a truth within this system is like finding a thing where one hid it: define the mammal, then say of a camel, “Behold a mammal.” Man lives with some repose only by forgetting that he is an artistically creating subject. The section ends by tracing the apparent lawfulness of nature to the forms of time, space, and number that we bring to it.

    Questions: The camel example is said to give a truth of “very limited value.” Is a truth of limited value a falsehood? If “right perception” is a “nonentity full of contradictions,” can the essay itself describe perception rightly?

  7. Stage 7“On Truth and Lies” §2 (pp. 187–192)

    Read: all of §2, from “As we saw, it is language which has worked originally at the construction of ideas” to the last sentence, in which the Stoic “walks away from beneath” the storm. The volume ends there.

    Why this stage: The second section turns from the origin of truth to its rivals. Science builds ever higher storeys on the “columbarium of ideas.” But the fundamental drive to form metaphors cannot be reasoned away; it finds another channel in myth and art. The early Greeks lived a waking life more like a dream. When the intellect can deceive without doing harm, it celebrates its “Saturnalia.” The essay ends with two figures, the rational and the intuitive man, and gives each its way of meeting misfortune.

    Questions: Does §2 praise the intuitive man, or describe him? The Stoic at the end also performs “the masterpiece of dissimulation.” If both figures dissemble, what distinguishes them?

After Stage 7, complete the writing exercise. It asks you to compare one argument in each essay.

A shorter first encounter

For an entrance before the full path, read five passages, about six pages in the free edition. From “Homer’s Contest”: the opening paragraph, the paragraphs on Hesiod’s two Eris-goddesses, and the paragraph on ostracism. From “On Truth and Lies”: the opening fable and the paragraphs from “Let us especially think about the formation of ideas” through “What therefore is truth?”

Then make one comparison. Each essay describes a practice that turns something unequal into something shared: the contest makes rivals equal enough to compete, and the concept makes unequal things equal enough to name. Write two sentences on what each practice gains, and one on what each loses. Then check your answer against the stages you skipped. This short route is an entrance, not a substitute for either essay’s second half, where the arguments are tested.

Texts, editions, and variants

The source guide recommends no single Kaufmann or Hollingdale edition for this material. The free translation assigned here is Maximilian A. Mügge’s. Modern anthologies often include one or both essays, sometimes in part and under different titles. If you use one, check that it prints the whole essay and compare its translator’s key terms with the notes below. Quotations on this page are Mügge’s wording, labeled as such, or this guide’s translation of the German critical text, also labeled.

Choose an edition and find passages

The free text. Project Gutenberg’s Early Greek Philosophy & Other Essays reproduces volume two of the English Complete Works edited by Oscar Levy (Edinburgh and London: T. N. Foulis, 1911), translated by Mügge. The translator says the text follows volume I of the German pocket edition (“Taschenausgabe”) and that the dates after the titles are the years of writing. The volume contains:

  • Mügge’s “Translator’s Preface” is his own summary of the essays. It says that traces of Nietzsche’s later ideas, “like Slave and Master morality, the Superman,” can be found everywhere in them. Read it as early reception, not as Nietzsche’s text.
  • Seven essays, in this order: “The Greek State” (“Preface to an Unwritten Book,” dated 1871); “The Greek Woman” (fragment, 1871); “On Music and Words” (fragment, 1871); “Homer’s Contest” (“Preface to an Unwritten Book,” 1872); “The Relation of Schopenhauer’s Philosophy to a German Culture” (“Preface to an Unwritten Book,” 1872); “Philosophy during the Tragic Age of the Greeks” (1873), with two prefaces and “Notes for a Continuation”; and “On Truth and Falsity in Their Ultramoral Sense” (1873).
  • Only the two essays in bold are assigned. The others are also unpublished early writings, with their own subjects. “The Greek State” is discussed on the greatness and the agon theme.
  • Footnotes. The truth essay has four footnotes in the free text: on Lessing’s son, and on the genders of German nouns and the word “serpent.” All are marked “—TR.” and are Mügge’s.

The five prefaces. The German critical text prints “Homer’s Contest” as the fifth of Fünf Vorreden zu fünf ungeschriebenen Büchern (Five Prefaces to Five Unwritten Books). Its dedication says they were written out for Cosima Wagner in the Christmas days of 1872. The five are “On the Pathos of Truth,” “On the Future of Our Educational Institutions,” “The Greek State,” “On the Relation of Schopenhauer’s Philosophy to a German Culture,” and “Homer’s Contest” (this guide’s translations of the titles). Mügge’s volume prints three of them and labels each “Preface to an Unwritten Book”; it does not print “On the Pathos of Truth,” which contains the first version of the truth essay’s fable. The set is an arrangement Nietzsche did make. It does not include the truth essay, which is later.

Text differences and translation choices

These were found by comparing Mügge with the critical text for both essays. This is not a full collation. Some items are differences of text; most are translation choices that affect an argument.

“Homer’s Contest.”

  • The date. The German text ends “Beendet am 29ten Dezember 1872”: finished on 29 December 1872. Mügge omits it.
  • Miltiades’ trial. After the siege is raised, the German adds “das Volksgericht erwartet ihn”: the people’s court awaits him (this guide’s translation). Mügge passes straight to the disgraceful death.
  • “Humanity.” The first word of the essay is Humanität, humaneness as an ideal, not Menschheit, the human race. Mügge’s “humanity” can mean either. The essay is about the ideal.
  • “Emasculate.” Mügge’s “the emasculate idea of modern humanity” translates weichlich, soft or effete.
  • “A new light.” Mügge: “at every great virtue a new light is kindled.” The German says a new greatness is kindled (“eine neue Größe”).

“On Truth and Lies.”

  • Concept, not “idea.” Mügge translates Begriff as “idea” throughout: “the formation of ideas,” “Every idea originates through equating the unequal.” The German word is concept. Read Mügge’s “idea” as concept, not as a Platonic Idea or a mental image.
  • Image, not “percept.” “A nerve-stimulus, first transformed into a percept! First metaphor!” (Mügge). The German has Bild, image.
  • “Transferences.” Mügge’s “what arbitrary metaphors!” translates “welche willkürlichen Übertragungen!”. Übertragung means transfer, the literal sense of the Greek metaphora, and the essay’s argument turns on it.
  • “Principle of sufficient reason.” Mügge’s “proposition of causality” translates Satz vom Grunde, the principle of sufficient reason.
  • “World history.” The fable calls the invention of knowing the most arrogant and mendacious minute of „Weltgeschichte“, in quotation marks. Mügge’s “the history of this world” loses the irony, and his “moment” replaces the German “minute.”
  • Die and balloon. The concept is “bony and hexa-hedral” in Mügge; the German says eight-cornered (achteckig), probably by a slip for a die. Mügge’s “swell up like a balloon” renders Schlauch, a wineskin or bladder.
  • “Appearance.” Mügge: “The word ‘phenomenon’ contains many seductions.” The German word is Erscheinung, appearance, the Kantian term.
  • Perception and intuition. Mügge renders Anschauung sometimes as “perception” and Intuition as “intuition.” In §2 the “intuitive man” is der intuitive Mensch; the “cemetery of perceptions” is the burial place of Anschauung.

Dating. Mügge dates the truth essay 1873, and the critical text’s heading gives no date. Mügge also dates “The Greek State” 1871; the critical text prints it among the prefaces written out in December 1872. This guide does not settle the history of drafts behind either date.

Interpretive problems

Two essays, not one theory

It is tempting to read the essays as two halves of one early philosophy: the contest as the social form of the will, the concept as its intellectual form. The texts do not license that step. “Homer’s Contest” is about one institution in one culture. “On Truth and Lies” is about language and knowledge in general. They share a pattern, a force that cannot be abolished and is given a form, and a verifiable textual link, the fable. Whether they share a theory is the reader’s question to test, not a premise.

Description and endorsement in “Homer’s Contest”

The essay describes Greek cruelty with evident admiration and calls modern humanity “emasculate” (Mügge). But its argument is that the Greeks did not simply release cruelty: they built a frame in which envy produced works instead of feuds. The greatness and the agon theme sets out what that argument does not say. It is not praise of competition in general. It is a claim about an arrangement with rules, an audience, and roughly matched rivals. Keep the admiration and the argument apart.

Who is inside the contest?

The contest Nietzsche describes is among citizens, poets, teachers, and cities. The “welfare of the whole” is the welfare of the civic body. The women and children sold into slavery in the essay’s second paragraph are not in it, and neither are slaves or foreigners. “The Greek State,” in the same set of prefaces, argues that culture requires slavery. The frame that makes envy productive has an outside, and the essay does not ask what the contest costs those who are not allowed to compete.

Philology as evidence

Nietzsche was a classical philologist, and the essay argues through sources: Hesiod’s Works and Days, Pausanias on a lead copy of Hesiod, Aristotle, the Ephesians’ verdict on Hermodorus, Herodotus on Miltiades, Thucydides on Themistocles. Those are historical claims, and they can be checked. His readings of them are interpretations. The claim that Athens and Sparta fell “as a proof” that states degenerate without rivalry is a further inference from two cases.

Genealogy and validity in “On Truth and Lies”

The essay explains how concepts are formed, by dropping differences, and how truthfulness began, as a social obligation. It then concludes that truths are “illusions of which one has forgotten that they are illusions” (Mügge). The first two are claims about origin. The conclusion is a claim about validity. A concept formed by omitting differences can still be accurate for a purpose, as the camel example half concedes: a truth of “very limited value” is still a truth. Ask where the essay argues for the step from origin to worth, and where it assumes it.

The problem of self-reference

If every truth is a worn-out metaphor, what is the essay’s own claim that truth is a mobile army of metaphors? Nietzsche does not simply ignore the problem. He says that we cannot claim the division into individual and species fails to correspond to the essence of things, since that would be a dogmatic assertion “just as undemonstrable as its contrary” (Mügge). The essay is sometimes more cautious than its famous sentences. Read for both.

A Kantian frame, not the later view

The essay still speaks of the “Thing-in-itself” as an inaccessible X, and traces the lawfulness of nature to the forms of time, space, and number that we bring to it. That is a Kantian and Schopenhauerian framework. In Twilight of the Idols, fifteen years later, Nietzsche abolishes the “true world” and, with it, the “apparent” one. The later book is not the early essay restated. Do not use one to settle what the other means.

Rank in both essays

Both essays end by ranking. The contest essay ranks Greek over modern and the man who competes over the man who stands alone and falls into hybris. The truth essay describes concepts as the ground of castes and grades, and ends by setting the intuitive man, who has “a harvest” from his intuitions, beside the rational man, who only wards off misfortune. Neither ranking is argued as fully as it is asserted.

“Homer’s Contest” step by step

The essay runs about twelve pages in Mügge’s translation and has no internal divisions. The notes below follow its movements. For each, they say what happens, where to watch the claim change level, and what to read alongside. Links open the page in the free edition on which a movement begins; all movements are in the single German section.

Nature and humaneness (p. 51)

What happens. The essay begins by denying the distinction on which the idea of humaneness rests. The “natural” and the “human” qualities have grown up inseparably; man in his highest capacities “is Nature and bears in himself her awful twofold character.” The capacities called dreadful and inhuman “are perhaps indeed the fertile soil, out of which alone can grow forth all humanity in emotions, actions and works” (Mügge).

Watch the step. “Perhaps” carries the claim. The essay will not prove that cruelty is the soil of culture; it will show that one culture made something of cruelty.

Read alongside. The Birth of Tragedy, published the same year, where Apollonian art is also a beautiful appearance over a terrible ground.

Free text · German critical text

Greek cruelty and the pre-Homeric world (pp. 51–54)

What happens. The Greeks, “the most humane men of ancient times,” have “a trait of cruelty, of tiger-like pleasure in destruction.” The evidence runs from Alexander at Gaza, a “sickening caricature” of Achilles dragging Hector, through the Corcyrean revolution and the law of war, to the sculptors and the Iliad. Behind Homer’s “artistic deception” lies a pre-Homeric world of night and horror, known through Hesiod’s theogony. One answer to such a world was the Orphic: loathing of existence, and existence as a punishment. That answer, Nietzsche says, is not specifically Greek; the Hellenic genius gave another.

Watch the step. The historical examples are real events in Greek sources. The pre-Homeric world is a construction from myth: Nietzsche asks us to “imagine” Hesiod’s air “still thickened and darkened.” The move from the one to the other is the essay’s first large inference.

Read alongside. The suffering and cruelty theme, and Beyond Good and Evil §229, where almost everything called higher culture rests on “the spiritualization and deepening of cruelty” (this guide’s translation).

Free text, p. 52

The two Eris-goddesses (pp. 54–56)

What happens. The Greek genius “admitted the existing fearful impulse, and deemed it justified” (Mügge). Pausanias, Nietzsche reports, saw an old lead copy of Hesiod’s Works and Days that began: “Two Eris-goddesses are on earth.” One furthers war; the other, set by Zeus among men, drives even the unskilled to work and makes the potter grudge the potter. Modern scholars, Nietzsche says, have called the potter verses spurious because they cannot see envy as good; Aristotle saw no offence. For the Greek, envy is “the effect of a beneficent deity.” But the same Greek fears the envy of the gods and never contends with them: Thamyris, Marsyas, and Niobe show the penalty.

Watch the step. The textual point, that Hesiod praises a good strife, is sound and is found in Hesiod (Works and Days 11–26). The claim that “the whole Greek antiquity” thought of envy as Hesiod did is a generalization from a poet and a philosopher.

Read alongside. Human, All Too Human I §170 (1878), where the Greek tragedians wrote to win, and “the Hesiodic good Eris, ambition, gave their genius its wings” (this guide’s translation).

Free text, p. 54

Rivals among the great (pp. 56–57)

What happens. “The greater and more sublime however a Greek is, the brighter in him appears the ambitious flame” (Mügge). Xenophanes attacked Homer out of the desire to inherit his fame, and so, later, did Plato. Themistocles could not sleep for the laurels of Miltiades. An opponent of Pericles says that even when he throws Pericles, Pericles denies that he fell and persuades those who saw it. “Every great Hellene hands on the torch of the contest.”

Watch the step. The examples move from literature to politics. Envy of the dead, envy of a rival, and a rival’s rhetoric are different things, and each is read as the same drive.

Free text, p. 56

Ostracism and the second genius (pp. 57–58)

What happens. The Ephesians banished Hermodorus with the words: “Among us nobody shall be the best; if however someone is the best, then let him be so elsewhere and among others” (Mügge). The saying is preserved in a fragment of Heraclitus. Nietzsche reads it as the original sense of ostracism: not a safety valve against tyranny, which it became later, but a stimulant, removing the one who excels so that “the contest of forces might re-awaken.” The Greek idea of the contest “abominates autocracy”; it assumes that there are always several geniuses who incite and restrain one another. “It desires as a preventive against the genius—a second genius.”

Watch the step. The distinction between an original and a later sense of ostracism is Nietzsche’s reconstruction. It is also the essay’s strongest argument against reading it as praise of domination: the contest requires plurality, and a single winner ends it.

Read alongside. The greatness and the agon theme, whose first encounter starts from this passage.

Free text, p. 57

Agonistic education and the rivalry of artists (pp. 58–60)

What happens. “Every natural gift must develop itself by contest” (Mügge). Modern educators fear ambition; only the Jesuits, Nietzsche says, agree with the ancients. The aim of Greek education was the welfare of the city: the youth ran and sang for his city’s glory, and in that his selfishness was both kindled and bridled. So individuals were “freer, because their aims were nearer and more tangible,” while modern man is hampered by infinity like Zeno’s Achilles. Teachers competed too: Pindar and Simonides, sophist against sophist, the dramatists in their festivals. Plato’s dialogues, Nietzsche argues, are an attempt to outdo the orators, sophists, and dramatists, after which Plato condemns imitative art.

Watch the step. The claim about freedom rests on a definition: freedom as having near and tangible aims. On that definition the ancient youth is freer; on others he is not.

Read alongside. “Schopenhauer as Educator” (1874), on education and culture in Nietzsche’s own time.

Free text, p. 58

Without the contest: Miltiades, Athens, Sparta, Alexander (pp. 60–62)

What happens. Remove the contest, and one looks at once into the pre-Homeric abyss. The Greek could not bear “fame without further struggle, and fortune at the end of the contest” (Mügge). Miltiades, alone after Marathon, turns his reputation to private revenge, breaks into a sanctuary at night, and falls; the envy of the gods has caught him. Athens and Sparta, victorious, abuse their power and fall “as a proof that without envy, jealousy, and contesting ambition the Hellenic State like the Hellenic man degenerates.” Alexander, “the coarsened copy and abbreviation of Greek history,” invents the cosmopolitan Hellene.

Watch the step. The Miltiades story comes from Herodotus. The step from one man’s fall to the fall of states, and from two states to a law, is the essay’s boldest inference, and it is stated as proof.

Free text, p. 60

“On Truth and Lies” step by step

The essay has two numbered sections. §1, about fifteen pages in Mügge, gives the origin of the drive to truth; §2, about five pages, turns to science, myth, and art. The notes follow its movements. Links open the page on which a movement begins; the German links open the section.

The fable of the clever animals (§1, pp. 173–174)

What happens. “In some remote corner of the universe, effused into innumerable solar-systems, there was once a star upon which clever animals invented cognition” (Mügge). It was the most arrogant and mendacious minute of “world history” (German: „Weltgeschichte“), and then the star cooled and the animals died. Even this fable, Nietzsche says, would not show how purposeless the human intellect is in nature. If we could understand the gnat, we would learn that it too feels itself the flying centre of the world.

Watch the step. The fable is a change of scale, not an argument. It shows the intellect as small; it does not show that what the intellect knows is false. The same fable, in the 1872 preface “On the Pathos of Truth,” ended with the animals discovering “that they had known everything falsely” and dying “cursing truth” (this guide’s translation). The 1873 essay drops that ending.

Free text · German §1 · the 1872 version

The intellect as dissimulation (§1, pp. 174–176)

What happens. The intellect is given to “the most unfortunate, the most delicate, the most transient beings only as an expedient.” Its chief power is dissimulation, by which weaker individuals survive without horns or teeth. In man “deception, flattery, falsehood and fraud” are the rule, and the eye only glides over the surface of things. Nature has hidden even the body from consciousness and “threw away the key”; beneath it man hangs “in dreams on the back of a tiger” (Mügge). “Whence, in the wide world, with this state of affairs, arises the impulse to truth?”

Watch the step. The intellect’s function is claimed to be self-preservation. That is a hypothesis about its origin. The essay treats it as a reason to doubt what the intellect can do, which is a separate question.

Free text, p. 174

The peace treaty and the liar (§1, pp. 176–177)

What happens. Humans want to live in society “from necessity and boredom,” and so make a peace treaty. The treaty fixes “a uniformly valid and binding designation of things,” and “the legislature of language also gives the first laws of truth” (Mügge). The liar uses the valid words to make the unreal seem real: he says “I am rich” when he is poor. Society excludes him not because it hates deception but because it hates the harm some deceptions do. Likewise man wants truth only for its agreeable, life-preserving consequences.

Watch the step. This is a claim about the social origin of the distinction between truth and lie. It explains why truthfulness became a duty. It does not yet say anything about whether the agreed designations fit the things.

Free text, p. 176

Words as metaphors (§1, pp. 177–179)

What happens. “What is a word? The expression of a nerve-stimulus in sounds” (Mügge). To infer a cause outside us from the stimulus is already a misuse of the principle of sufficient reason. We call the stone hard, the tree masculine, the plant feminine: “what arbitrary metaphors!” The creator of language designates only the relations of things to men. A nerve stimulus becomes an image, the first metaphor; the image becomes a sound, the second; each time there is a complete leap into another sphere. The deaf man who studies Chladni’s sound figures in the sand and thinks he now knows what tone is: so it is with all of us and language.

Watch the step. “Metaphor” here means transfer between spheres (see the variants). The argument depends on the claim that transfer between spheres preserves nothing of the original. The Chladni example suggests the opposite: the figures in the sand are caused by, and vary with, the vibrations.

Free text, p. 177

Concepts: the leaf and honesty (§1, pp. 179–180)

What happens. Every word becomes a concept by fitting countless cases that are never equal. “Every idea originates through equating the unequal” (Mügge; “idea” for concept). No leaf is exactly like another, yet the concept “leaf” suggests a primal form of which leaves are bad copies. We say a man acted honestly “on account of his honesty,” and so make the leaf the cause of the leaves. Nature knows no forms or species, only an inaccessible X. Yet we cannot say that our distinction of individual and species does not correspond to things, for that would be “a dogmatic assertion.”

Watch the step. The honesty example is a precise point: an abstraction used as an explanation adds nothing. The general claim, that every concept falsifies because it omits differences, is a further step. The essay’s own caution about dogmatic assertion limits it.

Free text, p. 179

“What therefore is truth?” (§1, p. 180)

What happens. “A mobile army of metaphors, metonymies, anthropomorphisms: in short a sum of human relations which became poetically and rhetorically intensified, metamorphosed, adorned, and after long usage seem to a nation fixed, canonic and binding; truths are illusions of which one has forgotten that they are illusions; worn-out metaphors which have become powerless to affect the senses; coins which have their obverse effaced and now are no longer of account as coins but merely as metal” (Mügge).

Watch the step. The sentence is a definition built from the preceding argument, and it is the most quoted sentence of the essay. Notice “to a nation”: truths are binding within a community. The claim is as much about a people’s conventions as about knowledge in general.

Free text, p. 180

The feeling for truth and the columbarium of concepts (§1, pp. 180–183)

What happens. Nietzsche returns to his question. Society obliges us to be truthful, “that is, to use the usual metaphors,” which, “expressed morally,” is the obligation “to lie according to a fixed convention” (Mügge). We forget the obligation, and from forgetting arises a moral feeling for truth. Man now puts his actions under the rule of abstractions, and within them builds “a pyramidal order with castes and grades,” a world of laws and privileges. The edifice of concepts has “the rigid regularity of a Roman Columbarium.” “Truth” now means to use each die as it is marked, and “never to violate the order of castes and the sequences of rank.” Man is to be admired as an architectural genius, building on running water, but not for his impulse to truth.

Watch the step. Nietzsche calls it lying only when it is “expressed morally”; the essay’s own standpoint is outside morality. The link between concepts and social rank is a striking claim, and it is stated rather than shown.

Free text, p. 180

The mammal and the camel (§1, pp. 183–184)

What happens. Finding a truth within reason is like hiding a thing behind a bush and finding it there again. “If I make the definition of the mammal and then declare after inspecting a camel, ‘Behold a mammal,’ then no doubt a truth is brought to light thereby, but it is of very limited value” (Mügge). It is anthropomorphic through and through. The seeker of such truths sees the world as the echo of man, “man as the measure of all things,” and forgets that his original metaphors were metaphors.

Watch the step. The example concedes that a truth has been found. The objection is to its value and its independence of us, not its truth. That is narrower than “truths are illusions.”

Free text, p. 183

Forgetting the artistic subject (§1, pp. 184–185)

What happens. Man lives with “repose, safety and consequence” only by forgetting himself as “an artistically creating subject.” If he could leave the prison of that faith for a moment, his self-consciousness would be destroyed. The insect and the bird perceive other worlds, and to ask which world-perception is more correct is senseless, since there is no standard of “right perception.” Between subject and object there can be at most “an æsthetical relation,” “a stammering translation into quite a distinct foreign language” (Mügge). A painter without hands, singing his picture, would reveal more of it than the empirical world reveals of the essence of things.

Watch the step. Here the argument turns from language to perception itself, and the positive term appears: the relation of mind to world is aesthetic, a matter of creation. That prepares §2.

Free text, p. 184

The laws of nature (§1, pp. 185–187)

What happens. Nietzsche raises an objection to his own view: nature’s laws are so consistent, from the telescopic to the microscopic, that they cannot be a product of fancy. He answers in two steps. If each of us perceived differently, as bird or worm, no one would speak of such lawfulness. And what we know of natural laws is only their relations, and in them only what we bring: time, space, and number. We produce these forms “with that necessity with which the spider spins,” so it is no wonder we find them everywhere. Concepts, finally, imitate the relations of time, space, and number in the realm of metaphors.

Watch the step. This is Kant’s claim that time and space are forms we bring to experience, put to a new use. It gives the essay a reply to the objection from science, but it also commits the essay to the Kantian framework. See the interpretive problems.

Free text, p. 185

Science and the drive to metaphor (§2, pp. 187–189)

What happens. Language first built the edifice of concepts; science now builds on it, filling “that great columbarium of ideas, the cemetery of perceptions” (Mügge). The man of science builds his hut beside it for protection, because other “truths” press on him. The drive to form metaphors, “that fundamental impulse of man, which we cannot reason away for one moment,” is not subdued by the edifice. It finds another bed in myth and art, and constantly confuses the concepts with new metaphors. Pascal is quoted: an artisan who dreamed every night for twelve hours that he was king would be as happy as a king. The early Greeks, for whom a tree might speak as a nymph, lived a waking life more like a dream. Nietzsche writes that “every honest Athenian did believe” that Athena rode with Pisistratus through the market of Athens.

Watch the step. Herodotus, the source of the Pisistratus story, tells it as a trick played on the Athenians with a tall woman dressed as Athena. Nietzsche turns a report of deception into evidence of a mythic way of seeing. That is in keeping with his argument, but it is his reading.

Free text, p. 187 · German §2

The intellect’s Saturnalia (§2, pp. 189–190)

What happens. “Man himself, however, has an invincible tendency to let himself be deceived” (Mügge), and is enchanted when the rhapsode or actor deceives him. When the intellect can deceive without doing harm, it is freed from its service as a slave and “celebrates Its Saturnalia.” It throws metaphors into confusion, calls the river “the mobile way” that carries man, and treats the scaffolding of concepts as a toy. It is now led “no longer … by ideas but by intuitions,” for which no word is made, so that it speaks “in forbidden metaphors and in unheard-of combinations of ideas.”

Watch the step. The same condition that made the liar dangerous, deception, is harmless in art. The essay’s standard is harm, not truth, in both places.

Free text, p. 189

The rational man and the intuitive man (§2, pp. 190–192)

What happens. There are ages when the rational man and the intuitive man stand side by side, “the one full of fear of the intuition, the other full of scorn for the abstraction” (Mügge). Both want to rule life. Where the intuitive man prevails, as in early Greece, a culture can form and art can rule life; even the house, the gait, and the earthen jug seem to express “a sublime happiness.” The rational man only wards off misfortune; the intuitive man reaps “enlightenment, enlivenment and redemption,” but suffers more and learns nothing from experience. The essay ends with the Stoic, who in misfortune performs “the masterpiece of dissimulation” and walks away from the storm in his cloak.

Watch the step. The comparison is weighted toward the intuitive man, but the essay gives the last image to the Stoic. Its ending is a portrait, not a verdict.

Free text, p. 190

Early essays and later books

Both essays have long afterlives in Nietzsche’s published work, but the later texts are not the early essays in finished form. The comparisons below help most. Keep the dates in view, and ask what changed.

The contest after 1872

Human, All Too Human I §170 (1878) names “the Hesiodic good Eris” as the ambition that gave the Greek tragedians their wings, and adds that they wrote to meet their own standard, not public taste (this guide’s translation). Beyond Good and Evil §229 (1886) generalizes the essay’s opening thesis: almost everything called higher culture rests on the spiritualization of cruelty. In Twilight of the Idols, “What I Owe to the Ancients” §3 (1888), Nietzsche says he saw the Greeks’ strongest instinct, “the will to power,” and saw “all their institutions grow out of protective measures” against the explosive material within them (this guide’s translation). The 1888 account keeps the idea of institutions built against inner violence. It adds a vocabulary the 1872 essay does not have.

Truth and language after 1873

Human, All Too Human I §11 (1878), “Language as a Supposed Science,” says that man long believed he had knowledge of the world in the names of things. The Gay Science §§110–111 (1882) give the origin of knowledge and logic in useful errors, and §354 (1887) ties consciousness to the need to communicate. Beyond Good and Evil §4 (1886) says that the falseness of a judgment is not yet an objection to it. Twilight’s fable of the “true world” ends: “with the true world we have also abolished the apparent one!” (this guide’s translation). That sentence is the clearest departure from the 1873 essay, which still speaks of a thing-in-itself behind appearances. The perspectivism theme follows these texts in order.

Writing exercise

Compare two institutions. Choose one passage from each essay:

  • From “Homer’s Contest”: the paragraph on ostracism and the “second genius,” or the paragraph on agonistic education.
  • From “On Truth and Lies”: the paragraph on the peace treaty and the liar, or the paragraph on the columbarium of concepts.

Write two short columns. In the first, record what the institution in the first essay does: what force it works on, what it makes possible, and whom it excludes, with page references and no more than a sentence of quotation. In the second, do the same for the institution in the second essay. Then write a paragraph that answers three questions. Does each essay describe the institution, praise it, or both? Where does each move from how something began to what it is worth? Do the essays share a single view, or only a pattern? End with one sentence on what each essay must still be read for on its own terms.

Where they lead

Back to what Nietzsche published. The Birth of Tragedy (1872) is the published book of the same years, with its own account of Greek art as appearance over horror. Compare its argument and its caution.

Forward to the public essays. The Untimely Meditations (1873–1876) take up culture, education, and history for a public, in the same years as these private essays.

Across to the rest of the volume. “The Greek State,” in the same set of prefaces and in Mügge’s volume, argues that culture rests on slavery. Read it with the greatness and the agon theme before deciding how admirable the contest is.
