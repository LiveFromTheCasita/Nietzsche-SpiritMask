Overview and status

These are two distinct unpublished essays, not a book Nietzsche arranged as a pair. “Homer’s Contest” asks how the Greeks might turn envy and rivalry into public achievement; “On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense” asks how language turns unequal experiences into concepts that later feel like truths. Their common pressure is on what a culture makes from forces it cannot simply abolish. The link between them is a reading proposal, not a claim that Nietzsche planned a unified work.

The essays are early experiments. Read them near The Birth of Tragedy, but do not treat them as a finished account of his later views on truth, power, or morality. In particular, identifying a concept’s metaphorical origin does not by itself show that every claim made with it is false. Likewise, describing an agon in Greek culture does not establish that every rivalry improves its participants.

Guided reading path

Read both essays in full in the order below. Their titles differ across English editions. The links open Mügge’s collection; use its contents to locate the named essay and stop at the next essay’s heading. Each question is meant to be answered from the assigned text.

  1. Read now“Homer’s Contest” (1872), complete

    Question: Which forms of envy and ambition can an institution redirect, and what keeps a contest from becoming annihilation? Nietzsche distinguishes destructive strife from a contest that solicits effort. Test his picture against those excluded from its arena and against his own willingness to praise competition.

  2. Read now“On Truth and Falsity in Their Ultramoral Sense” (1873), complete

    Question: How does an image or experience become a shared word, then an apparently fixed concept? Track the essay’s account of social convention, lying, and forgotten metaphor. Ask whether a useful convention can also be corrigible or accurate for a particular purpose.

Compare the essays: Can a convention both constrain people and enable a practice they value? Apply that question to the rules of contest and to the shared language of truth. Write a short account of one benefit and one cost in each case, citing a passage rather than attributing a single theory to all of Nietzsche’s work.

Texts and editions

Our source guide does not recommend a single matching Kaufmann or Hollingdale edition for this historical collection. The free Early Greek Philosophy & Other Essays is translated by Maximilian A. Mügge. It contains a translator’s preface and seven essays; this guide assigns only “Homer’s Contest” and the final truth essay. Mügge’s “Falsity” and “Ultramoral” in the title correspond to other editions’ “Lies” and “Nonmoral.” If reading a modern anthology, locate the two individual essays by title and compare its translator’s terminology rather than treating Mügge’s words as Nietzsche’s only English wording.

The collection’s other essays, including “The Greek State,” are also unpublished early writings, but pursue different questions. Mügge labels both “The Greek State” and “Homer’s Contest” as prefaces to unwritten books. Nothing on this page silently assigns the whole volume. These two essays were written in 1872 and 1873 and not published by Nietzsche during his lifetime; the collection’s 1911 arrangement is editorial.

What the essays test

The agon and its limits

“Homer’s Contest” treats rivalry as something a culture shapes rather than a vice it can abolish. An institutional contest may turn competition into a succession of achievements instead of one final victory. That thesis invites two tests: whether Nietzsche’s Greek example describes the people outside the contest, and whether productive rivalry can coexist with protection from domination. The essay does not settle either question for modern institutions.

Language and the value of truth

The truth essay begins with the smallness of human knowing, then asks why a community demands truthful naming. Its movement from sensation to image to word to concept highlights what a general term leaves out. Follow the stages carefully: a genealogy of a concept’s formation, a criticism of forgetting that formation, and a claim about what a word can know are related but distinct arguments. A reader can accept one without automatically accepting all three.

Early and later Nietzsche

Greatness and the agon follows conflict into later contexts; perspectivism examines truth and interpretation across the published work. Use those pages to compare claims, not to backdate later positions into drafts Nietzsche withheld.

Where they lead

Continue to the Untimely Meditations for public essays about culture and education. Return to The Birth of Tragedy to see what Nietzsche published at the time; the comparison may reveal both an overlapping cultural ambition and different kinds of argument.
